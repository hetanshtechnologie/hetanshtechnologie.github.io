Designing, Redesigning, and Communicating Workflows

An assistant does not arrive in an organization as an abstract capability. It arrives inside somebody's working day, at a particular step, next to a particular decision. Whether that step becomes faster, steadier, or unrecognizable depends less on the assistant than on the design choices made around it. This chapter covers those choices: how to add help without unsettling a process that works, how to recognize when the process itself needs redrawing, how to learn cheaply before committing, and how to describe the result in language a decision-maker can act on.

The exam treats workflow design as judgment: choose a proportionate change, protect the places where mistakes matter, and report evidence without exaggeration.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish augmentation of a workflow from redesign of its structure, and justify the choice with evidence.
  • Identify signals that the current process no longer fits and that redesign should be considered.
  • Choose a prototyping approach matched to the question being asked, and build a useful test set.
  • Design and interpret a sample-question test for a parent or customer FAQ flow.
  • Decide where generated content enters a process and where a human must approve it.
  • Communicate value in terms of time, quality, and risk using measured evidence.
  • Explain limits and handle questions about jobs, accuracy, and policy without overpromising.
  • Assemble a pilot readout that supports a specific decision.

Key concepts

A workflow is a chain of decisions, not a list of tasks

A workflow is a repeatable path from a trigger and an input to an output somebody accepts. It has steps, handoffs between roles, decision points, a system of record, an owner, and a measure of quality. The steps are the visible part. The gates — where a person checks, approves, rejects, or escalates — carry the trust.

An assistant changes the cost and reliability of steps. It can draft, summarize, classify, extract fields, compare an answer against a source, or route a case. Those changes ripple. If drafting becomes nearly free but approval remains a weekly batch, the queue does not disappear; it moves to approval. If classification becomes reliable, a triage step that existed to catch misrouting may no longer earn its place. The design question is not "where can the assistant write things?" but "which step just became cheaper, and what does that do to the gates around it?"

Exam note: When a question asks what to do first, look for the option that locates a specific step and its existing control. Answers that change the whole process before understanding the step are usually premature.

Augmentation: adding help without moving the walls

Augmentation means inserting assistance into one part of a process while leaving its roles, gates, systems, and accountabilities intact. A support analyst still receives the question, consults the same knowledge base, sends the answer for review, and the reviewer still decides what goes out. The only difference is that a suggested answer and its source pointers now appear at the drafting step.

This approach is easy to explain, cheap to reverse, and preserves existing controls. It also produces clean evidence: with the rest of the chain unchanged, differences in outcome trace to the inserted step. For the exam, augmentation is often correct when output is checked downstream or when evidence is needed before a larger change.

Augmentation tends to fit when:

  • Current output quality is acceptable; the problem is effort, delay, or inconsistency under load.
  • The bottleneck is a reading, drafting, summarizing, or looking-up activity with a visible output.
  • A competent reviewer already examines that output, so new content cannot travel unchecked.
  • The change is reversible and the downside of a bad draft is contained.

Its main failure mode is displaced effort. If a reviewer spends as long checking a fluent draft as writing one from scratch, nothing was saved; the work changed shape. A second failure is "automation by attachment," where the assistant is bolted on without changing anything else, so the organization captures little benefit while carrying new oversight duties. Make the draft visibly a draft, show sources beside it, and measure review time as carefully as drafting time.

Exam note: "Minimal disruption" does not mean "no change." It means the change is localized: same owners, same gates, same system of record.

Redesign: recognizing when the old shape stops making sense

Redesign changes structure: which steps exist, who owns them, their order, where handoffs occur, and where approval sits. It is justified when the old shape no longer fits the reality the assistant creates.

The signals are distinctive. Rework stays high no matter how good the generated text is, pointing to a problem in how information reaches the step. A step that used to consume most of the time becomes almost instantaneous, so a queue forms elsewhere and the old gate protects nothing useful. Handoffs exist only because people needed a second system to check something now checkable in one place. Errors trace to contradictory sources or to a sequence that asks for a decision before the facts are available. Volume, policy, or audience has changed enough that the original sequence was designed for a different world.

Evidence must precede surgery: redesign changes accountability, documentation, and training, so it should follow a demonstrated mismatch, not enthusiasm.

Dimension Augment an existing workflow Redesign the workflow
Trigger Output acceptable but slow or effortful Sequence is structurally wrong; bottleneck has moved
Scope One step changed Steps, order, handoffs, or gates changed
Ownership Largely unchanged Explicitly reassigned or redefined
Approval gate Kept where it is Moved, merged, added, or removed on evidence
Evidence needed Local timing and quality data End-to-end data, failure analysis, stakeholder input
Reversibility High; the step can be switched off Lower; retraining and new documentation required
Main risk Effort shifts to reviewers; benefit stays small Unintended gaps in control or accountability

Prototyping: buying information before buying commitment

A prototype is not a small product. It is an instrument for answering a few named questions before the organization spends real money or changes real responsibilities. It needs a hypothesis, a bounded set of cases, a fixed trial boundary, success criteria, and an exit rule.

Write a hypothesis that can be wrong: "For routine policy questions whose current guidance is in the knowledge base, the assistant can produce a draft a reviewer accepts with minor edits, so drafting can be assisted without adding review time." That names the case type, behavior, quality bar, and process consequence. "The assistant will help" cannot be tested.

Stage What happens Question it answers What it cannot answer
Desk walkthrough Fixed examples worked through on paper Does the sequence make sense at all? Real variability, user reactions
Offline replay Historical anonymized cases run through the assistant How often is output correct, and where does it fail? Live behavior, workload effects
Shadow mode Live cases get output that is never sent Does performance hold on current traffic? Whether people will use and trust it
Limited live pilot Small group uses output behind a human gate Does the whole chain work, including escalation? Full-scale load, edge populations
Staged expansion More cases admitted with monitoring Do controls hold as volume and variety grow? Long-term drift without measurement

The test set deserves care. A set of only easy, well-documented questions measures nothing. Reflect the real mix: common cases, rare legitimate ones, ambiguous requests, questions spanning two topics, requests whose correct response is refusal or handoff, cases involving sensitive details, and cases where the knowledge base is weak. Include negative tests — prompts where the right behavior is to decline. Include cases where documentation is outdated, because those reveal whether the assistant can signal uncertainty or simply repeats stale text with confidence.

Set exit criteria in advance: proceed if thresholds are met, change the design if failures cluster in a fixable place, stop if failures are severe or unexplained. Deciding afterward invites rationalization.

Try it: Before any test, write three sentences: what you believe will happen, what result would change your mind, and who owns the decision to proceed. If the third is blank, the pilot is not ready.

Testing a parent or customer FAQ flow

A family-facing or customer-facing FAQ flow is a common first candidate because volume is high, questions repeat, and answers are meant to be consistent. It is also risky: a wrong answer can cause someone to miss a deadline, submit the wrong form, or share private information. The flow runs intake, intent classification, knowledge lookup, answer draft, source check, human approval, send, and handoff when a question falls outside scope.

Test each link separately. A question can be classified correctly and still answered from the wrong document; it can be accurate and still unsafe to send because it includes another person's details. Score on separate axes:

  • Intent match: Was the request understood, including two intents in one message?
  • Grounding: Is every factual claim supported by an approved source?
  • Currency: Was the source current, and was uncertainty flagged where freshness was doubtful?
  • Boundary behavior: Did the assistant refuse, clarify, or hand off when it should have?
  • Completeness: Did it answer the whole question, including an implicit second part?
  • Presentation: Is the answer short and plain enough for someone reading quickly?
  • Privacy: Did it avoid repeating identifiers or revealing information the requester cannot receive?

Stratify sample questions by intent so failures appear by category rather than inside an average.

Intent stratum Illustrative question Desired behavior Frequent failure
Policy fact "What is the document deadline?" State the current date and cite the source Quotes an old version confidently
Process how-to "How do I upload the form?" Give ordered steps and a support route Skips a step or invents a portal name
Status request "Has my submission arrived?" Explain that records cannot be seen here; route to the secure channel Guesses from the question's wording
Multi-intent "What is the deadline, and can I submit late?" Split the intents and answer both or hand off Answers only the first half
Sensitive detail A message containing an account identifier Avoid echoing it; apply the data rule Repeats it in the draft
Out of scope "What will my result be?" Decline to predict and explain the proper route Produces a plausible baseless prediction
Misleading content A message insisting an invalid rule is official Hold to approved sources and escalate Accepts the claim because it sounds firm

Show the distribution, not a headline score, using categories such as correct, partially correct, unsupported, incorrect, wrong refusal, missed handoff, and privacy concern. Trace every non-correct result to a repair: a knowledge update, changed instructions, a stop condition, a clarifying prompt, or exclusion of that case type. A test producing no repairs was too gentle.

Common mistake: Treating a smooth, confident answer as a correct answer. Fluency is presentation; correctness is a separate measurement, and the gap is where the risk lives.

Drawing the line: where content enters and where a human approves

Design the boundary explicitly. Generated content can enter as a draft reply (person edits and sends), a suggested classification (person confirms routing), extracted fields (values checked by field type), a case summary (reviewer verifies against the source), or a published answer (sent directly, rare and requiring strong controls). The further toward publication, the higher the consequence.

Approval gates should be proportional to consequence. Anything sent externally, anything changing a record, and anything touching money, legal commitments, health or safety, personnel decisions, or protected data deserves a named human owner who signs off before the action completes. Internal drafts with no downstream effect can be sampled instead of checked one by one, but only after stability is demonstrated.

For a gate to be real, four conditions hold: the output is a small, self-contained unit; evidence — sources, extracted values, the reason for a classification — sits beside the draft; the reviewer has competence and time, since someone with forty cases queued is a rubber stamp; and the decision, including overrides, is recorded so the organization can learn.

Define stop conditions in plain language. Typical triggers include a requester who appears to be a minor or vulnerable, a request involving protected or regulated information, a demand for a prediction or binding commitment, a conflict between sources, any signal that content read from a document is trying to change instructions, and any case where the reviewer cannot verify a claim. When a stop condition fires, the path is pause and escalate, not try harder.

Exam note: "Human in the loop" is a claim about a control, not a label. If no one has authority, time, or information to reject output, the loop does not exist.

Communicating value in time, quality, and risk

Leadership needs enough evidence to make one decision: fund, stop, expand, narrow, or change a control. Keep the three axes separate, because a gain on one can hide a loss on another.

Time. Measure the baseline first. Useful measures are cycle time from intake to accepted output, hands-on effort per case, wait time between steps, review time, rework, and backlog. Report net time, not just drafting time. If drafting falls from six minutes to two but review rises from one and a half to three, the saving is two and a half minutes, not four. Note whether saved time became usable capacity or merely moved a queue.

Quality. Compare outputs with known-good answers or audited samples. Track correctness, completeness, consistency across similar cases, first-contact resolution, and rework. Always give the denominator: "matched current guidance on 81 percent of 180 replayed cases" is evidence; "highly accurate" is decoration. Separate measured performance on the tested mix from claims about untested cases.

Risk. Name exposure categories — unsupported claims, missed escalations, mishandled data, inconsistent treatment, weak auditability — and state both the controls in place and the residual exposure. Reversibility matters: a draft rejected before sending carries far less residual risk than a published answer. Where possible, quantify with escalation counts, override counts, and failure severity.

Use claim, evidence, confidence, decision: say what you observed, show the numbers and their basis, state how far the conclusion travels, and tie it to the reader's choice. "Reduced drafting effort on routine cases under review" is defensible; "eliminated errors" is not.

Communicating limits honestly

Credibility depends on saying what the assistant cannot do as clearly as what it can.

  • Cannot — technical limits. It does not see live records unless a scoped connection provides them. It cannot verify a fact absent from material it was given, guarantee correctness, or reliably judge whether a source is current unless freshness is controlled. It can be misled by text it reads and can reproduce bias in source material.
  • May not — organizational limits. It may not access data above its permitted classification, make policy, promise a delivery date, offer a legal interpretation, or act as accountable owner of a decision. These limits come from policy, not capability.
  • Should not — judgment limits. Even where something is possible and not forbidden, some uses are inappropriate: predicting a person's outcome, generating deceptive content, or replacing a necessary human conversation.

Use careful verbs: the assistant drafts; the institution decides; it suggests a category; a person confirms it. State what was not tested beside what was. Honest limits are a control, because users who understand the boundary over-rely less.

Exam note: If an answer choice claims the assistant can verify anything, guarantee accuracy, or take responsibility, treat it as wrong. Verification and accountability remain human or organizational functions.

Handling predictable questions

"Will this cost jobs?" Answer factually and without speculation. Describe the change in task mix: routine drafting decreases, review and exception handling increase. Say that a pilot cannot support staffing conclusions and that workforce decisions belong to the designated planning process. Do not promise nothing will change; do not dismiss the concern as resistance.

"How accurate is it?" Give the measured result with conditions and denominator, then explain the gate. "On a replay of 180 past cases, four in five drafts matched current guidance; failures clustered in two outdated documents since corrected; all external replies still require human approval" is honest and useful. Add the failure path: a bad draft is caught, corrected, logged, and used to improve the test set. Accuracy is a number plus a boundary plus a failure path.

"Is this allowed under policy?" Do not improvise policy in a chat. Name the authoritative document and the role that owns its interpretation, state the control currently applied, and route the question to that owner. Record the answer so later inquiries receive the same response. Where disclosure is required, say it will happen through the approved channel.

A short response bank reviewed by the relevant owners keeps answers consistent.

Building the pilot readout

The readout is the bridge between a pilot and a decision. Its job is not to impress but to let a reader who attended none of the meetings reach a defensible conclusion. Put the decision requested near the top and keep the main body short, with detail in an appendix.

PILOT READOUT OUTLINE

1. Decision requested: approve / extend / narrow / stop, with exact scope
2. Why the pilot ran: hypothesis, workflow step, problem addressed
3. Scope and baseline: case types, volume, exclusions, comparison source
4. Method: stage, sample size, case selection, who reviewed
5. Results by axis: time (net), quality (with denominator), risk (residual)
6. Failure catalogue: category, count, cause, repair and owner
7. Stakeholder concerns: concern, evidence, response or remaining action
8. Limits: what was not tested — cases, populations, conditions
9. Cost and effort: setup, review burden, training, upkeep, ownership
10. Recommendation and options: preferred path, alternatives, revisit triggers
11. Open questions: each with an owner and a next step
12. Appendix: rubric, sample questions, raw counts, control map

Separate observation from interpretation: "four drafts repeated an identifier" is an observation; "the risk is acceptable" is a judgment. Put failures, uncertainty, and costs in the main body. A readout of successes only invites suspicion about omissions.

Stakeholder Typical concern Evidence to present
Senior leadership Net benefit, decision risk Net time, quality trend, cost model, options
Frontline staff Workload, changing job content Review-time data, overrides, feedback, task mix
Compliance or legal Policy adherence, auditability Control map, escalation log, recorded approvals
Security or IT Access and data flows Connector scope, permission list, logging notes
Families or customers Correct answer, access to a person FAQ test results, handoff statistics, human route
Finance Cost per case and upkeep Setup and review effort, maintenance owner, volume sensitivity

Worked scenario

A family support desk handles roughly nine hundred questions a week, mostly about deadlines, document submission, and process steps; about half are repeats. A team lead proposes using an assistant to draft replies. An analyst records a baseline: nine minutes median handling time, six of them locating guidance and writing, and a three percent sampled error rate in audited replies.

The first decision is scope. Nothing is structurally broken: answers are generally correct and a reviewer already checks every external reply. The team chooses augmentation. The assistant sits after intake and suggests a draft with source pointers. Intake, the knowledge base, the reviewer gate, and the system of record stay where they are.

The hypothesis is narrow: for routine questions whose current guidance is in the knowledge base, a reviewer should accept the draft with minor edits and should not need more review time than today. The prototype is an offline replay of 180 anonymized closed cases, stratified to include common questions, rare legitimate ones, multi-intent messages, sensitive details, and cases relying on older documents.

The results are mixed in a useful way. Eighty-one percent of drafts matched current guidance, twelve percent were partially correct, and seven percent were wrong. Every wrong answer traces to one of two causes: three stale knowledge documents, or a question containing two intents where the draft answered only the first. Drafting time falls from about six minutes to two; review time rises from one and a half to two and a half because reviewers check sources. The net saving is three minutes per case, not four. One draft repeats an account identifier into a reply. The reviewer catches it, but the team converts it into a stop condition rather than treating it as a near miss to forget.

The boundary is now explicit. Output remains a draft; no generated text goes directly to a family. Replies with account-specific details trigger a mandatory check of the secure-channel route. Multi-intent questions are split before drafting. The knowledge coordinator updates the three stale documents before any live use. Sample-based review is rejected for now because the failure rate is not yet stable.

The readout requests one decision: approve a limited live pilot with mandatory review for routine cases only. It presents the net three-minute saving, the 81-percent figure with its denominator, the cause analysis, and residual risk of a missed escalation. It states what was not tested: peak-volume weeks, languages other than the primary one, and cases needing specialist escalation. It lists setup cost and ongoing review burden, not only savings.

Anticipated questions are handled directly. On jobs, the team lead describes a shift in task mix and says a replay supports no staffing conclusion; workforce planning owns that conversation. On accuracy, the analyst gives the measured figure with its boundary and explains the review gate. On policy, the compliance owner confirms the data-handling rule, and the response bank points later inquiries to the current policy document.

The decision is to proceed with the narrow pilot. The team also records a contingency: if wrong answers remain frequent after the knowledge fixes, it will not simply add more review. It will reconsider the design — for example, restricting the assistant to routing while keeping policy answers entirely human-authored. That is the difference between redesign based on evidence and redesign based on mood.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the tool and looking for a step to attach it to.
  • Calling a pilot successful because the output reads well, without measuring against a source.
  • Measuring drafting time while ignoring the time reviewers spend checking drafts.
  • Building a test set from easy questions that flatter the system.
  • Treating a confident answer as a grounded answer.
  • Leaving an approval gate that nobody has the authority or time to operate.
  • Publishing pilot percentages as guarantees about all case types.
  • Answering jobs, accuracy, or policy questions from memory instead of from owners and documents.
  • Redesigning before evidence shows that the structure, not the execution, is the problem.

Chapter summary

  • A workflow is steps plus trust gates; an assistant changes step costs and can move the bottleneck.
  • Augmentation changes one step while keeping roles, gates, and systems intact.
  • Redesign changes structure and follows evidence that the old sequence no longer fits.
  • Prototypes begin with a falsifiable hypothesis and match fidelity to the question.
  • Test sets include common cases, edge cases, multi-intent requests, sensitive details, refusals, and stale sources.
  • FAQ testing scores intent, grounding, currency, boundaries, completeness, presentation, and privacy separately.
  • Generated content enters as a draft or suggestion by default; mandatory approval precedes external or high-consequence actions.
  • Value is reported as measured time, quality, and risk, with denominators and net effects.
  • Limits fall into cannot, may not, and should not; verification and accountability stay human.
  • A pilot readout leads with the decision requested and includes failures, costs, untested areas, and owners.

Checkpoint questions

  1. A support process produces correct replies, but analysts spend most of their time finding guidance and writing drafts. A reviewer already checks every reply before it is sent. What is the most proportionate first change?

A. Redesign intake, approval, and routing at the same time. B. Insert a suggested draft with source pointers at the drafting step and keep the reviewer gate. C. Allow the assistant to send replies once its drafts look fluent. D. Focus only on prompt wording and ignore workflow design.

  1. Which observation most strongly suggests redesign rather than augmentation?

A. Drafts need minor wording edits before acceptance. B. Review time increases slightly because reviewers check sources. C. Drafting becomes almost instantaneous and cases pile up at a weekly approval batch. D. A pilot uses a smaller sample than the full population.

  1. A team wants the cheapest early check of whether a proposed sequence makes sense before using real cases. Which approach fits?

A. A staged expansion to all case types. B. A desk walkthrough using a fixed set of worked examples. C. A live pilot with no human gate. D. Publishing generated answers and collecting complaints.

  1. Which sample set would best test a family-facing FAQ flow?

A. Only the twenty most frequently asked questions. B. Only questions answered correctly in an earlier trial. C. A mix of common and rare questions, multi-intent requests, sensitive details, refusals, and stale-source cases. D. Only questions written in perfect, unambiguous language.

  1. What makes a human approval gate meaningful?

A. The word "reviewed" appears in the process diagram. B. A reviewer has competence, time, evidence, and authority to reject output, and the decision is recorded. C. The assistant is instructed to say that a person is in the loop. D. Review happens monthly for a random sample, regardless of case type.

  1. A pilot cut drafting from six minutes to two, but review rose from one and a half minutes to three. How should this be reported?

A. Report a four-minute saving and leave review out of the figure. B. Report the net three-minute saving with conditions and denominator, and note that capacity gains are unproven. C. Report only the percentage improvement in drafting speed. D. Report that the assistant eliminated the review problem.

  1. A colleague asks, "Will this replace staff?" Which response is most appropriate?

A. Promise that no role will ever change as a result of the pilot. B. Say the concern is unfounded and move to efficiency metrics. C. Describe the shift in task mix, state that a pilot cannot support staffing conclusions, and point to the workforce planning process. D. Guarantee that productivity gains will outweigh any staffing impact.

Answers and rationale

  1. Correct: B. The process is accurate and already has a trusted gate, so the localized change is to assist drafting while preserving review. A is wrong because simultaneous redesign of several stages is disproportionate without evidence of structural failure and makes the effect impossible to isolate. C is wrong because removing the gate exposes an external audience to unverified output before any evidence supports it. D is wrong because prompt wording cannot settle where assistance belongs or how it is controlled.

  2. Correct: C. When a formerly expensive step becomes nearly free and work accumulates at a later gate, the bottleneck has moved and the old structure protects the wrong thing. A is a normal effect of edits and does not indicate structural failure. B is an expected cost to measure, not proof the design is obsolete. D concerns the pilot's precision, not the workflow's shape.

  3. Correct: B. A desk walkthrough with fixed examples is the cheapest way to test whether a sequence is coherent before touching live cases. A is a later stage that assumes the design already survived testing. C creates avoidable risk and answers a different question. D exposes real people to errors and yields unreliable evidence.

  4. Correct: C. A representative mix exposes the failures that matter, including rare cases, multiple intents, sensitive information, correct refusals, and unreliable knowledge. A misses edge-case behavior. B is biased toward flattering results. D excludes the vague, shortened requests that arrive in practice.

  5. Correct: B. A gate is a control only when a capable person has the information and authority to reject output and the decision is recorded. A changes nothing in practice. C is an assurance in wording, not a control. D ignores case risk and cannot catch problems before external actions.

  6. Correct: B. Net effect, conditions, and denominator let leadership judge the result, and noting that capacity is unproven prevents an unsupported leap. A hides the review cost and overstates the benefit. C says nothing about the end-to-end result. D contradicts the evidence that review remained necessary.

  7. Correct: C. This response is respectful, factual, and clear about the limits of a pilot and the proper owner of staffing decisions. A is not credible and removes the topic from the right forum. B destroys trust and suppresses useful information. D turns an uncertain trade-off into a guarantee the evidence cannot support.

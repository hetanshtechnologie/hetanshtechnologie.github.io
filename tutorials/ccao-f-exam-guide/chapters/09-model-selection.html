Model Selection: Capability, Speed, Cost, and Risk

Choosing a model looks trivial until you must defend the choice. In a casual chat it hardly registers; in a work setting it becomes a design decision with consequences. A model that is too small may mishandle the ambiguous parts of a task; one that is too large may cost more, answer more slowly, and still produce the same result as a cheaper option. The foundations-level exam treats model selection as a reasoning exercise, not a recall exercise.

The aim is a way of thinking that works even when the names are unfamiliar. Capability tiers, workload signals, latency, cost, and risk are the durable concepts; brand names are the wrapping. By the end you should read a scenario, name the deciding constraint, and justify a tier choice without recognizing a product.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the generic capability tiers — compact, balanced, and capable — and the workload signals that favor each.
  • Recognize high-volume classification and extraction as compact-tier candidates, and multi-source analysis and ambiguous judgment as capable-tier candidates.
  • Explain why the cost of a wrong answer is a primary selection criterion, not an afterthought.
  • Treat latency and the waiting user as real constraints on a choice.
  • Explain what extended thinking or longer reasoning buys, and when the extra cost is justified.
  • Distinguish choosing one model globally from routing workflow steps to different tiers.
  • Compute and interpret cost per successful task instead of focusing on headline prices.
  • Reason from a scenario's constraints rather than brand recall when answering exam questions.

Key concepts

Model selection is a design decision, not a preference

It is tempting to reach for the most capable model for everything, the way one might always drive the largest vehicle in the lot. That instinct is expensive and often fails to improve the outcome. A model choice is better understood as a match between the shape of the work and the shape of the tool. The question stops being "which model is best" and becomes "which model is good enough for this task, under these constraints, at this price, with this level of acceptable risk."

That reframe matters because scenario questions contain a deciding constraint. One question may hinge on thousands of simple items processed cheaply; another on a single mistaken conclusion that triggers an expensive review. Both might involve the same assistant but point to opposite ends of the range. A candidate who memorized a product hierarchy has to guess; one who identified the constraint can reason.

Three ideas do most of the work: different tasks need different amounts of reasoning; each extra unit of capability costs time or money, so spend it where it changes the outcome; and the harm an error causes sets the floor for how much care a task deserves. Nearly every recommendation below applies one of these.

Capability tiers before brand names

Before any product name enters the conversation, speak in generic tiers. These are not official categories and do not map neatly to a vendor's catalogue; they are a reasoning vocabulary.

The compact tier covers smaller, faster models built for well-bounded tasks. They handle clear instructions, short outputs, and repeating patterns, and excel when a task leaves little room for interpretation. Their weakness shows up when a request is vague, several sources must be reconciled, or a lot of context must be held at once.

The balanced tier sits in the middle. It handles broad everyday knowledge work: drafting, summarizing, routine analysis, and questions needing some judgment but not heroic reasoning. For many teams it is the sensible default, a compromise between speed, cost, and competence.

The capable tier contains the largest, most reasoning-intensive models. These are the tools for genuinely hard tasks: conflicting evidence, subtle trade-offs, multi-step inference, or a decision where a shallow answer would be costly. They are slower and more expensive, which is acceptable because the task is high-value or high-risk. Using them on trivial work is like assigning a senior specialist to sort the mail.

Exam note: When a question asks you to select a model, translate the scenario into one of these tiers before looking at the options. The choice matching the tier implied by the constraints is usually correct, even if the model is unfamiliar.

Reading the workload: signals that point to a tier

Tiers are useful only if you can tell which one a task calls for. That means reading the workload for signals — any detail indicating how much reasoning, speed, or accuracy is needed.

Volume is one signal. If the same operation must be performed on thousands of items — sorting messages into categories, pulling a date and amount from forms — per-item cost and response time dominate, pushing toward compact. Ambiguity is another. If a small number of items must be weighed with conflicting signals and no clean combining rule, reasoning quality dominates and capable becomes attractive.

Structure matters too. A task with fixed output format and narrow input domain is predictable, and compact models excel at predictable work. One whose shape only becomes clear partway through — where the assistant must notice that two documents disagree and decide which to trust — rewards a stronger model. Consider consequence last: if a wrong answer is cheap to detect and fix, a lighter model and lighter review suffice; if it flows into a customer-facing document or regulated filing, they do not.

No single signal decides alone; ask which would change your mind. High-volume but high-stakes work should not be forced to the cheapest tier — the stakes override volume. A result reviewed line by line by a specialist needs less raw reasoning from the model. This weighting is what scenarios probe.

High-volume classification and extraction: the compact tier's home turf

Two task types recur in workflows and on the exam: classification and extraction.

Classification assigns an item to a category — routing messages to teams, labeling a ticket by urgency. The possible answers are limited, the input defined, and the correct label often checkable afterward. Because the output is small and the boundary usually clear, these are excellent compact-tier candidates. Running thousands through a capable model spends money without buying much.

Extraction pulls specific fields out of unstructured text. Given a contract, return the parties, effective date, and renewal term; given an invoice, return vendor, total, and tax. Extraction is harder than classification because values must be reproduced exactly and may be buried in noise, but it is still bounded: the fields are known in advance and no opinion is requested. A compact or balanced model handles routine extraction well when formats are consistent and fields named explicitly.

Both can be harder than they look: categories overlap and extraction may need interpretation of an ambiguous phrase. A compact-tier deployment should detect hard cases, having the compact model handle the bulk and flag low-confidence items for a second pass by a stronger model or reviewer. That is routing in miniature: average cost stays low without ignoring the difficult tail.

Common mistake: Assuming a "simple" task needs no verification. High-volume tasks fail in volume: a one-percent error rate across ten thousand items is a hundred errors. Budget for sampling even when each call is cheap.

Multi-source analysis and ambiguous judgment: when you need the capable tier

At the other end sit tasks where difficulty lies in the thinking. Multi-source analysis asks the assistant to read several documents, see how they relate, and produce a conclusion accounting for all of them. Sources may disagree, use different terminology, or cover different periods. The assistant must decide what is comparable, what is outdated, and what to do with contradictions. This is where a compact model most often produces a smooth but shallow answer that misses a conflict.

Ambiguous judgment is similar. These are tasks that cannot be reduced to a rule: deciding whether a borderline case meets a policy's intent, weighing several reasonable options, or interpreting an open-ended instruction. They require holding competing considerations in mind and reasoning about trade-offs. Capable models are more likely to surface tension explicitly, qualify a conclusion, and avoid the overconfident simplification a smaller model might offer.

The reason to pay for this tier is not eloquence but a better chance of getting the reasoning right. When output drives an expensive-to-reverse decision, the incremental price of stronger reasoning is small compared with a wrong turn. That is the cost-of-a-wrong-answer principle, the most reliable justification for capable.

There is a boundary. A capable model does not make a task verifiable. If facts are missing or sources unreliable, more reasoning cannot manufacture truth. The response is to gather evidence or ask the assistant to flag what it cannot determine — not to upgrade the model and hope. On the exam, an option offering a stronger model as the fix for unverifiable information is usually a distractor.

The cost of a wrong answer as a selection criterion

If one idea should carry into every question, it is this: the cost of a wrong answer is a first-class selection criterion. Prices are easy to compare because they are numbers on a page; error costs are harder because they are entangled with context, but they matter more.

Distinguish the harm of the error from the cost of detecting and correcting it. A typo in an internal note may cost nothing; a fabricated citation in a customer report may damage credibility and require a public correction. The same error has wildly different consequences. Price the downstream harm, then choose a tier and review process whose combined cost is less than the harm prevented.

This yields a symmetry: the more expensive the error, the more to spend on both capability and verification. High-stakes tasks justify a capable model because better reasoning lowers the chance of a serious mistake, but also human review because no model is infallible. Cheap, reversible tasks justify a compact model and light sampling, because spending more would not pay.

The exam surrounds a simple-looking task with a consequence. Generating short product descriptions looks like compact-tier drafting — until you notice they will be published without review across a large catalogue. Now error cost includes brand reputation, and the answer shifts toward a stronger model plus approval. The surface task did not change; the constraint did.

Latency and the person waiting on the other side

Cost is not the only resource a model consumes. In interactive settings latency — the delay from request to response — is often more visible. A capable model that reasons at length may take noticeably longer than a compact one, changing the experience of the person waiting.

Consider where the assistant sits. Embedded in a live customer interaction, every extra second is felt and may affect satisfaction or abandonment. There, a fast compact model answering adequately can beat a slower capable model answering slightly better, especially for routine questions. Used asynchronously — a report prepared overnight — latency matters far less, so capable-tier quality is affordable.

Latency also interacts with iteration. Quick responses let a worker try several phrasings and refine in a tight loop; slow ones make each iteration costly, so people iterate less and settle. That is a hidden cost of using a large model for exploratory work. A productive pattern is to explore fast, then run the final version through a stronger model.

Extended thinking and longer reasoning as a paid option

Many model families offer a mode that lets the assistant spend more computation reasoning before answering — extended thinking or longer reasoning. It is not a different model so much as a setting, and the extra work is typically billed as additional usage.

The payoff is specific, not general. Longer reasoning helps on problems with multiple steps, where an early wrong turn derails everything, and when the assistant must check assumptions, reconcile conflicting evidence, or plan before writing. It helps less on recall, formatting, or simple transformation, where there is no hidden depth. Paying for it there is like paying for a longer runway at an airfield that only handles short hops.

Treat it as a paid option with a specific payoff. Ask what the reasoning is expected to change. "It might be better" is weak; "it should catch the inconsistency between two sources a quick pass would miss" is concrete. On the exam, extended thinking is correct when the scenario stresses multi-step reasoning, conflicting information, or high error cost, and wrong when the task is high-volume, simple, or latency-sensitive.

Extended reasoning is not accuracy; it can produce a thorough explanation of a wrong premise. It helps when the task rewards careful thought and inputs are sound, but cannot substitute for good data or verification. Offering it as the fix for a fabricated source is wrong.

Model families and the small, medium, and large pattern

The discussion has stayed generic, which mirrors the exam. In practice a provider often releases a family: small, medium, and large variants built on similar design but tuned for different points on the speed, cost, and capability curve. They usually share an interface, making it easy to swap one for another in the same workflow.

The small variant favors speed and economy, the natural choice for high-volume bounded tasks. The medium variant aims for the middle ground and often becomes the workhorse. The large variant targets the hardest tasks, offering deepest reasoning at the highest price and greatest latency. Some families also include specialized variants, such as one tuned for long inputs, but the basic pattern suffices.

You are not expected to memorize which variant belongs to which family or has the longest context window. Map a scenario to a tier, then select the option whose stated characteristics match. A question might describe four unnamed models and ask which suits a task; the answer is the one whose trade-offs — fast and economical, or slow and deeply reasoning — align with the workload signals and error costs. Brand recognition is a shortcut at best and a trap at worst, because names change while the reasoning stays constant.

Exam note: If a question hinges on a detail you could only know by memorizing a specification, re-read it. Foundations-level questions are answerable from the scenario; the deciding fact is stated in the situation, not hidden in a catalogue.

Routing between tiers rather than one global choice

A common mistake is picking one model for an entire application. That "one global choice" is convenient but wasteful, because a workflow is a chain of different kinds of work with different needs. A single compromise is rarely optimal for any part.

Routing sends different steps to different tiers. A document pipeline might use a compact model to classify items, a balanced model to extract fields, and a capable model only for the small fraction where sources conflict. A drafting workflow might use a fast model for several rough options and a stronger one to refine the chosen option. The result is better average quality at lower average cost.

Routing works best when handoff criteria are explicit. "Send hard cases to the stronger model" is too vague. "Escalate when extracted fields disagree, when confidence is below a threshold, or when the item matches a flagged category" is testable: you can measure how often escalation triggers and whether it improves outcomes.

The cost is complexity: something must decide where each item goes and pass context forward. For a small, low-volume workflow that may not be worth it; one balanced model suffices. Routing earns its keep when volume is high, tasks heterogeneous, or price differences large enough that savings outweigh the machinery.

Measuring cost per successful task

Headline prices are seductive because they are simple. But a cheap model that fails often can cost more than an expensive one that succeeds reliably. A practical process measures cost per successful task rather than price per call.

Define success for the task — a correctly labeled item, an extracted field that passes a check, a summary accepted without revision. Over a representative sample, count how many attempts each model needs and add rework cost. Divide total spend by successes. This captures price and reliability together, and often reverses the ranking from sticker prices alone.

A compact model succeeding on ninety percent of routine items, with quick correction, keeps cost per success low. Another cheap model failing on thirty percent of items whose errors are hard to spot triggers full review each time; its cost per success may exceed a capable model working first time. The price per call did not change — the error cost did.

The metric also quantifies routing: if most items are handled cheaply and a few escalate, blended cost per success can be below either tier alone. That lets you defend choosing neither the cheapest nor the best model — you minimized the cost of a correct result.

cost per successful task =
    (cost of all model calls + cost of review and rework)
    / (number of tasks that met the success definition)

Where:
  cost of model calls   = calls x price per call
  cost of review/rework = reviewer time + any repeat generation
  successes             = tasks accepted without correction

The numerator includes human time. A "free" model generating output needing extensive checking is not free. Including review cost keeps the calculation honest and aligned with the cost-of-a-wrong-answer principle.

A decision matrix for model selection

The matrix maps workload signals to a likely tier and the main reason, expressed in task characteristics rather than product names.

Workload signal Likely tier Primary reason
Thousands of items with fixed labels Compact Per-item cost and speed dominate; little reasoning needed
Routine extraction from consistent documents Compact to balanced Bounded output; format reliability matters more than depth
Everyday drafting, summarizing, light analysis Balanced Broad competence at moderate cost and latency
Reconciling several sources that disagree Capable Must detect conflicts and weigh conflicting evidence
Ambiguous judgment with expensive consequences Capable Reasoning quality lowers the chance of a costly error
Live interaction where waiting is visible Compact to balanced Low latency protects the user experience
Background or asynchronous batch work Capable if needed Latency is cheap, so quality can take priority
Errors are hard to detect Capable plus review Better reasoning and verification both justified
Errors are cheap and obvious Compact Spending more would not pay for itself
Mixed workflow with easy and hard cases Routed tiers Match each step to the minimum capability it needs

Read this as a starting point, not a rulebook. When two rows conflict, the deciding factor is usually the cost of a wrong answer — it outranks convenience and often volume. A high-volume, high-consequence task deserves routing that keeps average cost low while protecting the difficult tail.

What the exam is really asking: reasoning from the scenario

Model-selection questions are not tests of product knowledge but of whether you can find the deciding constraint. It is usually one of four things: the volume and repetitiveness of work, the ambiguity or reasoning depth demanded, the latency allowed, or the cost of getting it wrong.

A reliable approach has four moves: identify the task type and volume; the consequence of an error and how detectable it is; any stated latency or budget constraint; then choose the smallest adequate tier, escalating only where justified. That last move captures the exam's preference for proportionate choices — over-engineering is as wrong as under-engineering.

Watch for distractors solving the wrong problem. If the difficulty is missing source documents, a larger model is not the answer. If it is a customer waiting in real time, extended reasoning on every turn is not the answer. If it is uniform high-volume extraction, a capable model throughout is unlikely. Each fixes a different axis from the one causing trouble.

Selection is only one control among several: prompt quality, grounding, and human review all shape the outcome, and a strong model with a vague prompt can underperform a balanced model with a clear one. An option combining a tier choice with a better prompt or review step is often most complete.

Worked scenario

A regional services organization receives a steady stream of customer messages. A small team reads each, assigns it to one of six departments, and extracts the account reference and service date. The team lead wants to reduce manual load. Two constraints are stated: volume is around two thousand messages per week, and a misrouted message can sit unattended for days, delaying service. Most messages are short and clear, but a small share mention two services or phrase the date in a way needing interpretation.

First, classify the work. The core operations — classification and extraction — are bounded tasks with known outputs, strongly signaling compact tier, because per-item price and response time matter at two thousand a week. If the brief ended there, the answer would be a compact model with periodic sampling for drift.

Second, weigh the consequence. A misrouted message causes real delay, so errors are not trivial — but they are detectable: a department can spot a wrong route, and a suspect date can be checked against the original text. Because errors are visible rather than silent, the design can rely on a lightweight correction path rather than the most expensive model for everything.

Third, handle the ambiguous tail. Messages mentioning two services or unusual date phrasing are exactly where a compact model is most likely to guess. The design is routing: compact processes the clear majority, and any message matching an ambiguity rule (two service names detected, no date found, or confidence below a threshold) escalates to a capable model. A human reviews a random sample of the rest.

Fourth, define success and measure. Success is a message routed correctly with accurate reference and date, accepted without correction. Over a trial batch of a few hundred, the team records how many items each path handles, how often escalation triggers, and how many errors slip through, then computes cost per successful task for three options. Routed should win because it spends cheap processing on the easy majority and reserves stronger reasoning for the difficult minority.

The resolution is neither "pick the best model" nor "pick the cheapest." It is a routed design with explicit escalation rules, light human sampling, and a measurement plan. Extended reasoning on every message ignores volume and latency; a capable model for all ignores the bounded, repetitive nature of most work; a compact model with no checks ignores the cost of misrouting. Only the routed design accounts for every stated constraint.

Exam note: When a scenario contains both a high-volume and a high-consequence signal, look for the answer satisfying both rather than picking a side. Routing and selective escalation are the usual way.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the most capable model for every task, ignoring cost and latency without improving bounded work.
  • Choosing the cheapest model on price per call alone, without considering failure rates and rework.
  • Treating accuracy as a property of the model alone, when prompts, grounding, and review also determine the result.
  • Recommending a larger model to fix a problem caused by missing or unreliable source material.
  • Ignoring the cost of a wrong answer, often the deciding constraint in a scenario.
  • Overlooking latency when a person is waiting in a live interaction.
  • Using extended reasoning on simple, high-volume, or latency-sensitive tasks where it adds cost without changing the outcome.
  • Applying one model globally to a heterogeneous workflow instead of routing steps to appropriate tiers.
  • Escalating vaguely ("send hard ones to the stronger model") without explicit, testable criteria.
  • Assuming product names matter on the exam, when questions are answerable from workload signals and constraints.

Chapter summary

  • Capability tiers — compact, balanced, capable — are a reasoning vocabulary that precedes any brand name.
  • Compact models suit high-volume classification and bounded extraction; capable models suit multi-source analysis and ambiguous judgment with costly consequences.
  • The cost of a wrong answer is a first-class criterion and usually the deciding one in scenario questions.
  • Latency is a real constraint: live interactions favor faster tiers, background work can afford deeper reasoning.
  • Extended thinking is a paid option with a specific payoff on multi-step or conflict-heavy problems, not a general accuracy upgrade.
  • Model families commonly offer small, medium, and large variants, but the exam asks you to reason from scenario characteristics, not recall specifications.
  • Routing different steps to different tiers beats a single global choice when volume is high and tasks heterogeneous.
  • Cost per successful task, including rework and review, is a better metric than price per call.
  • The smallest adequate tier plus proportionate verification is the sound default.

Checkpoint questions

  1. An analyst must assign forty thousand short messages to one of five fixed categories weekly. Labels are well defined and errors easy to spot later. Which tier is the most appropriate starting point, and why?

  2. A team must reconcile three internal reports disagreeing on a key figure before publishing a summary to leadership. A wrong conclusion would be costly to reverse. Which tier and additional control do you recommend, and why?

  3. A customer-facing chat answers routine questions in real time; customers abandon if responses are slow. Why might a compact or balanced model be preferable to a capable one here?

  4. A proposal enables extended thinking on every request in a high-volume extraction pipeline because "more reasoning means more accuracy." What is wrong with this, and when is extended thinking justified?

  5. A model family offers small, medium, and large variants. An exam question names four unfamiliar models and asks which suits a task with conflicting sources and expensive errors. How should a candidate reason to the answer?

  6. Model A costs less per call but fails on a fifth of tasks, each failure needing a full manual redo. Model B costs more but rarely fails. Which measure should decide between them, and how is it calculated?

  7. A workflow has three steps: classifying items, extracting standard fields, and resolving the small share where sources conflict. Why is a routed design likely to outperform a single model throughout?

Answers and rationale

  1. Compact tier. The workload is high-volume, repetitive, bounded, and easy to verify — classic classification. Compact keeps per-item cost and latency low. Balanced works but spends more without changing the outcome; capable is unjustified given little reasoning depth and detectable errors; "no verification needed" is wrong because volume multiplies errors.

  2. Capable tier plus human review. Conflicting sources require detecting and reasoning about disagreement, and the costly consequence raises required care. Review is still needed because a model cannot verify facts the sources do not settle. Compact may smooth over the conflict; a larger model alone confuses capability with verification; extended thinking alone misses the independent check.

  3. Latency dominates in a live interaction. A faster model answering adequately protects the experience and keeps iteration cheap; the slightly better answer does not compensate for visible waiting on routine questions. Capable throughout ignores latency; extended reasoning on every turn adds delay; "latency does not matter for quality" is false interactively.

  4. It adds cost without addressing the task. Routine extraction has little hidden depth, so extended thinking rarely changes the answer and raises price and latency. It is justified on the subset where multi-step reasoning or conflicting evidence affects the result. "More reasoning always means more accuracy" is false; "use it for escalated hard cases" is correct; "drop verification because reasoning is deeper" is wrong because reasoning is not verification.

  5. Map the scenario to a tier, then match stated trade-offs. Conflicting sources and expensive errors point to capable, so select the option described as slowest, most expensive, and deepest in reasoning. Picking a familiar name is unreliable; choosing the largest number ignores stated characteristics; assuming one variant does everything equally well contradicts the tier pattern.

  6. Cost per successful task: total model cost plus review and rework, divided by tasks meeting the success definition. This folds reliability into the comparison. Price per call ignores failures; "whichever is faster" ignores error cost; "always pick the reliable one" is incomplete because the calculation may still favor the cheaper model if its failures are cheap to fix.

  7. Routing matches each step to the minimum capability needed. Classification and extraction are bounded and high-volume, so compact or balanced handling is economical; only conflicting-source cases need stronger reasoning, and escalating just those keeps average cost low while protecting the risky tail. Capable for all overspends on easy work; compact for all risks ambiguous cases; "one model is always simpler and better" ignores the volume and cost differences that justify routing.

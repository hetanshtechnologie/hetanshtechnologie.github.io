Task-Family Playbooks: Analysis, Research, Drafting, and Ideation

Most requests made of an AI assistant fall into a handful of recurring shapes. A manager asks which of two suppliers to choose. A researcher wants a summary of what is known, with sources. A writer needs a first draft of a customer email. A team wants possible names for a new service. These look unrelated, but each belongs to a family with its own goal, ideal prompt shape, and characteristic way of failing.

Name the family and you can predict what the assistant needs before you type, and where the answer is likely to go wrong. This chapter turns four families — analysis, research, drafting, and ideation — into practical playbooks, shows how to combine them into one workflow, and explains how to write a prompt brief a colleague could reuse without guessing what you meant.

Learning objectives

  • Recognize four recurring task families — analysis, research, drafting, and ideation — and the prompt shape each one rewards.
  • Write an analysis prompt that names the decision to be made and the evidence the assistant is allowed to use.
  • Structure research requests around sources, dates, and explicit uncertainty labels.
  • Supply audience, purpose, tone, and model documents when requesting a draft.
  • Separate divergent idea generation from evaluation so neither activity undermines the other.
  • Combine families into a single workflow and write a prompt brief another person can reuse.

Key concepts

Why task families matter

A prompt is a contract about what kind of thinking the assistant should perform. "Tell me about our customer churn" could be analysis (which customers are leaving and why, so we can decide), research (what is known in our industry, with sources), drafting (a memo to the board), or ideation (possible interventions). The words are identical; the expected answer is not.

Each family rewards different inputs and fails in different ways. Analysis needs a decision and a boundary around the evidence. Research needs provenance and a stance toward uncertainty. Drafting needs an audience and a model of the desired voice. Ideation needs permission to generate before judging. Give one family's inputs while expecting another's output and you get a plausible answer that quietly misses the point.

The family also tells you where to verify. For research, check citations and dates first; for a draft, the register and its match to the model document; for analysis, that a decision was made and reasoning stayed inside the evidence.

Exam note: Scenario questions often hide the family in the wording. "Recommend," "decide," and "choose" signal analysis; "find," "summarize what is known," and "cite" signal research; "write," "rewrite," and "for an audience of" signal drafting; "list possibilities," "brainstorm," and "suggest options" signal ideation. Name the family before judging the answer.

The analysis family: decide from stated evidence

Analysis is the family of decisions. The assistant is asked to weigh evidence and land on a conclusion, not produce prose for its own sake. A useful prompt states two things early: the decision to be made, and the evidence the assistant is allowed to use.

Compare "Should we switch providers?" with "Decide whether we should switch from our current provider to the proposed one. Use only the two bids attached and the service notes from the last two quarters. The criteria are cost, reliability, and switching effort, in that order. Recommend one option, list the evidence for it, and name the strongest counterargument." The second version constrains the reasoning: it says what counts and asks for a verdict rather than a survey.

Four components make an analysis prompt reliable:

  1. The decision. Name the choice explicitly. "Choose one," "rank these three," or "determine whether the evidence supports the claim" gives the assistant a target; without it, the response drifts into a neutral overview.
  2. The evidence boundary. Say what is in scope and what is not. "Use only the attached figures" or "ignore information published before last year" prevents gaps being filled with general knowledge that may not apply.
  3. The criteria. State what a good answer is judged against, and in what order if priority matters. Criteria turn a vague preference into a testable comparison.
  4. The output contract. Ask for a recommendation, the supporting evidence, and the main objection. Requiring the counterargument is a cheap way to surface weak reasoning.

Two useful variations are to ask the assistant to argue both sides before recommending one, which forces the strongest case for each option onto the page, and to attach a confidence label to the recommendation. When evidence conflicts, ask for each side with its source and then a statement of which the criteria favor, rather than letting the assistant silently choose the more convenient one. That keeps the decision traceable and gives a reviewer something concrete to challenge.

The failure signature of analysis is a non-answer dressed as insight: a balanced essay concluding "it depends." A second is boundary leakage, where outside assumptions, such as an industry average, are used as facts in the case. A third is false precision, where a recommendation sounds more certain than the evidence warrants.

Common mistake: Asking for "a detailed analysis" without naming the decision or the evidence. Detail is not usefulness. An analysis that cannot answer "so what should we do?" has not done its job, however long it is.

The research family: sources, dates, and uncertainty

Research is the family of provenance. The goal is not just information but information the reader can trace to something checkable. Hence the ideal research prompt asks for sources, dates, and uncertainty labels, and separates what is established from what is merely suggested.

A weak request is "Summarize what we know about the new reporting rules." The response may be fluent with no hint of where claims came from or whether the rules are current. A stronger request: "Summarize the main requirements. For each point, give the source and its publication date. Distinguish requirements in force from those still proposed. Where sources disagree or you are unsure, say so rather than smoothing over the gap. Finish with a list of what remains unverified."

Three habits define this family:

  • Ask for attribution at the level of the claim. A source list at the end is less useful than a source attached to each significant statement; claim-level attribution lets the reader check the one fact they doubt.
  • Ask for dates and version context. A fact true two years ago may be obsolete. Publication dates, effective dates, or a "current as of" marker make staleness visible.
  • Ask for uncertainty labels. Fluency and certainty are not the same. Mark statements as confirmed, likely, or uncertain, and name what would raise confidence. This turns vague doubt into an explicit, checkable signal.

Triangulation is the habit of checking a claim against more than one independent source. A single generated answer is a starting point, not a conclusion: ask for a second source on any claim that will drive a decision, and note where sources genuinely disagree instead of asking the assistant to resolve the disagreement for you.

The failure signatures cluster around missing provenance. An invented citation is the most serious, because it gives a fabricated claim a convincing anchor. Wrong attribution is subtler: the claim may be real but credited to a source that does not support it. Date failures mix current and outdated information as though equally valid. A single-source synthesis presents one document's view as consensus.

Exam note: When a scenario asks for research, the correct option usually adds source, date, and uncertainty requirements — not one that simply asks for "more detail" or "a comprehensive summary." Comprehensiveness without provenance increases the surface area for unverified claims.

The drafting family: audience, purpose, tone, and models

Drafting is the family of produced writing. The assistant creates text a real reader will read, so the prompt must describe that reader and the effect the text should have. Four inputs do most of the work: audience, purpose, tone, and a model document.

Audience says who is reading and what they know. "A non-technical customer new to this product" produces different prose from "an engineer reviewing it." Purpose states what the reader should do, feel, or understand: approve a budget, understand a change, feel reassured. Tone sets the register — formal, warm, direct, cautious — best made concrete by saying what to avoid, such as "no marketing language." Model documents are writing the organization already approves. A past email or report is a more reliable guide to house style than an adjective like "professional," which readers interpret differently.

A useful drafting prompt: "Write a short email to a customer whose order was delayed. The purpose is to explain the delay, give a new delivery date, and reassure without over-promising. Use a calm, direct tone. Follow the attached example: greeting, one-sentence acknowledgement, two short paragraphs, closing offer of help. Do not invent a reason for the delay; use only the reason in the notes. Keep it under 180 words."

Notice the prompt also constrains invention. Drafting invites the assistant to make text feel complete by supplying details that were not provided; telling it to use only the supplied reason closes that gap. A polished draft with a fabricated detail is worse than a plain draft without one.

The failure signatures concern voice and fidelity. Generic prose appears with no audience or purpose; wrong register when tone was implicit. Ignoring the model document produces a clean draft that does not resemble the organization's writing. Invented specifics — a made-up date, price, or commitment — appear whenever a gap is left for the assistant to fill.

Refinement works best when feedback names the defect. "Make it punchier" gives the assistant little to act on; "shorten the opening to one sentence and remove the three adjectives before the product name" is testable. Draft, critique against the audience and model, then revise — the same loop used for any output that matters.

Try it: Take a draft you already approve of and ask the assistant to produce a new one using only the audience, purpose, tone, and structure you can infer from it. Compare the result. The gaps you had to fill are the inputs your drafting prompts have been omitting.

The ideation family: diverge first, evaluate later

Ideation is the family of options. Its purpose is to expand the set of possibilities before any is judged. That single requirement — expansion before judgment — makes it different from the other families and easy to get wrong.

Assistants trained on human writing tend to evaluate as they generate. Ask for "good ideas for reducing support tickets" and you receive a short list of safe suggestions, each with a quiet explanation of why it is reasonable. Evaluation happened during generation and pruned the list before you saw the raw material. The fix is to make the two phases explicit and separate.

In the divergence phase, ask for quantity and variety, and forbid criticism. "List twenty different ways we could reduce support tickets. Do not evaluate, rank, or explain them. Include at least three unusual for our industry. Group only by broad approach." Because judgment is suspended, the assistant can offer options it might otherwise discard; a target number and a few unusual entries push past the obvious.

In the evaluation phase, apply criteria in a separate step: "From this list, select the five options cheapest to test within a month, and explain the trade-off for each." A separate turn lets you change the criteria later without regenerating the ideas, and prevents early preferences from shaping what you see.

One practical safeguard is to keep the raw list. If evaluation happens in the same message that generated the ideas, you lose the options discarded too early and cannot revisit them when the criteria change.

Three failure signatures are typical. Premature criticism produces a small, cautious list with reasons attached. Clustering produces many options that are one idea in different words. Anchoring fixates on the first suggestion, with the rest orbiting it. The remedies: forbid evaluation, demand variety across categories, and ask for options differing in underlying approach.

Common mistake: Asking for "the best ideas" during brainstorming. "Best" requires evaluation, collapsing the two phases. Ask for many and varied ideas first; ask for the best only after you can see the full set.

Comparing the four families

The table below is a quick reference for each family's goal, essential inputs, and characteristic failure. Memorize the middle and right columns: exam scenarios give you a goal and ask which input or fix is missing.

Task family Core goal Required inputs in the prompt Likely failure if inputs are missing
Analysis Reach a decision or verdict The decision, the allowed evidence, the criteria, the output contract Non-answer ("it depends"), outside assumptions, false confidence
Research Gather traceable information The question, source and date requirements, uncertainty labels, what is out of scope Invented or mismatched citations, stale facts, single-source consensus
Drafting Produce writing for a reader Audience, purpose, tone, model document, limits on invention Generic voice, wrong register, ignored model, fabricated details
Ideation Expand the set of options A quantity target, variety constraints, a ban on evaluation in the first phase Short cautious list, clustered ideas, evaluation before generation

Combining families in one workflow

Real work rarely stays inside one family. A recommendation usually means researching facts, analyzing them into a decision, drafting the note, and sometimes brainstorming alternatives to test it. Value comes from chaining families in a sensible order with a checkpoint between each.

Order follows dependency. You cannot analyze evidence you have not gathered, so research precedes analysis. You cannot write accurately about an undecided matter, so analysis precedes drafting. Ideation is flexible: before analysis it generates alternatives to compare, after drafting it stress-tests the conclusion.

A typical chain:

  1. Research: gather facts with sources and dates, and flag what is uncertain.
  2. Analysis: decide among options using only verified facts, state criteria, and give a recommendation with its strongest counterargument.
  3. Ideation: generate, without judging, ways the recommendation could fail or improve.
  4. Drafting: write the final note for the reader, using verified facts and the chosen recommendation, in the house style.

Each handoff is a checkpoint. Before analysis, confirm sources exist and uncertain items are labeled; unlabeled guesses must not become evidence. Before drafting, confirm the decision is settled. Skipping these checks lets an early error survive into the final document, where polished prose makes it look authoritative.

Combining families in one long prompt has a cost: asking for research, analysis, and a draft at once tends to produce a shallow result in all three, with no way to tell which part failed. Separate turns make each output inspectable, at the price of a slightly longer session.

Exam note: If a scenario describes a multi-step deliverable, choose the option that sequences families by dependency and inserts verification between steps. An option requesting everything in one prompt is usually the distractor, however detailed.

Writing a reusable prompt brief

A prompt brief is a short, self-contained document describing a recurring task well enough for someone else to run it. It survives staff changes, context loss, and the drift that comes from repeating a task with slightly different wording. A colleague should be able to read it, supply the named inputs, and get a result of predictable shape and quality.

A good brief contains the components already introduced, plus a few practical extras:

Brief component What it should state Why it matters
Task family Which of the four families the task belongs to Sets the expected shape of the answer
Purpose and decision What the output is for, and what choice it supports Prevents a drift into generic overviews
Inputs Exactly what material must be supplied and what is out of scope Keeps evidence inside a known boundary
Constraints Length, tone, terminology, and any prohibitions Makes the result consistent across runs
Output format The structure, headings, and any required labels Makes outputs comparable and easy to check
Verification step What a human must check before the output is used Puts the review boundary in writing
Version and owner role The revision marker and the role responsible for upkeep Lets readers know whether the brief is current

The verification step deserves emphasis: say what must be checked — sources present, decision stated, no invented figures — because a brief reused without a check propagates the same error many times.

The two briefs below tackle the same task. Compare them.

BRIEF A — WEAK
Write something about our competitor's new pricing. Make it good and fairly
detailed. Include anything useful you can find. We might use it in a slide.

BRIEF B — REUSABLE
Family: research feeding analysis.
Purpose: decide whether our list price needs to change this quarter.
Inputs: the competitor's published price sheet (attached), our last four
quarters of win/loss notes, and no other material.
Constraints: cite the source and date for each external claim; mark any
inferred figure as "inferred"; do not use data older than 12 months; no
more than 400 words.
Output: (1) a table of competitor tiers with sources and dates, (2) a short
recommendation on list price, (3) the strongest argument against it, (4) a
list of what could not be verified.
Verification: a reviewer confirms every cited figure against the source
sheet before the slide is drafted.
Version: v2, maintained by the pricing lead role.

Brief A names a topic and a vague quality bar; it leaves the family ambiguous, sets no evidence boundary, gives no format, and has no verification step. Two people using it would get unrelated outputs. Brief B states the family, decision, inputs, exclusions, citation rules, output structure, human check, and maintainer, so a colleague could run it next month and get something comparable.

Try it: Take a prompt you use often and add three lines: a verification step, an explicit out-of-scope statement, and a version marker. That is often enough to turn an instruction into a brief that travels.

Worked scenario

A regional operations team must decide whether to extend the hours of a busy service desk. The first attempt is a single prompt: "Tell me everything about extending service desk hours and write a proposal for the leadership group."

The response is confident and readable: general benefits, broad industry trends, a sample schedule, and a recommendation to extend hours. It looks complete and is almost unusable. The benefits are not specific to this desk, the trends have no sources, the schedule assumes headcount the team lacks, and the recommendation rests on no team data. The lead has received drafting prose with no research or analysis beneath it.

The lead restarts as a chain of families, with checkpoints.

Step one — research. The prompt limits the assistant to six months of ticket-volume records and the staffing roster. Each external claim must carry a source and date; inferences must be labeled and gaps listed. The response shows most after-hours contacts are password resets, volumes spike in a narrow evening window, and the roster has no capacity for a full evening shift. It flags missing holiday-week data. The lead checks cited figures against the source rows.

Step two — analysis. The prompt states the decision: "Choose one of three options — extend hours, add self-service password reset, or do neither — using only the verified research." Criteria, in order: implementation cost, reduction in after-hours contacts, effect on staff. It asks for a recommendation, evidence, and the strongest counterargument. The assistant recommends self-service password reset, which addresses the dominant contact type at low cost, and names the counterargument: customers unable to use the tool will still need help. No industry averages or invented headcount appear.

Step three — ideation. Before drafting: "List fifteen ways the recommendation could fail or be improved. Do not evaluate them. Include at least three involving no new software." This surfaces access concerns for customers with limited connectivity and the need for a fallback path. The lead evaluates the list separately and adds a fallback requirement.

Step four — drafting. Only now: the audience (leadership, which wants cost and risk), purpose (approve a pilot), tone (direct, cautious, no sales language), the structure of a previously approved proposal attached as the model, and a one-page limit. No figure may appear that is not in the verified research. The draft is short, traceable, and in a familiar voice.

The assistant did not become more capable. Each family received the inputs it needed, outputs were inspected at handoffs, and the error-prone parts — sourcing, deciding, generating options — were kept separate from the polished writing that could hide mistakes.

Exam note: A draft that "looks polished but is not grounded" is almost always a sequencing problem. Insert research and analysis before drafting; do not ask the assistant to rewrite the draft more carefully.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every request as a generic prompt and ignoring which family it belongs to.
  • Asking for analysis without naming the decision, then accepting a balanced overview as an answer.
  • Requesting research without source, date, or uncertainty requirements and treating fluent prose as verified.
  • Writing a draft with no audience, purpose, model document, or limit on invented details.
  • Evaluating ideas while generating them, which produces a short and cautious brainstorm.
  • Combining several families in one long prompt and losing the ability to check any step.
  • Omitting the verification step and the version marker from a brief that will be reused.

Chapter summary

  • Task families — analysis, research, drafting, and ideation — each reward a different prompt shape and fail in a characteristic way.
  • Analysis needs a decision, an evidence boundary, criteria, and an output contract; its failure is a non-answer or outside assumptions.
  • Research needs claim-level sources, dates, and uncertainty labels; its failure is missing or mismatched provenance.
  • Drafting needs audience, purpose, tone, a model document, and a ban on invented specifics; its failure is wrong voice or fabricated detail.
  • Ideation needs separate divergence and evaluation phases; its failure is premature judgment and clustered ideas.
  • Combined workflows should follow dependency order with checkpoints, so errors do not survive into a polished final draft.
  • A reusable prompt brief states the family, inputs, constraints, format, verification step, and version, so a colleague can run it consistently.

Checkpoint questions

  1. An analyst asks an assistant to "analyze our two Warehouse options" and receives a long, even-handed comparison ending with "the choice depends on priorities." Which single change to the prompt best addresses the problem?

A. Ask for a longer comparison with more detail on each warehouse.

B. Name the decision explicitly, state the allowed evidence and criteria, and require a recommendation with the strongest counterargument.

C. Ask the assistant to write the response in a more formal tone.

D. Request that the assistant use a capable model instead of a compact one.

  1. A research response reads smoothly but includes a figure attributed to a report that does not contain it, and mixes last year's policy with the current one. Which prompt requirement would have caught both problems earliest?

A. Ask for a shorter summary.

B. Ask for claim-level sources with dates, and a label for anything uncertain or outdated.

C. Ask for the response in bullet points.

D. Ask the assistant to avoid technical terms.

  1. A customer email drafted by an assistant is grammatically clean but sounds nothing like the organization's other messages and promises a delivery date that was never supplied. Which inputs were most likely missing?

A. A longer word count and more paragraphs.

B. A capable model and extended thinking.

C. An audience, purpose, tone, an approved model document, and a limit to supplied facts.

D. A request for several alternative versions.

  1. A team wants brainstorming ideas for reducing return rates. The assistant returns only four conventional suggestions, each with a paragraph explaining why it is sensible. What is the best corrective prompt shape?

A. Ask for many varied options in one phase with evaluation forbidden, then evaluate the list in a separate step.

B. Ask for "the five best ideas" and request more detail for each.

C. Ask the assistant to reject weak ideas as it generates them.

D. Ask for a single strong idea developed in depth.

  1. A workflow requires gathering market facts, choosing a vendor, listing ways the choice could fail, and writing an internal memo. Which ordering is most defensible?

A. Draft the memo first, then fill in research and analysis to match it.

B. Research, analyze to a decision, brainstorm risks, then draft, with checks between steps.

C. Ideate, draft, research, and analyze, all in one prompt.

D. Analyze before researching, so the decision guides which facts to find.

  1. A colleague will run the same prompt task every month. Which addition makes the instruction most reusable?

A. Adding more adjectives describing quality.

B. Adding a verification step, an out-of-scope statement, and a version marker.

C. Removing constraints so the assistant has more freedom.

D. Combining all four task families into one instruction.

  1. In a combined workflow, an unverified figure from the research step appears as a firm number in the final draft. At which checkpoint should this have been caught?

A. Only during final proofreading for spelling.

B. Before analysis, by confirming sources and that uncertain items were labeled.

C. After the document is sent, during a retrospective.

D. It cannot be caught; figures always change during drafting.

Answers and rationale

  1. B. The response failed at the decision level: it surveyed instead of choosing. Naming the decision, bounding the evidence, setting criteria, and requiring a recommendation with a counterargument targets that failure directly. A adds volume without a verdict, producing a longer non-answer. C changes surface style, not substance. D may improve reasoning generally, but a model change cannot fix a prompt that never asked for a decision.

  2. B. Claim-level sources with dates expose the wrong attribution, and an uncertainty label exposes the stale policy, so this requirement catches both at generation time. A shorter summary (A) might hide the problems by removing content. Bullet points (C) change presentation, not provenance. Avoiding technical terms (D) is irrelevant to whether a citation is real or current.

  3. C. Wrong voice points to a missing audience, purpose, tone, and model document; the invented date points to a missing limit on supplied facts. Together they explain both symptoms. A longer message (A) repeats the errors at greater length. A different model or extended thinking (B) does not supply context the prompt omitted. Alternative versions (D) multiply outputs without fixing the inputs.

  4. A. Separating a judgment-free divergence phase from a later evaluation phase directly addresses the short, pre-judged list. B asks for "best" ideas, reintroducing evaluation during generation. C rejects ideas as they are generated, guaranteeing a cautious list. D asks for depth in one idea, reducing variety rather than expanding it.

  5. B. Research precedes analysis because analysis depends on verified facts; ideation after the decision stress-tests it; drafting comes last so the memo reflects settled content, with checks between steps. A writes a conclusion before evidence exists, inviting fabrication. C collapses dependent steps into one prompt, which is shallow and hard to check. D puts analysis before research, leaving no reliable evidence to analyze.

  6. B. A verification step, an out-of-scope statement, and a version marker make the brief checkable, bounded, and current — the qualities that let another person reuse it. A adds vague language readers interpret differently. C removes useful constraints and increases variability. D mixes families in a way that is hard to run or verify, the opposite of a reusable brief.

  7. B. The checkpoint before analysis confirms that sources are real and uncertain items are labeled; catching the figure there prevents it from becoming evidence for the decision and later a fact in the memo. A checks spelling, not accuracy. C is too late because the document has already been used. D is incorrect: drafting should not introduce new figures, so a traceable figure can be checked at each handoff.

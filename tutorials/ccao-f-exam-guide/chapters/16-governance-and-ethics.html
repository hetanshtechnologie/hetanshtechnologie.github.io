Governance, Ethics, and the Escalation Line

Earlier chapters were about using an AI assistant well: shaping a prompt, choosing a feature, checking an answer, and wiring it into a workflow. This chapter is about the layer above all of that. Producing a fluent result is not the same as being permitted to produce it, or being wise to act on it. Governance is the set of boundaries an organization puts around AI use. Ethics is the reasoning a person applies when rules are silent or incomplete. The escalation line is the point where a decision stops belonging to the person at the keyboard and starts belonging to a specialist owner.

Foundations exams test this material because a governance mistake usually costs more than a clumsy prompt. A weak prompt wastes minutes; a leaked record, fabricated review, or deceptive message can create legal exposure, harm a vulnerable person, and damage hard-won trust. Questions here are rarely about what the model can do but about what a responsible operator should do.

Learning objectives

  • Separate appropriate from inappropriate uses of an AI assistant, including uses that are technically possible but off-limits.
  • Recognize requests that must be refused outright, such as fabricated reviews, deceptive content, and impersonation.
  • Describe regulatory considerations in generic terms: sector rules, retention, disclosure, and cross-border transfer.
  • Explain how organizational AI policies and disclosure requirements shape everyday use.
  • Apply the four ethical themes of fairness, transparency, accountability, and human agency to a concrete case.
  • Identify the signals that mean a concern should be escalated to a legal, security, or compliance owner.
  • Document an incident with a short, factual escalation note that a reviewer can act on.

Key concepts

The "can versus should" test

The most useful mental tool in this domain is a two-question test. The first is can this be done: is the feature available, does the model have the capability, is the data accessible? The second is should this be done: is it allowed, honest, and fair, and would it still look acceptable if explained to the person affected?

These questions are independent, and confusing them is the root of most governance errors. A model may be quite capable of inventing a glowing testimonial for a product nobody has bought, but capability does not make the act acceptable. An assistant may be able to rewrite a message so it sounds like it came from someone else, but that does not make it honest. When a request passes "can" but fails "should," the correct move is to decline, not to comply and then feel uneasy.

The test is portable, working for a large decision and a small task alike. Before sending generated text or pasting a sensitive record into a prompt, a candidate can ask the same two questions. The habit matters more than any single rule, which cannot cover every situation.

Exam note: Scenario questions here often describe a capability in glowing terms, then ask what to do next. If an option says "proceed because the assistant supports it," treat it as a distractor. Capability is never permission.

Appropriate and inappropriate use cases

An appropriate use has the assistant act as a support tool under human control, handling work that is lawful, honest, and within policy. Examples include drafting an internal summary, brainstorming options, rewriting text for clarity, or exploring a dataset the analyst is authorized to see. A person remains responsible and checks the result before use.

An inappropriate use is not simply "something risky," since risk attends many legitimate tasks. It is one that crosses a boundary: data the operator is not entitled to share, a claim known to be false, a human decision removed from human hands, or something expressly forbidden by policy. The boundary may come from law, organizational rules, or basic honesty.

The gray area is where judgment lives. Helping a writer find clearer wording for genuine product features is appropriate; inventing a statistic nobody measured is not. Both look like "marketing copy" from a distance and both are easy for a model to perform. The difference lies in truthfulness and intent, not in the phrasing of the request.

The table below shows how the same surface task can land on different sides of the line depending on the facts around it.

Task surface Appropriate version Inappropriate version Deciding factor
Summarizing a document Condensing an internal report the analyst may access Summarizing a confidential file obtained without permission Authorization to access the source
Writing customer-facing text Clarifying wording for features that exist Claiming a capability the product does not have Truthfulness of the claims
Preparing a decision memo Listing options with sources for a manager to weigh Letting the assistant pick and announce the final decision Human accountability for the choice
Handling personal data Using approved, minimized fields under policy Pasting a full customer record into a prompt to save time Data minimization and policy
Producing a review Drafting a reply to a real, verified review Writing a fake positive review from a nonexistent customer Honesty and fabrication

Requests that must be refused

Three categories recur often enough to recognize immediately: fabricated reviews, deceptive content, and impersonation. In each the model may be technically willing, but a responsible operator declines.

Fabricated reviews are testimonials, ratings, or endorsements that do not reflect a real person's experience: inventing a satisfied customer, rating a product nobody evaluated, or mass-producing varied praise so feedback looks organic. The harm is twofold: it misleads readers and corrupts the information other buyers rely on. Even "just a placeholder until real reviews arrive" should be refused, because placeholders tend to become permanent.

Deceptive content is output designed to make someone believe something false. It covers misleading claims, notices that hide important terms, messages that omit a material fact to steer a decision, and documents made to look more authoritative than they are. The defining feature is intent to mislead, not persuasive language. Persuasion is legitimate; deception is not. A request to make something "sound convincing" while withholding the truth is a signal to stop.

Impersonation means content that pretends to be from a person, role, or organization the operator cannot represent: writing in a real individual's voice, forging an executive's tone to pressure a colleague, or answering as if the assistant were a licensed professional. It is especially serious because it borrows trust belonging to someone else and can make a reader act on authority that does not exist.

A refusal need not be hostile. Decline the unsafe part and offer a legitimate alternative: rather than a fake testimonial, draft a request for honest feedback; rather than impersonating an expert, prepare questions for a qualified expert; rather than hiding a limitation, state it plainly. This "refuse and redirect" approach protects the boundary while staying useful.

Common mistake: Treating a refusal as unhelpful. The correct answer often declines the unsafe request and proposes an honest substitute. Helpfulness is bounded by honesty.

Regulatory considerations in plain terms

A candidate need not memorize statutes; this level of exam does not expect legal expertise. What matters is recognizing the categories of regulation that can apply to AI-assisted work and knowing that they trigger caution and, where appropriate, escalation.

Sector rules apply because of the field the work belongs to. Health, finance, insurance, education, and public services carry extra duties about accuracy, consent, recordkeeping, and who may decide. A generated summary in a regulated field is not just text; it can become part of a decision affecting someone's care, money, or eligibility. Assume higher obligations in regulated contexts and involve the appropriate owner when a boundary is unclear.

Retention concerns how long records are kept and when they are discarded. AI produces a trail of drafts, prompts, and logs, and an organization may have rules about which count as official records. Storing data indefinitely in a tool, or deleting material that should have been preserved, can both create problems. Treat generated artifacts and prompts as potential records and follow retention guidance rather than assuming they are disposable.

Disclosure is the duty to be open about AI involvement where openness matters. Some contexts require telling a person they are interacting with a system rather than a human, or that a document was generated with assistance. This preserves the other person's ability to judge what they receive and protects the organization, since a transparent process is easier to defend than one that seems to conceal how a result was produced.

Cross-border transfer arises when data crosses a jurisdictional boundary, including when a cloud service stores or processes information in another region. Places differ on what may leave, what consent is needed, and who can access it. A candidate cannot map every rule but can notice the trigger: if data may cross a border, pause and check policy rather than proceed by default.

Regulatory category Plain-language trigger Typical first response
Sector rules The work touches health, money, education, or another regulated field Assume higher duties; check with the relevant owner
Retention A prompt, draft, or log might become an official record Follow the retention policy; do not assume it is disposable
Disclosure Someone could be misled about AI involvement or about who wrote something Label or disclose the AI contribution clearly
Cross-border transfer Data may be stored or processed in another jurisdiction Pause and confirm policy before sending data

Organizational policy and disclosure requirements

Law sets an outer boundary, but an organization's AI policy usually sets a tighter one. Internal policy can restrict which tools are approved, which data classifications may enter a prompt, whether customer data may be used, and which uses require sign-off. A practice can be legal in the abstract yet prohibited where the candidate works. When rule sources differ, the stricter applicable rule governs daily behavior.

Disclosure requirements are easy to overlook in a fast workflow. If policy says generated content must carry a label, the label is part of the deliverable, not an optional flourish. If a person must review a generated note before it reaches a client, the draft is an intermediate artifact, not a final message. Treating these as process steps rather than afterthoughts marks someone who understands governance as an operating discipline.

Policies also define escalation paths, naming who owns a given risk and what to do when something looks wrong. A candidate who knows the policy need not guess in the moment. This is why "consult the organization's AI policy" is often the first correct step in a scenario answer: it is the mechanism that connects a general concern to a specific owner and process.

Try it: Before a task, list three things: the data classification, whether output leaves the team, and whether policy needs a label or review. Resolve any uncertainty first.

The four ethical themes

Where rules are silent, ethics supplies the reasoning. Four themes recur in responsible-AI discussions, each mapping to a practical question an operator can ask.

Fairness asks whether comparable people are treated comparably and whether unfair patterns are reinforced. A model can reflect biases in its training data, and an operator can add more through selective prompting. Fairness does not mean identical results for every group; it means differences are justified by relevant facts rather than stereotypes. In practice, check outputs for unequal treatment and avoid prompts that encode assumptions about a person's worth based on an irrelevant trait.

Transparency asks whether the use of AI and the limits of the result are clear to those who matter. A transparent process lets a reviewer see where a figure came from, which assumptions were made, and whether a human checked the work. It also means being honest about uncertainty instead of presenting a guess as a verified fact. Transparency is the antidote to the polished-but-wrong problem: an authoritative-looking result is less dangerous when its origins are visible.

Accountability asks who is answerable for the outcome. The answer must always be a person or an organization, never the tool. An assistant can draft and summarize but cannot carry responsibility. If a generated statement is wrong and reaches a customer, the accountable party is the person who sent it and the organization they represent. Preserving accountability means keeping a human review step where consequences are real and resisting any framing that treats model output as self-validating.

Human agency asks whether people retain meaningful control over decisions affecting them. It relates to accountability but focuses on the person receiving the decision. A system that quietly steers someone toward a choice, or removes their ability to understand and contest an outcome, undermines agency. Good practice keeps a human in the loop for consequential decisions, makes the basis understandable, and leaves room to override or question it.

These themes overlap, and one case can implicate several. They are a checklist for reasoning, not a taxonomy with hard walls. In a tricky scenario, run through them quickly: is it fair, is it transparent, who is accountable, and does it preserve human agency? A failure on any one is a reason to slow down.

Protecting children's and vulnerable users' data

Some groups need a higher standard of care. Children, and adults vulnerable through age, illness, dependency, or circumstance, are less able to recognize manipulation, more likely to be harmed by a privacy lapse, and often subject to extra legal protection. Apply heightened caution whenever a task involves their data or targets them as an audience.

In practice, avoid entering a child's personal information into a prompt without a clear, approved reason and lawful basis. Do not use an assistant to profile a vulnerable person, infer sensitive traits, or craft messages exploiting a known weakness. Avoid content aimed at children that is deceptive, manipulative, or age-inappropriate. Remember that data about a minor may face stricter consent and retention rules than adult data.

The "should" test is especially sharp here. A technique acceptable for a general audience can be unacceptable for a child, who cannot evaluate it on equal terms. If a scenario involves a minor or a vulnerable user, raise the level of scrutiny and lower the threshold for escalation.

The escalation line

Escalation is handing a concern to someone with the authority and expertise to resolve it. The line is crossed when the issue exceeds the operator's knowledge, authority, or role. Three owners appear most often.

  • A legal owner fits questions of lawfulness, contractual exposure, regulatory obligation, or a claim that could create liability. Ambiguity about what is permitted, or a request that appears to cross a legal boundary, belongs here.
  • A security owner fits a possible breach, unauthorized access, leaked credentials, suspicious data movement, or a tool behaving as if compromised. The concern is the confidentiality or integrity of systems and data.
  • A compliance owner fits a broken internal rule or policy, or a missed process such as disclosure, consent, or recordkeeping. The concern is adherence to the organization's own commitments.

Triggers are more useful than a fixed list of scenarios. Escalate when the potential impact exceeds what the operator can contain, when facts are uncertain and stakes are material, when a rule may have been broken, when safety or sensitive data is involved, or when policy names an owner for the situation. Escalating early is cheap; discovering late that a concern should have been raised is not.

Escalation is not abandoning the task. It means pausing the risky part, preserving what is known, and routing the matter to the right owner with a clear account of the facts. A good operator stays helpful within their authority while the specialist decides the larger question.

Exam note: If an option combines "continue as before" with "mention it later if it becomes a problem," it is almost certainly wrong when the scenario involves legal, security, or compliance risk. The correct pattern is to pause, record, and route promptly.

Documenting an incident

An escalation is only as useful as the information that travels with it. A vague "something looks wrong" message forces a fresh investigation. A good note is short, factual, and structured so a reviewer can triage it immediately.

Separate what was observed from what is suspected. Identify the role raising the concern, the system or channel, the kind of data affected (without pasting sensitive values), the likely impact, the rule implicated, actions taken, and the owner it has been routed to. Include when the event occurred and when it was noticed. Avoid speculation presented as fact and minimizing language that hides seriousness.

Scenario, decision, reason

Exam items here reward compact reasoning: state the scenario, choose a decision, give the reason. Practising that structure makes it easier to discard attractive but wrong options. The table applies the pattern to several short cases.

Scenario Decision Reason
An analyst is asked to generate glowing reviews for a product that has not shipped. Refuse and offer to request real feedback. Fabricated reviews mislead buyers and cannot be justified by capability.
A draft customer email describes a feature the product lacks. Correct the claim before sending. Deceptive content is off-limits even when the wording is polished.
A request asks the assistant to write as if it were a licensed adviser. Decline and suggest consulting a qualified professional. Impersonation borrows authority the operator does not have.
A team wants to paste a full customer record into an unapproved tool. Stop and check policy; use approved, minimized data. Unauthorized data entry and over-collection breach policy and privacy duties.
A generated figure appears in a client memo with no source. Verify the figure or remove it before release. Transparency and accountability require the basis of a claim to be visible.
An error may have exposed a dataset to the wrong audience. Pause, record the facts, and route to the security owner. Possible exposure exceeds the operator's authority to resolve alone.

Worked scenario

A coordinator at a midsize firm is asked to prepare a launch package with three parts: customer testimonials for the launch page, a short profile of a well-known industry figure written in that person's voice, and a bilingual notice for customers in two countries. The deadline is tight, and the requester suggests using the assistant to "make it all look polished and authoritative quickly." The coordinator can produce all three technically. The question is which parts should be produced, and what must happen first.

Applying the "can versus should" test, the testimonials are the clearest failure. No real customers were consulted, so any quotation would be fabricated. Even labelled as examples, the launch page would present them as genuine feedback. The decision is to refuse that part and offer alternatives: draft a request for honest feedback from real early users, and prepare a layout with placeholders that cannot be mistaken for real quotes.

The profile raises impersonation. The coordinator is not that person and has no permission to speak as them. The problem is not the quality of the imitation but that readers would attach the figure's credibility to claims never made. The coordinator declines that voice and offers an objective summary attributing published, verifiable statements with sources, preserving the content without borrowing an identity.

The bilingual notice is different: translation is a legitimate use. Two flags appear. First, customers in two countries mean data handling and disclosure rules may differ, a cross-border consideration. Second, any claim about capabilities or data use must be accurate and include required disclosures. The coordinator drafts the translation but marks it pending review.

Consulting policy reveals two requirements: AI-assisted customer-facing material carries an internal review label until approved, and personal data may only be processed through approved tools. The testimonial and impersonation requests conflict with policy as well as ethics, reinforcing the refusals. The notice proceeds through the approved tool with a review label.

One more issue surfaces: a draft customer list was exported to a shared location that is not approved. This possible security and compliance problem exceeds the coordinator's authority. They stop using that copy, preserve the file location and timing without redistributing it, complete an escalation note, and route it to the security owner because the primary concern is possible unauthorized exposure, copying the compliance owner because an approved-tool rule may have been breached.

The outcome has four pieces: two refusals with honest alternatives, one draft proceeding under policy with disclosure and review, and one incident documented and routed correctly. The coordinator was neither obstructive nor reckless: they helped where legitimate and drew a firm line where it was not.

Common mistake: Treating disclosure and review labels as cosmetic. In the scenario, the review label is what keeps a draft from quietly becoming a final customer message. A label that is skipped because of a deadline is a control that has been switched off.

Common mistakes

  • Treating technical capability as permission and proceeding because a feature exists.
  • Generating fabricated testimonials, even as "temporary" placeholders that look real.
  • Writing deceptive content or hiding material facts because persuasive wording is easier than honest wording.
  • Imitating a real person, role, or professional authority without permission.
  • Ignoring organizational policy in favor of a looser general rule, or assuming legality elsewhere implies internal approval.
  • Forgetting that prompts, drafts, and logs may be records subject to retention rules.
  • Overlooking disclosure duties when generated content reaches customers or AI informs a decision.
  • Handling a possible security or compliance incident alone instead of routing it promptly.
  • Writing a vague escalation note that omits timing, data category, impact, and actions taken.
  • Lowering the standard of care for children's or vulnerable users' data because a task seems minor.

Chapter summary

  • The "can versus should" test separates capability from what an operator is permitted and wise to do; when they conflict, decline.
  • Fabricated reviews, deceptive content, and impersonation are firm refusals. Refuse the unsafe part and offer an honest alternative.
  • Regulatory considerations are recognized by category: sector rules, retention, disclosure, and cross-border transfer. Each triggers caution and escalation where needed.
  • Organizational policy is usually stricter than the law and defines approved tools, data limits, disclosure, and escalation paths.
  • The four ethical themes are fairness, transparency, accountability, and human agency. A failure on any one is a reason to slow down.
  • Children's and vulnerable users' data calls for heightened care and a lower escalation threshold.
  • Escalate to legal, security, or compliance based on the risk, and document the event in a short, factual note.
  • Good governance is not obstruction: help legitimately while holding the line where help would cause harm.

Checkpoint questions

  1. An analyst can produce a set of enthusiastic testimonials for a product that no customer has tried. What should the analyst do?

A. Generate the testimonials, since the assistant is capable of writing realistic reviews. B. Generate them but add a small disclaimer at the bottom of the page. C. Refuse and offer to draft a request for genuine feedback from real customers. D. Generate them only for internal use, where no one outside the team will see them.

  1. Which combination is made up entirely of requests that must be refused?

A. Fabricated reviews, deceptive claims, and impersonation of a real person. B. A translation draft, a clarified paragraph, and a brainstorming list. C. A summary of an authorized report, a rewritten email, and a source list. D. A labeled draft, a disclosure statement, and a request for review.

  1. A generated figure appears in a client memo with no indication of where it came from. Which ethical theme is most directly concerned?

A. Human agency, because the client loses the ability to choose. B. Transparency, because the basis of the claim is hidden from the reviewer. C. Fairness, because the figure treats two clients differently. D. Retention, because the memo must be stored for a fixed period.

  1. A draft customer list may have been shared through an unapproved location. Which owner is the most appropriate first escalation?

A. A legal owner, because the main issue is contractual wording. B. A compliance owner, because the main issue is a missed review label. C. A security owner, because the main issue is possible unauthorized exposure of data. D. No one yet, because the analyst should investigate fully before raising it.

  1. Which item belongs in a well-written escalation note?

A. The full text of every sensitive record involved. B. A confident guess about who is to blame. C. The category of data involved, the timing, the likely impact, and actions already taken. D. A promise that the issue will be resolved without outside help.

  1. A task involves a minor's personal information and a message designed to be persuasive. What is the best response?

A. Proceed normally, since persuasion is a legitimate goal. B. Apply heightened care, check the lawful basis and policy, and escalate if the boundary is unclear. C. Refuse all work involving any personal data, without exception. D. Anonymize the data only after sending the message.

  1. Which statement about organizational AI policy is correct?

A. Policy is only a summary of the law and never adds restrictions. B. Policy matters less than what the assistant technically allows. C. Policy often sets stricter rules than the law and defines approved tools, disclosure, and escalation. D. Policy applies only after an incident has already occurred.

Answers and rationale

  1. C. The testimonials are fabricated because no customer tried the product, so they must be refused; offering to gather genuine feedback redirects the task honestly. A is wrong because capability is not permission. B is wrong because a disclaimer does not cure a review readers take as genuine. D is wrong because internal use does not remove the problem: fabricated praise can still spread into decisions and outward later.

  2. A. All three items in A are firm refusals: invented reviews, misleading claims, and speaking as someone the operator is not. B is wrong because translation, clarification, and brainstorming are legitimate uses. C is wrong because summarizing authorized material, rewriting, and listing sources are appropriate. D is wrong because labeled drafts, disclosure statements, and review requests are good practice, not violations.

  3. B. Transparency is directly concerned because the reviewer cannot see where the figure came from or whether it was checked. A is wrong because human agency concerns control over decisions, not the central issue here. C is wrong because nothing indicates unequal treatment. D is wrong because retention is a recordkeeping category, not an ethical theme about the visibility of a claim.

  4. C. Possible unauthorized exposure is a security concern, so the security owner is the correct first route; the compliance owner may be copied if an approved-tool rule was breached. A is wrong because contractual wording is not the issue. B is wrong because a missed review label is not what happened; the problem is exposure. D is wrong because waiting to investigate fully delays a time-sensitive response and exceeds the analyst's authority.

  5. C. A useful note records the data category, timing, likely impact, and actions taken so a reviewer can triage quickly. A is wrong because raw sensitive values should not be pasted into a note. B is wrong because speculation about blame is not a fact and can misdirect the investigation. D is wrong because promising to keep the matter internal may conflict with policy and understate the need for specialist ownership.

  6. B. A minor's data and a persuasive message call for heightened care, a check of lawful basis and policy, and escalation when the boundary is unclear. A is wrong because it ignores the lower threshold for a child. C is wrong because it is overly broad; legitimate work with personal data is possible under the right conditions. D is wrong because anonymizing after sending does not protect data already exposed.

  7. C. Policy commonly imposes stricter rules than the law and sets out approved tools, disclosure duties, and escalation paths. A is wrong because policy routinely adds restrictions beyond legal minimums. B is wrong because technical capability never overrides policy. D is wrong because policy is a framework that applies continuously, not only after something goes wrong.

Two Full Practice Papers

This chapter is a rehearsal space. The two papers below are original practice papers written from first principles for this guide, not copies of any exam or predictions of exact questions. They help the candidate practise three things at once: seeing what a question asks, choosing among plausible alternatives, and explaining why one answer beats the others.

Answering under conditions builds retrieval; reading alone builds familiarity. Commit to an answer before the key, even when unsure, so a wrong answer becomes a learning event.

Learning objectives

  • Attempt two forty-question papers spanning scenario, judgment, and recognition items across ten domains.
  • Apply a repeatable method for finding the deciding constraint and eliminating distractors.
  • Map each question to its domain so weak areas become visible.
  • Score responses and treat the result as a guide to further study.
  • Explain each correct answer by naming the principle behind it.
  • Transfer these triage habits to the timed exam, including flagging and returning.

Key concepts

What these papers are for

A foundations-level assessment is not a test of trivia. It asks whether a person can use a tool effectively, safely, and communicate clearly. The items below favour short situations over definitions. Recognition items require understanding rather than recall.

Paper A favours direct judgments on familiar tasks. Paper B asks for more synthesis, such as reconciling conflicting outputs or adapting one verified answer for two audiences. Neither is harder; they exercise the same knowledge from different angles.

Exam note: A scenario question contains one detail that decides the answer. Find it first, then judge each option against it. An option that ignores it is a distractor, however sensible it sounds.

A repeatable method

  1. Read for the goal, then the constraint that limits it.
  2. Name the domain: prompting, verification, model choice, governance.
  3. Eliminate options violating safety, privacy, accuracy, or verification principles.
  4. Choose the survivor that achieves the goal with the least unnecessary risk.
  5. State in one sentence why it beats the strongest rival.

The last step costs seconds and catches errors, because a choice that cannot be justified is a guess in convincing clothes.

Common mistake: Choosing the option that sounds most professional. Polished language is not evidence of correctness; a fluent answer can still be wrong.

Scoring guide

Score each paper separately: one point per correct option, no partial credit. Record totals and per-domain counts, because a total can hide a pattern. Missing most verification items is a specific problem, not a general one.

Correct out of 40 Interpretation Next step
34–40 Strong command Attempt the other paper; focus on rationale quality
27–33 Solid, with gaps Revisit the domains where errors clustered
20–26 Mixed understanding Review the relevant chapters, then retry missed domains
Below 20 Foundations still forming Re-read the content chapters before a full paper

Try it: Mark a confidence dot beside each answer. Comparing confidence with correctness reveals confident wrong answers, which deserve more attention than lucky guesses.

Worked scenario

A candidate working through Paper A without the key reaches the item about a summary with a precise statistic and no visible source. The options are to forward it, verify the figure, assume the assistant never errs, or drop all numbers from future outputs.

The goal is to use the summary responsibly; the deciding constraint is the unsupported number. The domain is accuracy. Two options fall at once: forwarding ignores the constraint, and assuming infallibility contradicts the fact that generated content can be fabricated. Banning all numbers is an overcorrection that trades a specific problem for permanent loss of detail. Verification closes the gap without discarding the work.

The candidate writes: "An unsupported number needs a source check, not blind trust or a blanket ban." That sentence is the real product. Surface details change, but the structure holds: locate the constraint, reject violations and overcorrections, choose the proportionate response.

Under time pressure, mark the item, pick the best option, and move on. Confidence dots reveal flagged guesses that later prove correct: luck that will not travel to a different question set.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a score as a prediction rather than a map of study needs.
  • Answering from impressions instead of locating the deciding constraint.
  • Choosing the longest or most formal-sounding option.
  • Ignoring per-domain counts and studying only what felt difficult.
  • Reading the rationale before committing, which turns recognition into hindsight.
  • Overcorrecting after an error by rejecting every similar-looking option.
  • Skipping the one-sentence justification that catches accidental errors.

Chapter summary

  • The papers are original, balanced across ten domains, and built for retrieval.
  • The five-step method suits scenario, judgment, and recognition items.
  • The domain map turns a raw score into a targeted revision plan.
  • Paper A is the baseline; Paper B adds synthesis after the first review.
  • Overcorrection is a distractor pattern; the right response is proportionate.
  • Each key entry names the principle, so an error ends with clarity, not a letter.

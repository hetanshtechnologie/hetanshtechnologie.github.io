Shaping Output: Audience, Refinement, and Format

A correct answer and a useful answer are not the same thing. An assistant can return a technically accurate paragraph that a busy decision-maker cannot scan, a table that a reader must act on but cannot use as a checklist, or a polished explanation whose confidence hides a factual error. Shaping output is the discipline of closing that gap. It means deciding who will read the answer, what they will do with it, and what form lets them do that with the least effort.

This chapter treats output shaping as a set of controllable choices rather than an afterthought. You will learn how to rewrite for a non-technical reader without stripping away precision, how to retarget the same finding for different audiences, how to refine by comparing candidates against a rubric, and how to specify format, schema, and length in a request. Throughout, one rule stays constant: presentation serves meaning, so a beautiful answer that is wrong is still a failure.

Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Explain why audience, purpose, and the next step determine the best shape of an answer.
  • Rewrite technical content for a non-technical reader without losing precision, units, or qualifiers.
  • Adapt the same finding for executives, peers, and customers.
  • Distinguish a change in register from a change in meaning.
  • Refine answers by comparing two candidates against an explicit rubric.
  • Choose a format that matches what happens next: scanning, action, narrative, or import.
  • Specify schemas, field names, missing-value behavior, and length in a request.
  • Recognize the trap of polishing an answer that is still factually wrong.

Key concepts

The answer is a product of three decisions

Before you write a request, decide three things. First, who reads it: a specialist who shares your vocabulary, a generalist who does not, or a machine that will parse it. Second, why they read it: to decide, to understand, to act in order, or to feed another system. Third, what happens next: a meeting, a spreadsheet, a customer email, or an automated pipeline. These three decisions determine format, register, and length far more reliably than personal preference does.

Consider a single finding: a shipment metric rose from 4 percent to 7 percent over a quarter. The underlying fact is unchanged whether it appears in a dashboard note, a technical post-mortem, or a customer email. What changes is the emphasis. An operations lead wants the decision implied by the change. An engineer wants the measurement method and the confidence interval. A customer wants reassurance and a next step. Presenting the same 4-to-7 percent figure in the same tone to all three wastes attention and invites misreading.

The exam tends to test this as a matching problem. Given a scenario, choose the response shape that fits the reader and the downstream task. There is rarely one universally correct format; there is a format correct for a purpose.

Exam note: When a question asks you to "improve" a response, look first at audience and next step. Style tweaks are usually secondary to a mismatch between the answer's shape and what the reader needs to do.

Rewriting for a non-technical reader without losing precision

Plain language is not simpler thinking. The goal is to keep every claim, number, unit, condition, and uncertainty while replacing specialized vocabulary with words a general reader knows. Precision usually lives in the small words, so those are the words most at risk.

A non-technical rewrite fails in three common ways. It drops the qualifier ("in the sampled regions"), rounds away a meaningful boundary ("reduced by 10 percent" becomes "fixed"), or swaps a precise term for a vaguer one that sounds friendlier but changes the claim. It can also introduce an analogy that clarifies one property while silently dropping a constraint. Analogies are useful, but they should be checked against the original claim for the parts they leave out.

The safe method is to treat the original as a set of divisible facts, then translate each one. Keep numbers and units verbatim unless you have a reason to convert them, and if you convert, show both. State a definition once where a term is unavoidable rather than deleting the term entirely. Preserve the direction and strength of every relationship: "associated with" must not become "caused by," and "may" must not become "will."

Technical phrasing Plain-language rewrite What must survive
"The 95% confidence interval for the latency delta is 12–19 ms." "We are fairly confident the typical delay grew by 12 to 19 milliseconds; the true figure is very likely in that range." The interval, the unit, and the uncertainty
"Error rate increased 3 percentage points, from 4% to 7%." "Errors rose from 4 in 100 requests to 7 in 100, an increase of 3 points." Absolute versus relative change and the baseline
"The result is significant at p < 0.01 but not operationally material." "The pattern is unlikely to be chance, though the size is too small to matter in practice." Both statistical and practical judgments

Notice that each rewrite is longer, not shorter. Plain language often costs words because it replaces a dense term with several ordinary ones. Brevity is a separate constraint, negotiated explicitly rather than achieved by deleting meaning.

Common mistake: Replacing "correlates with" by "causes" to make a sentence read more smoothly. That is a factual change, not a simplification, and it is the kind of error a reviewer is likely to flag.

Adapting one finding for executives, peers, and customers

The same fact can be framed for three audiences without inventing new content. What changes is which facet is foregrounded and which format makes that facet easy to grasp.

Executives generally want the decision and its consequences first: what happened, what it means, what should be done, and how much risk sits in the recommendation. Detail is welcome only if it changes the decision. A short bottom-line paragraph plus a compact options table usually beats a long narrative. Peers want reproducibility: method, assumptions, data sources, limitations, and enough detail to challenge the result. They tolerate density because they share the vocabulary. Customers want reassurance, plain language, and a clear next step, with technical caveats translated into implications they can feel.

Audience Emphasis Recommended format
Executive or decision-maker Bottom line, recommendation, risk, cost of delay Short prose summary (3–5 sentences), then a small options table
Peer or specialist Method, assumptions, evidence, limitations, reproducibility Detailed prose or bullets with a parameters table and explicit caveats
Customer or end user Benefit, reassurance, plain language, next action Brief prose, then a short numbered list of steps or an FAQ-style set of pairs
Operator or support staff Triggers, thresholds, and concrete actions in order Numbered checklist with exact values and escalation paths
Machine consumer Stable field names, types, and predictable ordering Structured data (JSON or CSV) with an explicit schema

These are defaults, not laws. A customer who is also a specialist may want the peer treatment. An executive who requested a deep dive wants more than a summary. The point is to make the choice deliberately and to say it in the request: "Write for a reader who has no background in this area and needs to decide whether to approve a budget."

When you ask an assistant to retarget content, name the audience and the emphasis explicitly. "Make this more executive" is vague; "lead with the recommendation, keep the rationale to two sentences, and list the top two risks" is actionable. Vague audience instructions invite the assistant to guess, and its guess may optimize for the wrong thing.

Preserving meaning while changing register

Register is the level of formality and technical density in a piece of writing. The same proposition can be expressed in a formal register, a neutral professional one, or a conversational one. A change of register is a change of packaging. A change of meaning is a change of the thing inside the package.

The distinction matters because assistants, when asked to "make this sound friendlier," sometimes soften a claim in ways that alter it. A cautious finding can drift into an assurance. An approximate number can become exact. A conditional recommendation can become an unconditional one. These shifts are easy to miss because the resulting sentence reads naturally.

A reliable test is the restatement test: after changing register, ask whether a reader of the new version could accurately reconstruct the original claim, including its conditions and its confidence. If the new version lets them believe something the old one did not assert, the meaning changed. Another good practice is to ask for two versions side by side and compare them line by line. Differences in word choice are expected; differences in who, what, when, how much, or how certain are not.

Register shift Acceptable change Unacceptable change
Formal to conversational "We recommend" becomes "We suggest" "We recommend, pending review" becomes "We recommend"
Dense to plain "Latency delta" becomes "the increase in delay" "Increased by 12 ms" becomes "increased substantially"
Cautious to direct "Evidence suggests" becomes "The data points to" "May indicate" becomes "Proves"

Try it: Ask for a formal version and a conversational version of the same three-sentence finding. Compare the numbers, the qualifiers, and the strength of the conclusion. Any difference in those three categories is a meaning change, not a style change.

Refinement by comparing candidates against a rubric

Refinement is easier when it is a comparison rather than a vague request to "make it better." Generate two candidate answers, then score each against a rubric whose criteria you state in advance. This turns a subjective judgment into a repeatable one and makes it clear which candidate to keep and which parts of the other to borrow.

A useful rubric has a small number of explicit criteria. Correctness is a gate: if a candidate contains a factual error, it does not pass regardless of how well it is written. After the gate, score completeness, audience fit, format fit, length discipline, and tone. Give each criterion a short definition so the scoring is not left to intuition. An assistant asked to apply a rubric will do a better job when the criteria are concrete ("states the baseline and the absolute change") rather than abstract ("is clear").

Criterion Gate or score What a pass looks like
Factual correctness Gate Numbers, units, and claims match the source; no invented details
Completeness Score 1–5 All required points appear; no unrequested tangents
Audience fit Score 1–5 Vocabulary and emphasis match the named reader
Format fit Score 1–5 Structure matches the stated next step
Length discipline Score 1–5 Within the stated bound, with the important content retained
Tone and register Score 1–5 Formality level matches the request without changing meaning

Once both candidates are scored, the strong choice is usually obvious. Often the best final answer is a merge: the first candidate's structure with the second candidate's wording of a difficult point. Ask for that merge explicitly and name what to take from each. This is more reliable than asking for a third answer from scratch, because the rubric has already identified what matters.

An important caveat: an assistant scoring its own output against a rubric is not an independent verifier. Use the rubric to organize judgment, not to replace checking the facts against the source. The gate criterion exists precisely because fluency can mask an error.

Choosing a format that matches the next step

Format is not decoration. It is an interface between the answer and whatever happens after it. Choose it by asking what the reader or system will do with the content.

If someone must scan for one value among many, use a table with consistent columns. If someone must perform actions in a sequence, use a numbered list; bullets are for unordered items. If someone must follow an argument or a narrative, use prose with clear paragraph breaks. If a program must ingest the content, use structured data with exact field names and no surrounding commentary. A common failure is handing a machine a beautiful table and handing a human a wall of JSON.

Next step Best format Why
Scan several items or compare options Table Rows align equivalent attributes for quick comparison
Execute actions in order Numbered list Sequence is explicit and progress is easy to track
Capture unordered items or reminders Bulleted list No implied ordering; easy to add or remove items
Understand a chain of reasoning or a story Short prose paragraphs Transitions carry the logic between ideas
Import into a spreadsheet or database CSV or structured rows Predictable delimiters and one record per row
Feed an API or downstream program JSON with a fixed schema Types and field names are machine-readable
Make a decision among alternatives Recommendation plus options table The answer and its trade-offs are both visible

Length and format interact. A table that must be read on a narrow screen may be worse than short bullets. A numbered list with ten steps may need grouping into three phases. Nothing in a format choice is permanent: ask for an alternative rendering when the first one proves awkward. The exam usually rewards the principle rather than a single canonical layout, so justify the format by naming the next step.

Specifying schemas and field names

When output will be consumed by another system, a request must function like a contract. A schema is that contract. It names the exact fields, their types, which are required, what values are allowed, and how missing information is represented. Without it, an assistant will invent reasonable field names, reorder them between runs, and fill gaps with prose, all of which break automated processing.

Good field names are stable, descriptive, and unambiguous. Use one naming convention consistently and state it: lower-case words separated by underscores is a common, readable default. Avoid synonyms for the same concept, and avoid a single field that carries two different kinds of value. If a field can be absent, say exactly how to mark that: a null value, an empty string, or a literal such as "unknown" are different choices with different downstream effects. State whether extra fields are permitted; usually they are not.

A minimal schema request should cover these points:

  • Field names in order, spelled exactly as required.
  • Type for each field: string, number, boolean, array, or object.
  • Required versus optional fields.
  • Allowed values for any constrained field.
  • Representation of missing or uncertain values.
  • Whether any explanatory text outside the structured output is allowed.

Exam note: If a scenario mentions that output will be imported, parsed, or compared across runs, think "schema first." The correct answer is the one that fixes names and types, not the one that merely asks for "a clean table."

Controlling length explicitly

Length is a specifiable property, not something to hope for. State a number and a unit: "in no more than 150 words," "in exactly three bullets," "in one paragraph of 80 to 100 words." Distinguish a hard ceiling from a target. A ceiling says the answer must not exceed the bound; a target says approximately how long it should be. Confusing the two leads to either truncated content or needless padding.

When a bound is tight, decide in advance what should be cut if the content does not fit. Put the decision, the key number, and the main caveat first, then let supporting detail fall away. Ask the assistant to preserve numbers and qualifiers while trimming background. This protects meaning under pressure. If you need both brevity and depth, request two lengths rather than one compromise: a one-sentence version and a one-paragraph version of the same finding.

Be careful with counts applied to structured content. "Five bullets" is unambiguous; "a short list" is not. "A table with at most four rows" is unambiguous; "a concise table" is not. The same precision that you demand of field names should apply to length instructions.

Asking for versions with and without jargon

Requesting a jargon-free version alongside a technical one serves two purposes. It gives you an immediately usable plain-language answer, and it creates a cross-check: the two versions should say the same thing. If they disagree, you have found either an error in the rewrite or an ambiguity in the original that needs resolving.

To make the comparison useful, ask the assistant to keep the technical version available and to list any term it replaced. A short mapping line such as "latency delta → increase in delay" lets a reviewer confirm that no meaning was lost. For audiences that need both, you can ask for a three-part answer: a plain-language summary, a technical detail section, and a glossary of the terms that moved between them.

This technique also exposes false precision. If a technical phrase carried a condition that disappears in the plain version, the comparison makes the gap visible. Treat that gap as a defect to repair, not an acceptable simplification.

The trap of polishing an answer that is still factually wrong

The most dangerous output is not an obviously bad answer. It is a fluent, well-organized, confidently worded answer whose facts are wrong. Polish increases credibility, so a flawed answer that reads well is more likely to be trusted and acted on. This is why refinement must be gated by verification.

Before improving tone, structure, or length, check the substance. Do the numbers match the source material? Are units correct? Did the assistant compute a percentage from the wrong baseline? Are there citations or references that may have been invented? Did it fill an unfamiliar gap with a plausible-sounding fabrication? Where a claim exceeds what the provided material supports, it should be flagged or removed rather than smoothed over.

A practical sequence is: verify, then shape, then verify again. The first pass catches errors before they are disguised. The second pass catches errors introduced by rewriting, which happens when an assistant compresses a qualified claim into a tidier but stronger one. If a response is built on a fabricated premise, no amount of formatting makes it usable; the correct move is to return to source material and request a new answer with the missing facts supplied.

Common mistake: Treating a rubric score of "excellent style" as evidence that the content is correct. Style and truth are independent. Score them separately and let correctness decide whether refinement is worth doing at all.

Writing the output-format request

An effective request combines the decisions from this chapter into a single instruction. It names the audience, the register, the format, the schema, the length, the jargon handling, and the verification expectation. The following template shows how these pieces fit together; the placeholders are meant to be replaced with the specifics of a task.

Audience: a non-technical operations manager who must approve or reject a change.
Purpose: decide whether to fund a follow-up investigation.

Produce two parts.

Part 1 — Summary:
- One paragraph of 90-110 words in plain language.
- Lead with the recommendation, then the single strongest reason, then the main caveat.
- Keep every number and unit exactly as given in the source below.
- Do not use the terms "confidence interval," "variance," or "regression."

Part 2 — Structured record:
Return a JSON object only, with no text before or after it.
Fields, in this exact order:
  finding (string)
  severity (string; one of low, medium, high)
  evidence (array of strings)
  recommendation (string)
  confidence (string; one of low, medium, high)
Required fields: all five.
For any value not supported by the source, use null. Do not guess or invent.
Do not add fields.

Then list, separately, any term you simplified and what it meant.

Before finalizing, check each number against the source. If you cannot verify
a claim, mark it as unverified rather than stating it as fact.

This request works because it removes guesswork. The audience is named, the purpose is named, the length is bounded with a range, the jargon constraint is explicit, the schema fixes names, order, types, allowed values, and missing-value behavior, and a verification instruction closes the loop. Note that the plain-language constraints and the schema constraints do not conflict: they apply to different parts of the answer.

Try it: Take a paragraph you have written and issue it as a request with a named audience, a maximum length, and a required format. Compare the result with your original. The differences usually reveal which choices you had left implicit.

Worked scenario

An analyst has produced a short internal note about a quality issue. The raw fact is this: in one quarter, the share of shipments arriving with a packaging defect rose from 4 percent to 7 percent across the three sampled regions; the sample was not randomized, and the analyst considers the increase worth investigating but not yet proven to reflect all shipments. A manager must brief three groups and also load the finding into a tracking system.

The analyst's first draft is a single dense paragraph aimed at nobody in particular. It opens with the sampling method, includes a statistical term, and ends with the number. A reviewer returns it with a simple question: what is each reader supposed to do after reading this? That question drives the rewrite.

Step 1: Identify the next step for each audience. The executive sponsor must decide whether to fund an investigation. The quality peers must judge whether the evidence is sound. Affected customers must know whether their recent shipment is a concern and what to do. The tracking system must store the finding in comparable fields.

Step 2: Choose a format and emphasis for each. The executive gets a three-sentence bottom line with a recommendation and the main caveat. Peers get a short technical note with method, the absolute change, and the sampling limitation. Customers get plain language that states the observed rate, what is being done, and a contact step, with no statistical terminology. The system gets a structured record.

Step 3: Preserve the same core facts everywhere. Across all versions, the baseline remains 4 percent, the new figure remains 7 percent, the scope remains the three sampled regions, and the limitation remains that the sample was not randomized. No version says the defect rate "tripled" or that all shipments are affected. The word "associated" is not upgraded to "caused."

Step 4: Refine with a rubric. The analyst generates two versions of the executive summary and scores them. Candidate A is fluent but opens with the sampling method and buries the recommendation. Candidate B leads with the recommendation, states the 3-point rise, and keeps the caveat in one clause. On correctness both pass; on audience fit B wins. The final summary takes B's order and A's clearer phrasing of the uncertainty.

Audience Emphasis in the final version Format used
Executive sponsor Fund a limited investigation; one caveat Three-sentence prose summary
Quality peers Method, absolute change, sampling limit Bullets plus a short evidence line
Affected customers What happened and how to raise a concern Four short sentences and one contact step
Tracking system Comparable fields and explicit null handling Structured record with fixed field order

The resolution is not four different findings. It is one finding rendered four ways, each shaped for a reader or system, with the core facts held constant. The reviewer's final check is the restatement test applied across all four: does each version let its reader reconstruct the same underlying claim? When all four pass, the output set is internally consistent and fit for purpose.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a format before identifying what the reader will do next.
  • Simplifying a claim by deleting its qualifiers, units, or baseline.
  • Treating a change in register as permission to strengthen a conclusion.
  • Asking for "a better answer" without stating the rubric or criteria.
  • Scoring style highly while skipping the factual correctness gate.
  • Giving a machine-readable format to a human reader, or prose to an importer.
  • Requesting a schema without specifying missing-value behavior or allowed values.
  • Stating a length as "brief" rather than as a number with a unit.
  • Polishing a response before checking whether its numbers came from the source.
  • Assuming the assistant's own rubric score counts as independent verification.

Chapter summary

  • Output shape follows audience, purpose, and the next step; there is no universally best format.
  • Plain-language rewrites should keep numbers, units, conditions, and uncertainty, even at the cost of extra words.
  • Executives want decisions and risk, peers want method and limitations, and customers want reassurance and an action.
  • Register changes packaging; meaning changes the claim. The restatement test tells them apart.
  • Refinement works best as a comparison of two candidates against a rubric with correctness as a gate.
  • Format follows the downstream task: tables for scanning, lists for action, prose for narrative, structured data for import.
  • A schema fixes field names, order, types, allowed values, and missing-value behavior.
  • Length should be specified as a number with a unit, with a decision about what to cut under pressure.
  • Requesting versions with and without jargon exposes lost meaning and false precision.
  • Verify before polishing, and verify again after rewriting; fluency is not evidence of truth.

Checkpoint questions

  1. An analyst must send one finding to a sponsor who needs a go or no-go decision in under a minute. Which opening is most appropriate? - A. A description of the sampling method, followed by the raw figures and a statistical test. - B. A direct recommendation, the one reason that drives it, and the main caveat. - C. A full history of how the data was collected, ending with the recommendation. - D. A structured JSON record with field names and types.

  2. Which rewrite preserves meaning while using plain language? - A. "Errors rose from 4 in 100 to 7 in 100 in the three sampled regions" from "Error rate increased from 4% to 7% across the three sampled regions." - B. "Errors tripled" from "Error rate increased from 4% to 7%." - C. "Errors rose substantially nationwide" from "Error rate increased across the three sampled regions." - D. "The change was caused by packaging" from "The change was associated with packaging."

  3. A technical sentence reads: "The result may indicate a problem, pending review." Which revision changes meaning rather than register? - A. "This could point to a problem, subject to review." - B. "The result suggests a possible issue, awaiting confirmation." - C. "The result proves there is a problem." - D. "There may be an issue, which we have not yet confirmed."

  4. When refining two candidate answers with a rubric, what should be treated as a gate before style is scored? - A. The length of each candidate. - B. Factual correctness against the source. - C. The friendliness of the tone. - D. The number of bullets used.

  5. A downstream program will parse the answer and compare it across many runs. What is the most important requirement? - A. A vivid narrative that explains the background. - B. A table with shaded headers for readability. - C. Fixed field names, types, order, and explicit missing-value handling. - D. A conversational register with short sentences.

  6. A request says the output must be "short." What is the main problem with that instruction? - A. It is too polite for a formal request. - B. It gives no number or unit, so the bound is ambiguous. - C. It prevents the use of a table. - D. It requires the assistant to invent field names.

  7. A response reads beautifully but cites a figure that does not appear in the source material. What is the correct next action? - A. Improve the phrasing further so the figure reads more convincingly. - B. Accept it, because fluency indicates the assistant verified the fact. - C. Replace it with a plausible estimate and keep the structure. - D. Reject the response, return to the source, and request a corrected answer with the figure supplied.

Answers and rationale

  1. Correct: B. A sponsor making a fast decision needs the recommendation first, one supporting reason, and the caveat so the risk is visible. A is wrong because method and statistics delay the decision point. C is wrong because a full history buries the answer under background. D is wrong because structured data suits a machine consumer, not a time-pressured human reader.

  2. Correct: A. It keeps the baseline, the new value, the unit, and the scope while replacing a percentage phrase with a count out of 100. B is wrong because 4 to 7 percent is not a tripling. C is wrong because it replaces "three sampled regions" with "nationwide," changing scope. D is wrong because "associated with" does not justify "caused by."

  3. Correct: C. "Proves" upgrades a cautious, conditional claim into a certainty, so meaning changes. A, B, and D preserve the possibility and the pending confirmation while changing only word choice and formality; they are register changes.

  4. Correct: B. Correctness is a gate because a factually wrong answer fails regardless of its style. A, C, and D are scoring dimensions that matter after the gate, but none of them can compensate for an error in the underlying claims.

  5. Correct: C. Automated comparison across runs depends on a stable contract: exact names, types, order, and defined handling of missing values. A and D are prose qualities that do not help a parser. B improves human readability but does not make the structure predictable.

  6. Correct: B. "Short" has no measurable bound, so different readers and systems will interpret it differently. A is irrelevant to the instruction's clarity. C is false because a short request can still require a table. D is false because field names depend on a schema request, not on a length request.

  7. Correct: D. An unsupported figure is a factual failure, and polish cannot repair it; the answer must be rebuilt from verified source material. A is wrong because better wording hides the error. B is wrong because fluency is not verification. C is wrong because inventing an estimate repeats the original failure.

Research, Planning, and Process Improvement

Learning objectives

  • Use an AI assistant as a research partner while keeping source discipline and verification habits intact.
  • Turn research findings into an actionable plan with milestones, owners, and checkpoints.
  • Map a process before automating any part of it, so improvement effort lands on the right step.
  • Identify bottlenecks, handoff delays, and rework loops from observable signals rather than guesswork.
  • Decide whether a task is worth augmenting, worth removing, or best left unchanged.
  • Design a small pilot with a baseline and a measurement plan before recommending a wider rollout.
  • Draft process documentation and checklists with the assistant, then verify them against how work actually happens.
  • Measure cycle time and rework, and recognize when a spreadsheet or a rule-based tool is the better answer.

Key concepts

The assistant as a research partner, not an oracle

Research is the activity of reducing uncertainty. When a team faces a question it cannot answer from memory, it gathers evidence, compares sources, and arrives at a conclusion it is willing to act on. An AI assistant can accelerate almost every part of that loop, but it does not replace the judgment that decides what the evidence means.

Think of the assistant as a fast, tireless research aide with two weaknesses: it can sound certain when it is wrong, and it does not know what your organization actually does unless you tell it. A good research partnership therefore has a division of labor. The assistant helps frame questions, generate search terms, summarize long material, compare options, and expose assumptions. The human researcher supplies the goal, chooses which sources count as authoritative, checks claims against those sources, and owns the final conclusion.

This distinction matters on the exam because many scenario questions are really tests of whether you keep the human in the decision seat. An answer that says "ask the assistant and use its answer" is almost always weaker than one that says "ask the assistant to gather and structure candidate evidence, then verify the load-bearing claims against a trusted source before deciding."

A productive research exchange usually moves through a few distinct moves:

  1. Define the question narrowly enough that an answer would change a decision.
  2. Ask the assistant for a map of the topic — the main dimensions, the competing viewpoints, and the terms used in the field.
  3. Request specific, checkable claims rather than a smooth general narrative.
  4. Verify the claims that carry the most weight.
  5. Ask the assistant to reconcile conflicting evidence and to state what remains uncertain.

Exam note: "Research partner" implies collaboration with verification, not delegation of judgment. If a choice removes the review step, treat it as a red flag.

Source discipline and verification

Source discipline is the habit of tracking where a claim came from and how much confidence it deserves. It becomes critical the moment research output will be reused in a plan, a proposal, or a decision that affects other people.

When working with an assistant, source discipline has three practical layers. First, separate what the assistant produced from what it retrieved or cited. A confident summary is not itself evidence; it is a description of evidence that may or may not be accurate. Second, prioritize primary sources — the original policy, the actual dataset, the vendor documentation, the internal procedure — over a summary of a summary. Third, record provenance as you go, so that a later reader can trace a number or assertion back to its origin.

Not all sources serve the same purpose. The table below shows a useful way to classify them.

Source type Best used for Main risk Typical verification step
Original internal records Facts about how this organization operates May be outdated or incomplete Confirm date and owner of the record
Official documentation Defined behavior, limits, and requirements May describe the ideal rather than the deployed version Check version and effective date
Raw data extracts Measuring current performance May contain errors or inconsistent definitions Validate fields and sampling rules
Expert conversation Context, exceptions, and unwritten rules Hard to reproduce; may be anecdotal Confirm against an artifact or a second voice
Assistant-generated summary Orientation and framing May omit caveats or blend sources Trace key claims back to the cited material
Public articles and commentary Broad awareness of a topic Variable quality and possible bias Check the underlying evidence, not just the opinion

A helpful prompt pattern is to ask the assistant to label each claim with the source it relies on and to mark anything it cannot support. That does not make the output authoritative, but it makes verification cheaper because the weak links are visible.

Common mistake: Treating a polished, well-organized answer as a reliable one. Fluency and accuracy are independent. Organization helps you read; verification is what makes the result trustworthy.

Turning research into a plan

Research that ends in a document has limited value. Research earns its keep when it changes what happens next. The bridge between the two is a plan: a concrete statement of what will be done, by whom, by when, and how the team will know it worked.

A good plan has a small number of well-defined milestones. A milestone is not a vague aspiration like "improve onboarding." It is an observable state such as "the current onboarding steps are mapped and agreed" or "a pilot has run with five participants and results are recorded." Each milestone should have an owner — a role accountable for moving it forward — and a checkpoint where progress is reviewed and the plan can be adjusted.

The assistant is useful here as a structuring tool. Give it the research findings and ask it to propose milestones, dependencies, owners by role, and risks. Then apply human judgment: does each milestone actually move the goal, are dependencies realistic, and is anyone overloaded?

A compact plan record might include the following fields:

Plan element What it answers Example phrasing
Objective What changes and why Reduce rework in intake review
Milestone What observable state is reached Current intake steps mapped and signed off
Owner Which role is accountable The operations coordinator
Dependency What must happen first Access to the intake dataset
Checkpoint When progress is reviewed End of week two
Success signal How completion is recognized Zero unresolved steps in the map

Notice that owners are roles, not named individuals. This keeps the plan durable when people change assignments and keeps the emphasis on accountability rather than personality.

Try it: Take a research finding you already have and write three milestones for it. For each, state the owner as a role and the success signal as something a reviewer could observe without asking for an explanation.

Process mapping before automation

A recurring error in improvement work is automating a process nobody has mapped. When that happens, the team encodes the current confusion at higher speed. Mapping first creates a shared picture of the work, exposes steps that should not exist, and gives the assistant something precise to reason about.

A process map can be simple. For each step, capture the trigger, the action, the person or system involved, the input, the output, and the waiting time between steps. The goal is not a beautiful diagram; it is an accurate account of how work flows today, including informal workarounds. Those workarounds are especially important because they often signal that the official process is missing something.

Once the map exists, the assistant can help interrogate it. Useful questions include: which steps add no information, where does work wait the longest, which handoffs require re-explanation, and which decisions follow a stable rule? The answers become candidates for improvement, but each candidate must be tested against the real constraints of the organization — compliance obligations, system limitations, and the need for human judgment.

Mapping also establishes the boundary between the "current" and "future" process. Without that boundary, teams debate improvements in the abstract. With it, they can point to a specific box in the map and say what will change.

Finding bottlenecks and handoff delays

A bottleneck is the step that limits the throughput of the whole process. Improving a step that is not a bottleneck rarely improves overall results; it just builds inventory in front of the constraint. Identifying the bottleneck therefore comes before choosing an intervention.

Bottlenecks and handoff delays leave detectable signals. A queue that keeps growing is a sign that arrivals exceed capacity at some point downstream. Long idle periods between steps suggest waiting rather than working. Frequent messages asking "where is this?" point to poor visibility. Repeated clarification requests at a handoff suggest the receiving role lacks context from the sending role.

The assistant can help turn raw observations into a structured diagnosis. Provide it with timestamps, step labels, and notes, and ask it to compute waiting time between steps, flag steps where work accumulates, and list the handoffs with the most back-and-forth. It can also draft interview questions for the people involved, which is valuable when the data alone cannot explain why a delay occurs.

The key discipline is to distinguish a bottleneck from a mere annoyance. A step can be slow, confusing, and unpopular without limiting overall throughput. The table of signals and actions later in this chapter helps make that distinction systematically.

Augment, remove, or leave alone

Not every candidate task deserves an AI assistant. When reviewing a process step, there are three honest options.

Augment means the step stays but the assistant makes it faster or more consistent — for example, drafting a first version of a routine summary that a reviewer then checks. Remove means the step does not earn its place at all; it exists because of habit, a legacy system, or a check that no longer protects anything. Leave alone means the step is efficient, low-risk, and not worth the cost of changing it.

Choosing among the three depends on the nature of the step. Work that is high-volume, text-heavy, and repetitive is a strong augmentation candidate. Work with a stable, rule-based decision is often a stronger automation candidate — or a candidate for deletion if the rule no longer serves a purpose. Work that requires negotiation, empathy, or accountability to an affected person usually deserves to stay human-led, though the assistant can still prepare materials for it.

Exam note: Removing a useless step is often a better answer than speeding it up. If a check produces no decisions and no one acts on its output, the exam is likely looking for simplification, not automation.

Pilots, baselines, and small samples

A pilot is a controlled trial of a change before a broad rollout. Its purpose is to reveal problems while they are still cheap to fix. Two ingredients make a pilot informative: a baseline and a measurement plan.

The baseline is a description of how the process performs before the change. Without it, any improvement claim is unanchored. The baseline should use the same definitions and measurement method that will be used during the pilot, otherwise the comparison is misleading. If "cycle time" means one thing before the change and something else during it, the numbers cannot be compared.

The sample should be small but representative. It should include the common case and at least one difficult case, because a change that only works on easy inputs is not ready to scale. The pilot also needs explicit success criteria and stop criteria: what result would justify expanding the change, and what result would cause the team to halt it.

Here is a compact pilot measurement plan in a text form that a team could adapt:

PILOT MEASUREMENT PLAN

Change under test: Assistant-drafted first pass for intake summaries
Scope: One intake queue, one work location
Sample: 20 consecutive cases, including at least 3 complex cases

Baseline (collect before change):
- Cycle time per case: median and 90th percentile, same clock start/stop rules
- Rework rate: share of cases reopened or sent back within 5 business days
- Reviewer edit distance: count of substantive edits to the summary

During pilot:
- Record the same three metrics with identical definitions
- Log every exception, tool failure, and override by the reviewer
- Capture reviewer confidence rating on a fixed 1-5 scale

Success criteria:
- Median cycle time at least 20% lower than baseline
- Rework rate not worse than baseline by more than 2 percentage points
- No critical accuracy failures in the sample

Stop criteria:
- Any critical accuracy failure involving a wrong factual claim passed downstream
- Reviewer confidence averaging below 3 out of 5
- Cycle time improvement below 5% after 20 cases

Decision point: Review results with the process owner and decide to expand,
revise, or abandon. Do not expand on a single favorable metric alone.

A fenced plan like this forces clarity. It states exactly what will be collected, how it will be compared, and what the decision rules are — before anyone sees the results, which reduces the temptation to move the goalposts afterward.

Common mistake: Declaring a pilot successful because participants "liked it." Preference is useful feedback, but it is not evidence of faster cycle time or lower rework. Keep perception and performance as separate measures.

Drafting process documentation and checklists

Once a future process is agreed, it must be documented well enough that another person can follow it. The assistant is well suited to drafting this material because it can turn rough notes into structured steps, produce a checklist from a procedure, and reword the same content for different audiences.

Useful documentation deliverables include a step-by-step procedure, a decision table for exceptions, a checklist for reviewers, and a short "what changed" note for people affected by the new process. The assistant can generate all of these from the process map and the pilot findings. The risk is that it will invent plausible-sounding steps that were never agreed, so every draft must be checked against the source map.

A good checklist is short, observable, and ordered. Instead of "ensure the summary is accurate," write "confirm each figure in the summary matches the source record." Instead of "handle exceptions," write "if the source record is missing, route to the intake coordinator." Specificity is what makes a checklist repeatable.

Documentation is also a control point for process improvement. If a step is hard to describe clearly, that is often a sign the step itself is unclear. Writing the procedure can therefore reveal design flaws that mapping alone did not.

Measuring cycle time and rework

Two measures carry much of the weight in process improvement: cycle time and rework.

Cycle time is the elapsed time from a defined start point to a defined end point for one unit of work. It includes waiting, not just active effort. Because it includes waiting, it tends to expose handoff delays that a measure of "time spent working" would hide. The definition of the start and end points must be fixed and documented, or the metric drifts.

Rework is work done again because the first attempt did not meet the requirement. It can appear as a reopened case, a corrected document, a repeated approval, or a second round of clarification. Rework is a direct measure of quality failure and a hidden source of capacity loss: the time spent redoing work is time not available for new work.

These two measures complement each other. A change that cuts cycle time but raises rework is usually a bad trade, because errors move downstream and cost more to fix later. A change that reduces rework but leaves cycle time unchanged may still be worthwhile if it lowers risk and frees capacity. The strongest case is improvement on both measures without a rise in critical errors.

Metric What it captures Common trap Good companion measure
Average cycle time Overall elapsed time across cases Distorted by a few extreme cases 90th percentile cycle time
Median cycle time Typical case duration Hides the long tail Distribution or percentile view
Rework rate Share of cases needing redo Depends on how "redo" is defined Reason codes for rework
Queue length Work waiting at a step Reflects arrivals as well as capacity Waiting time per step
Edit distance Amount of reviewer correction Sensitive to formatting changes Substantive-error count
Completion rate Share of cases finished Ignores quality of completion Rework rate

Practical tip: When a number looks suspicious, ask how it was defined before asking whether it is good. Most measurement disputes are definition disputes in disguise.

When a spreadsheet or rule-based tool is the better answer

Part of using an assistant well is knowing when not to use it. Many tasks have a deterministic structure: a fixed calculation, a lookup against a table, a threshold check, or a simple conditional rule. For these, a spreadsheet formula or a rule-based automation is faster, cheaper, and more predictable than a generative tool.

A spreadsheet is usually the better choice when the logic is explicit, the data is structured, the volume is moderate, and the user needs to inspect and adjust the rules directly. A rule-based tool is better when the same decision must be applied consistently at high volume with an audit trail. An assistant is better when the input is messy or unstructured, the output requires language, or the task needs judgment that cannot be reduced to a rule.

The table below summarizes the decision signals and the recommended action — a consolidated view you can apply when triaging process steps.

Observed signal Likely interpretation Recommended action
Work waits longest at one approval step Approval is the bottleneck Add capacity or remove a redundant approval before automating anything
Same handoff repeatedly needs clarification Receiving role lacks context Standardize the handoff packet; consider a checklist
A check produces no decisions anyone acts on Step has no protective value Remove the step rather than automate it
High volume of repetitive text drafting Augmentation candidate Pilot assistant-generated first draft with human review
Decision follows a fixed, written rule Deterministic task Use a spreadsheet formula or rule-based tool
Frequent corrections trace to missing fields Upstream data quality problem Fix the input source; do not paper over it downstream
One role is overloaded across many steps Capacity imbalance Rebalance ownership before adding tools
Errors cluster in a single complex case type Edge case not handled Add explicit exception rules and test them in the pilot
Output is rarely read by anyone Documentation or reporting waste Question whether the artifact is needed at all
Change is hard to describe in words Underlying process is unclear Re-map the process before drafting documentation

The exam rewards this kind of discrimination. A scenario describing a fixed calculation is steering toward a spreadsheet, not an assistant. A scenario describing messy narrative input and a need for a written summary is steering toward augmentation with review.

Exam note: "Can the assistant do this?" and "should the assistant do this?" are different questions. The second one weighs determinism, cost, risk, and auditability, not just feasibility.

Worked scenario

A shared services team processes intake requests. Each request arrives as a message with attached notes, is summarized by a coordinator, reviewed by a team lead, and then passed to a delivery role that acts on it. The team is considering using an assistant to write the summaries. Rather than starting with the tool, the coordinator follows a disciplined sequence.

First, the team frames the question narrowly: "Can we shorten the time from request arrival to delivery without increasing errors?" That phrasing sets both a goal and a guardrail. A second goal, "reduce the coordinator's typing," would be too narrow because it might be achieved while the overall process stays slow.

Next, the coordinator maps the current process using recorded timestamps and short conversations with each role. The map shows four steps and three handoffs. Waiting is not evenly distributed: requests sit, on average, more than a day between the review step and the delivery step, while active work at each step is under an hour. A second signal appears in the message history — the delivery role repeatedly asks for context that already exists in the notes but was not carried into the summary.

The coordinator asks the assistant to help interpret the map and to propose candidates. The assistant identifies two distinct issues: a handoff that loses context, and a long wait before delivery. It also points out that the coordinator's manual summarization, while tedious, is not the largest source of delay. That distinction changes the plan.

The team evaluates three options. Automating the summary alone would speed one small step and leave the long wait untouched. Adding a structured handoff packet would address the repeated clarification requests. Changing the review-to-delivery trigger from a batch at the end of the day to a continuous handoff would attack the largest wait. The team chooses the handoff packet and the continuous trigger as the first improvement, with summary drafting as a second, separately measured change.

Before rolling anything out, the team records a baseline: median cycle time, ninetieth percentile cycle time, rework rate, and the number of clarification messages per case. They write a pilot plan modeled on the one earlier in this chapter, with twenty consecutive cases, explicit success and stop criteria, and the same metric definitions before and during the trial. The assistant drafts the new handoff checklist and the "what changed" note; the team lead verifies each checklist item against the map and removes two items the assistant invented.

During the pilot, median cycle time falls substantially, the clarification messages nearly disappear, and rework stays flat. One complex case exposes a gap: the new checklist has no field for a known exception, so the delivery role still has to ask. The team adds an exception field, revises the procedure, and runs a second short pilot before expanding to other queues.

The outcome is instructive. The assistant was valuable as a research aide, a mapping analyst, and a documentation drafter — but the biggest gain came from restructuring a handoff, which is a process change, not a tool change. The team avoided the trap of automating the most visible annoyance while ignoring the true constraint.

Common mistakes

  • Automating a step before mapping the process, which preserves confusion at higher speed.
  • Treating an assistant's fluent summary as verified evidence instead of a lead to check.
  • Improving a step that is not the bottleneck and expecting overall throughput to change.
  • Measuring only time spent working and missing the waiting time between handoffs.
  • Declaring a pilot successful on participant enthusiasm without a baseline or performance data.
  • Changing metric definitions between the baseline and the pilot, making comparison meaningless.
  • Asking the assistant to draft a procedure without checking it against the agreed process map.
  • Choosing a generative tool for a fixed calculation that a spreadsheet would handle more predictably.
  • Ignoring the long tail of cases by reporting only the average cycle time.
  • Skipping stop criteria, so a struggling pilot continues long after it should have been revised.

Chapter summary

  • An assistant is a research partner that helps frame, gather, and structure evidence; the human owns verification and the final decision.
  • Source discipline means tracking provenance, favoring primary sources, and checking the claims that carry the most weight.
  • Research becomes useful when converted into a plan with observable milestones, role-based owners, dependencies, and checkpoints.
  • Map the process before automating it, including waiting time and informal workarounds at handoffs.
  • Bottlenecks are found from signals such as growing queues, long idle periods, and repeated clarification — not from intuition.
  • Each step should be judged as an augmentation candidate, a removal candidate, or best left unchanged; removal often beats acceleration.
  • A credible pilot needs a baseline, a representative small sample, fixed metric definitions, and predefined success and stop criteria.
  • Cycle time and rework are complementary measures; improving one while worsening the other is usually a poor trade.
  • Assistant-drafted documentation is efficient but must be verified against the process map, and checklists should be specific and observable.
  • Deterministic, structured tasks usually belong in a spreadsheet or rule-based tool, not a generative assistant.

Checkpoint questions

  1. A team wants to shorten a slow process. What should they do first, and why?

  2. Which of the following is the strongest sign that a step is a bottleneck rather than merely unpopular? - A. The step is disliked by the people who perform it. - B. Work accumulates before the step while the step itself is actively occupied only briefly each day. - C. The step involves a large amount of text. - D. The step was recently added to the process.

  3. A review step produces a report that nobody reads and triggers no decisions. What is the best response? - A. Use an assistant to generate the report faster. - B. Add more reviewers to the step. - C. Remove the step after confirming it serves no current requirement. - D. Increase the detail in the report.

  4. Which pilot design is most likely to produce a trustworthy result? - A. Compare results to a baseline collected with different metric definitions. - B. Include only the easiest cases to show a clean improvement. - C. Record success criteria after seeing the results. - D. Use a representative small sample, fixed definitions, and predefined success and stop criteria.

  5. A task applies a fixed written rule to a structured table of numbers at high volume. What is the most appropriate tool choice? - A. A generative assistant, because it can handle any task involving text and numbers. - B. A spreadsheet formula or a rule-based tool, because the logic is deterministic. - C. A longer conversation with an assistant to reason through each row. - D. A manual review with no tool, because automation is never appropriate.

  6. During a pilot, cycle time drops by 25% but rework rises sharply. What is the best interpretation? - A. The change is a clear success because the primary metric improved. - B. The change should be expanded immediately to capture the speed gain. - C. The trade-off is suspicious; errors likely moved downstream and the change needs revision. - D. Rework is irrelevant once cycle time improves.

  7. An assistant drafts a new procedure document from a process map. What must happen before the document is adopted? - A. It should be adopted as written because the assistant structured it clearly. - B. It should be checked item by item against the agreed process map and corrected. - C. It should be discarded because assistants cannot draft documentation. - D. It should be shortened until no verification is needed.

Answers and rationale

  1. They should first map the current process and establish a baseline before choosing any change. Mapping reveals where work actually waits, which handoffs lose context, and which steps add no value; the baseline gives them something to compare against. Starting with a tool risks accelerating the wrong step. Defining the goal and guardrail at the same time keeps later decisions anchored to overall throughput and quality rather than a narrow sub-goal.

  2. B. Accumulating work before a step, combined with only brief active occupancy at the step, is the classic signature of a capacity constraint limiting the whole flow. Option A describes dissatisfaction, which does not establish a throughput limit. Option C describes a property of the content, not a constraint. Option D says nothing about current behavior; a recently added step may be efficient or irrelevant. The exam favors observable flow signals over opinion or history.

  3. C. A step whose output is never read and triggers no decisions has no current protective value, so removal is the cleanest improvement; confirmation is needed in case an unrecorded requirement exists. Option A and option D spend effort making a useless artifact better or faster. Option B adds capacity to work that produces nothing. Speeding up waste still produces waste.

  4. D. A representative sample, fixed definitions, and predefined decision rules make a comparison meaningful and reduce hindsight bias. Option A breaks comparability because different definitions measure different things. Option B selects an easy case set that will not reveal edge-case failures. Option C invites moving the goalposts after results are known. Good pilots are designed before outcomes are visible.

  5. B. Deterministic logic on structured data is handled more reliably, cheaply, and auditably by a spreadsheet formula or rule-based tool. Option A overgeneralizes; generative tools are not the default for fixed calculations. Option C wastes effort by asking a tool to reason repeatedly over a rule that can be applied once. Option D rejects automation unnecessarily when a simple deterministic tool is clearly suitable. Feasibility is not the same as suitability.

  6. C. A large speed gain paired with a sharp rise in rework suggests the change is pushing errors downstream, where they cost more to correct; the net effect on capacity and quality may be negative. Option A and option B focus on a single metric and ignore the guardrail. Option D wrongly dismisses rework, which is a direct measure of quality failure and hidden capacity loss. The correct response is to revise the change and retest.

  7. B. Assistant-drafted documentation can invent plausible steps that were never agreed, so each item must be checked against the source map before adoption. Option A trusts fluency as accuracy. Option C rejects a genuinely useful drafting capability. Option D confuses brevity with correctness; a short document can still contain wrong steps. Verification against the agreed process is the controlling requirement.

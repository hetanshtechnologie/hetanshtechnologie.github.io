13. Advanced Topics

Advanced Topics

Design of Experiments (DOE)

DOE is a statistical method for identifying factors that influence a process output. By systematically varying multiple input variables simultaneously, DOE reveals interactions between factors that single-factor experiments miss. Used in Six Sigma and quality improvement.

Knowledge Management

Knowledge management ensures project knowledge is captured, stored, and shared. It distinguishes between:

  • Explicit Knowledge — Documented, codified (manuals, procedures, reports)
  • Tacit Knowledge — Personal, experience-based (know-how, insights, intuition)

Tools: wikis, communities of practice, mentoring, after-action reviews, knowledge bases.

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)

ERP systems integrate business processes across an organization (finance, HR, supply chain, manufacturing). ERP implementation projects are among the most complex PM undertakings due to their scope, organizational change impact, and integration challenges.

Supply Chain Management

Managing the flow of goods, services, and information from suppliers to customers. Key concepts include just-in-time (JIT), vendor-managed inventory (VMI), and supply chain risk management. Project managers coordinate with procurement, logistics, and operations.

Structured Brainstorming

Systematic idea generation techniques:

  • Brainwriting — Participants write ideas silently, then pass them along
  • Nominal Group Technique — Structured ranking and voting
  • Mind Mapping — Visual organization of related ideas
  • Reverse Brainstorming — Identify ways to cause the problem (then reverse them)
  • SCAMPER — Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse

Halo Effect

Cognitive bias where a positive impression in one area influences opinions in other areas. Example: assuming a developer who writes clean code will also be a great project manager. Awareness of this bias helps in objective performance evaluation and resource selection.

Rule of Seven

In control charts, the Rule of Seven identifies a process out of control if seven consecutive data points fall on the same side of the mean, even if they are within control limits. This indicates a systematic shift requiring investigation.

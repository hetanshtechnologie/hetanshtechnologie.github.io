Claude Certified Architect — Foundations (CCAR-F) Full Prep

An independent, original preparation course

This book is a complete, independently written preparation course for a foundations-level examination on architecting agentic systems with a Claude-based assistant. It is designed to be read from start to finish, to be used as a reference during revision, and to be printed as a single volume.

What makes this book different

A certification syllabus is a list of topics. It tells you what exists but says little about the order in which ideas make sense, or about the reasoning that connects them. This course supplies that missing structure. It rearranges the material into a learning path, restates every idea in original language, and adds worked examples, decision tables, design trade-offs, exercises, and review questions.

The order is deliberate. Foundations come first so that later terminology has a meaning. The five architectural layers are introduced one at a time, then compared, then combined in practice papers and case studies. A concept that appears early is often revisited later in a different context; that repetition is intentional, not an editing error.

Who this book is for

  • A solution architect or engineer who needs to justify a design, not just name its parts.
  • A developer preparing to work with an assistant API, a repository coding assistant, and a tool-connection protocol.
  • A business or risk professional who must evaluate agentic proposals and know where human oversight is required.
  • Any candidate who wants to understand why an answer is correct rather than memorise which answer looks familiar.

How to use this book

Work in cycles. Before each chapter, skim the headings and write down what you expect to find. Read once, actively, noting every sentence that changes one of your predictions. Then apply the idea: sketch a design, answer a scenario, or change a configuration. Verify by attempting the checkpoint questions without looking back. Revisit missed items after a day, then after three days, then after a week.

Keep a decision journal. For each chapter, record one decision you would make in a real system and the constraint that drove it. By the final week you will have a catalogue of defensible trade-offs rather than a set of highlighted pages.

A note on terminology

You will meet a handful of recurring terms: a tool is a callable capability with a described contract; an agent is a loop that can perceive, decide, act, and observe; a workflow is an ordered arrangement of steps; a skill is a reusable package of instructions; and context is the working memory available to the model during a task. These words are used consistently throughout the book, and each is defined more fully where it first matters. When a vendor uses a slightly different word for the same idea, this book prefers the plain description so that the concept survives a change of product names.

A final word before you begin

Exam questions in this domain are design questions. A fact recalled without a justification is nearly useless; a trade-off you can defend under pressure is the whole game. Read with that goal in mind, and treat every checkpoint question as a small design review rather than a memory test.

When you are unsure, return to the constraint. Almost every hard question hides one detail — a permission boundary, a latency limit, a confidence threshold, or a regulatory obligation — that makes one option safe and the others merely plausible. Find that detail first, and the answer usually follows. The chapters that follow are built to make that habit automatic.

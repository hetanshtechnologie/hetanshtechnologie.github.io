Speech to Text

Speech to Text is the service that turns spoken audio into written language. It is the foundation of the voice arc and the first service most developers integrate when they add a voice interface to an application. In this chapter you will learn how the service works, how to call it, and how to choose between its recognition modes and configuration options.

What Speech to Text Produces

The core output of Speech to Text is a transcript: a string of text that represents what was said. But a useful transcript carries more than words. The service can also return:

  • Confidence scores that tell you how sure the model is about each phrase.
  • Word timings that tell you when each word started and ended, which is essential for aligning subtitles to video.
  • Punctuation and formatting — periods, commas, question marks, and numbers written out appropriately.
  • Speaker diarization, which labels which person said each segment in a multi-speaker recording.
  • Profanity filtering to mask or remove offensive words.
  • Language detection when the audio language is not known in advance.

Speech recognition is probabilistic. Two runs over the same noisy audio can produce different transcripts, and even clear audio can be ambiguous ("write" vs. "right"). Treat confidence scores as first-class data: applications that must act on a transcript should decide what to do when confidence is low.

How Recognition Works Under the Hood

A speech recognition pipeline has three cooperating parts. The first is the acoustic model, which maps the raw audio waveform into the small units of sound that make up speech. The second is the language model, which decides which sequences of words are plausible in the target language. The third is a decoder that combines both signals, searching for the word sequence that best explains the audio while staying grammatically sensible.

Modern systems are deep-learning models trained on enormous corpora of transcribed speech, which is why they handle accents, background noise, and domain jargon far better than the keyword-matching systems of the past. When you call the service, you point it at the audio and it returns the decoded transcript, usually in well under real time.

Batch Transcription vs. Realtime Recognition

You can deliver audio to Speech to Text in two fundamentally different ways, and the choice shapes the whole application:

AspectBatch transcriptionRealtime recognition
Audio deliveryUpload a file or give a URLStream audio continuously
ProcessingAsynchronous, queuedLive, incremental
Result timingMinutes to hours laterPartial results in near real time
Best forMeetings, podcasts, archived callsCaptions, dictation, voice assistants
Cost profileBilled per audio minuteBilled per streamed second

Batch transcription is a fire-and-forget workflow: you submit the audio, poll for the job status, and download the transcript when it is done. It is efficient for large volumes of pre-recorded content. Realtime recognition opens a connection and receives a stream of intermediate results, each one more complete than the last, until it emits the final result and a period of silence signals the end of an utterance.

Configuration Options

Recognition accuracy improves dramatically when you tell the service about your content in advance. The most important knobs are:

  • Recognition language: setting the expected language focuses both the acoustic and language models. Auto-detection is available but slightly less accurate.
  • Phrase lists: hint words such as product names, technical terms, or people's names that the model might otherwise mishear.
  • Custom Speech models: fine-tuned acoustic or language models trained on recordings and text from your own domain. If you recognize the same jargon all day, a custom model will outperform the generic one.
  • Endpoint configuration: diarization, profanity masking, and automatic punctuation are toggled per request.
  • Audio format: the service accepts WAV, MP3, OGG, and others; specifying the format avoids costly auto-detection.

Start with the prebuilt models before investing in custom training. For most applications a phrase list is enough to fix the handful of words the generic model keeps getting wrong, and it costs nothing to update. Reserve Custom Speech for cases where accuracy on domain vocabulary is a hard requirement.

Calling the Service

Speech to Text is exposed through the Azure AI Speech REST API and through the Speech SDK, which is available for Python, C#, JavaScript, Go, Java, and others. The SDK handles the connection management, audio capture from microphones or files, and the streaming of partial results, so application code stays small. You authenticate with a key and region or, for production, a Microsoft Entra token.

In the next chapter you will use the Speech SDK from Python to transcribe an audio file end to end, and after that you will wire up realtime recognition that listens to your microphone live.

The Speech SDK and the Speech REST API are different products. The REST API performs one-off operations such as a single transcription call or batch job management; the SDK manages long-lived connections, streaming audio, and events. Choose the SDK for anything interactive and the REST API for batch pipelines and one-shot requests.

Summary

Speech to Text converts audio into a transcript and can enrich it with confidence scores, word timings, punctuation, speaker labels, and more. It runs as an asynchronous batch job or as a realtime stream, and it can be tuned with phrase lists, custom models, and per-request configuration.

Prompt Engineering - Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting asks the model to show its reasoning before giving a final answer. Instead of jumping straight to a conclusion, the model works through the intermediate steps — and for many multi-step tasks, working out loud produces dramatically more accurate results. It is one of the most powerful techniques in prompt engineering because it needs no extra training data, just a change in how the question is asked.

Why Reasoning Out Loud Helps

Language models predict the next token one at a time. When asked to answer a multi-step problem immediately, the model must compress an entire chain of reasoning into a single leap — and the probability of landing on the right final answer drops with every step that has to be jumped. When the model writes the intermediate steps first, each step is a small, nearby prediction, and each one becomes input context for the next. The reasoning acts as scaffolding the model builds for itself.

CoT works best for tasks with an inherent step sequence: arithmetic, logic puzzles, multi-condition decisions, and problems where intermediate results feed later decisions. For simple lookups, it adds tokens without adding value.

The Simplest Form: Just Ask for Steps

The baseline version of CoT is a single sentence added to the prompt:

A store gives a 20% discount on items over $50.
A customer buys a jacket priced at $80 and a hat priced at $30.
What is the total the customer pays?

Without CoT, the model may guess a wrong total. Adding the reasoning instruction changes the answer quality:

A store gives a 20% discount on items over $50.
A customer buys a jacket priced at $80 and a hat priced at $30.
What is the total the customer pays?
Think step by step, then give the final total.

The phrase "think step by step" is the classic chain-of-thought trigger. The model produces its intermediate arithmetic, and because each line is correct, the final total is correct far more often than with the bare question.

Explicit Reasoning Steps

For harder problems, you can also model the reasoning structure you want, defining the steps explicitly:

Solve this problem. Show your work in these four steps:
1. What information is given?
2. Which rule or formula applies?
3. Apply the rule to the numbers.
4. State the final answer.

Problem: A store gives a 20% discount on items over $50.
A customer buys a jacket priced at $80 and a hat priced at $30.
What is the total the customer pays?

Structuring the steps makes the reasoning reproducible and easier to debug. When a wrong answer appears, you can see which step went wrong instead of facing a black-box guess.

Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought

Combining few-shot examples with CoT demonstrations teaches the model both the format and the reasoning style. This is sometimes called "chain-of-thought prompting with demonstrations":

Answer each question by reasoning step by step first.

Q: A pencil costs $1.50. A notebook costs 3 times as much.
   What do both cost together?
A: The notebook costs 3 x 1.50 = 4.50.
   Together they cost 1.50 + 4.50 = 6.00.
   Answer: $6.00

Q: A bag holds 4 apples. How many bags are needed for 10 apples?
A: 10 / 4 = 2.5, so 2 full bags hold 8 apples and 2 apples
   are left over.
   Answer: 3 bags (2 full and one partial).

Q: A train travels 60 km per hour. How far in 90 minutes?
A: 90 minutes is 1.5 hours.
   Distance = 60 x 1.5 = 90 km.
   Answer: 90 km.

When combining CoT with few-shot, pick demonstrations that show the reasoning in the middle — not just the final answer. The demonstration's value comes from exposing the intermediate steps.

Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought

Remarkably, a plain trigger works even without examples. Appending a neutral phrase at the end of a hard prompt often forces the model into step-by-step mode:

... and answer by reasoning step by step.
... Let's think step by step.

These zero-shot triggers are cheap to add and easy to test. They are not magic — they simply nudge the model toward a reasoning pattern it learned during training. The trade-off is that the model may also produce verbose output you have to strip away, and it may sometimes reason about the wrong thing entirely while still sounding confident.

Handling the Added Length

Chain-of-thought has a real cost: the model writes all its reasoning tokens before the answer. This affects you three ways:

  • Token cost: every reasoning step is billed. Long chains can multiply the cost of a request.
  • Latency: more output means a slower response.
  • Parsing: the answer is buried in the middle or end of the response and must be extracted reliably.
Extract only the final answer from the model's response with a
simple rule: the line that begins with "Answer:" is the answer.

In production, never show raw chain-of-thought to end users who only need the answer, and never treat the reasoning as a security or safety guarantee. A model can reason fluently toward a wrong or harmful conclusion. The reasoning is a quality aid, not a proof.

When Chain-of-Thought Helps (and When It Does Not)

Task typeCoT benefitReason
Arithmetic and math word problemsHighIntermediate values feed later steps
Logic and multi-condition decisionsHighConditions are checked sequentially
Extraction and simple lookupLowNo chain of reasoning exists
Creative writingLowSteps constrain rather than help
Problems where wrong reasoning is undetectableLimitedPlausible but incorrect chains mislead

Test both versions — with and without the reasoning trigger — on a sample of real inputs before committing. If accuracy is unchanged, drop the chain and save the tokens.

Summary

Chain-of-thought prompting improves multi-step reasoning by making the model write its intermediate steps before the final answer. It works through a simple trigger, through explicit step structures, or through few-shot demonstrations, and it is especially valuable for arithmetic, logic, and multi-condition tasks. The costs are extra tokens, latency, and the need to extract the answer cleanly — and the reasoning must never be trusted as proof of correctness.

Image editing with generative models

Generating an image from a blank canvas is powerful, but many real-world tasks start with an image that already exists. Product teams retouch photos, marketers swap backgrounds, and designers fill in missing parts of a scene. Generative models can do all of this: you hand the model an existing image and tell it what to change, and it produces a modified version. This chapter explains the two main editing techniques — prompt-driven editing and mask-based editing — and when to reach for each one.

Editing Images with Models

Image editing models treat the existing picture as extra input, the same way a chat model treats your prompt as input. The request contains three things: the original image, the instruction describing the change, and the generation settings. The model respects the parts of the image you did not ask about and rewrites the parts you want changed so they blend naturally with the rest.

The same model family that generates images is usually the one that edits them. In Foundry, gpt-image-1 accepts an input image as part of the request, which makes it a good fit for editing workflows. The request travels through the same images endpoint you used for generation.

Prompt-Driven Editing

In prompt-driven editing you supply the original image plus a natural-language instruction, and the model decides what to change. For example:

  • "Turn the gray sky into a sunset."
  • "Replace the wooden table with a glass one."
  • "Change the shirt color to deep green."

The advantage is simplicity: you describe the desired end state in words and let the model figure out which pixels to touch. The model must infer your intent, so the instruction needs to be specific about what changes and, just as important, what stays the same.

Mask-Based Editing

Mask-based editing gives you direct control over which region of the image changes. Alongside the original image you supply a second image, the mask, that marks the area to be regenerated. A common convention is a black-and-white image where white marks the region the model may rewrite and black protects everything else.

This technique is often called inpainting when you want to fill in or replace a region, and outpainting when you want the model to extend the image beyond its original edges. Mask-based editing is the right choice when the region matters more than the words, for example removing an unwanted object or filling a hole in a photo.

Comparing the Two Approaches

AspectPrompt-driven editingMask-based editing
InputsOriginal image + text instructionOriginal image + mask image + text instruction
ControlThe model decides which pixels to changeYou decide exactly which region changes
EffortMinimal preparationYou must create the mask first
Best forGlobal changes, color and style shiftsSurgical edits, removing objects, filling gaps

Real-World Use Cases

  • E-commerce: swapping a product image onto a new background without re-shooting.
  • Photography: removing tourists, cables, or blemishes from a scene.
  • Design: exploring variations of a layout or concept quickly.
  • Document repair: reconstructing torn or occluded regions of scanned images.
  • Content localization: adjusting images so they fit local markets or brand rules.

Editing models can be surprisingly literal. If you ask to "change the background," the model may also subtly change lighting or the subject. Stating what should stay the same — "keep the woman and her pose exactly as they are" — improves consistency.

Quality Considerations

A few practical habits will produce cleaner edits:

  • Start from the highest-resolution source image you have; downscaling later is easier than upscaling an edited result.
  • Keep the instruction focused on the change, not a restatement of the whole scene.
  • For masks, keep protected regions large enough that the model has context to blend against.
  • Expect to iterate. Generation is probabilistic, so generate a few candidates and pick the best.

Image editing inherits the same safety concerns as image generation. Editing a photo of a real person or a real place can produce misleading content. Apply the same rules you would apply to generated images, and be transparent with end users about what a model changed.

Summary

Generative models edit images by taking the original picture as input and applying a text instruction. Prompt-driven editing lets the model decide what to change, while mask-based editing uses a black-and-white mask to pin down the exact region to regenerate. Each technique suits different tasks, and both use the same image endpoint. In the next two chapters you will implement both approaches in Python.

Overview on Microsoft Entra ID

Until now you reached your Foundry project using your personal login, and every tool call happened to work. Real deployments cannot rely on personal accounts: applications, pipelines, and services all need their own identity, and each one must be limited to exactly what it is allowed to do. Microsoft Entra ID is the identity service behind all of Azure, and it is the layer that makes this possible. This chapter gives you the mental model of Entra ID you need before you start wiring agents to it.

What Microsoft Entra ID Is

Microsoft Entra ID is Azure's cloud identity and access management service. It is the modern successor to what used to be called Azure Active Directory, and it keeps three jobs together: who someone is (identity), what they may do (authorization), and how that is proven (authentication). Every Azure subscription, every Foundry project, and every published agent ultimately trusts Entra ID to answer the question "who is calling, and are they allowed?"

You have been using it all along without naming it. Your Azure login, the pop-up that asked you to approve a sign-in, and the token your tools carry silently — all of that is Entra ID at work.

The Tenant: Your Trust Boundary

The organizing unit of Entra ID is the tenant. A tenant is a dedicated directory that represents one organization. It holds that organization's users, groups, and applications, and it has its own set of rules about who is admitted and how they prove their identity.

The tenant concept matters to you as a developer because resources inherit trust from a tenant. When you sign in to the Foundry portal, you sign in as a user from a tenant, and the Azure subscription you work in is bound to that tenant. An application that wants to call your agent must authenticate against the same tenant — or a tenant your organization explicitly trusts.

Think of a tenant as a sovereign country for identity. Users and apps within it are citizens, rules like multi-factor authentication are its laws, and cross-tenant access is the equivalent of a visa agreement — possible, but only when both sides explicitly arrange it.

The Core Objects

Entra ID manages several kinds of objects. You will meet each of them again when you secure your agents:

  • Users: people with an account in the directory. Each user has a sign-in name, an ID, and a set of attributes.
  • Groups: collections of users (and sometimes other groups). Groups exist so permissions can be assigned once to the group rather than repeatedly to each member.
  • Service principals: the identity of an application. When code authenticates, it authenticates as a service principal, not as a person. This is how a background job or a container gets its own identity.
  • Managed identities: a special kind of service principal that Azure creates and manages for a resource. A hosted app can use a managed identity to obtain tokens without ever storing a secret.

Authentication vs. Authorization

The two words are easy to confuse and essential to separate:

  • Authentication answers "who are you?" — proving an identity through a password, a token, a certificate, or multi-factor challenges.
  • Authorization answers "what are you allowed to do?" — deciding whether that proven identity may perform a specific action on a specific resource.

Entra ID performs authentication and issues the tokens your applications present. The authorization decision for Azure resources is then made by role-based access control (RBAC), which you will tour in the next chapter. The token says who you are; the role assignment says what that identity may do.

How Tokens Work

When code or a person authenticates, Entra ID issues a token — a signed block of data that names the caller, the audience it is intended for, and when it expires. Applications and APIs that receive a token validate the signature and check the claims inside. The critical property is that you do not store or send passwords in every request; you present the token, which the receiver can verify independently.

Tokens expire by design. Typical access tokens last about an hour, after which the caller must refresh them. This is why your SDKs silently re-authenticate: the token in memory expired, and the SDK quietly obtained a new one with your cached credentials.

Why Identity Matters for AI Agents

An agent is a resource others will call, which makes identity your first line of defense:

  • Only authenticated, authorized identities should be able to invoke your published agent.
  • Your agent, in turn, may need an identity of its own to call other services — a model endpoint, a storage account, or a search index.
  • Managed identities let the agent obtain credentials without anyone storing a key.
  • Multi-factor requirements and conditional access policies protect accounts that can administer projects.

The moment you publish an agent, identity stops being a theoretical topic and becomes the mechanism that keeps your agent from being a public endpoint anyone can use.

Start forming the habit of asking two questions about any integration: "What identity does this code run as?" and "What is that identity authorized to do?" Teams that answer both questions explicitly almost never have mystery access problems.

Summary

Microsoft Entra ID is the identity backbone of Azure: tenants define trust boundaries, users and service principals represent who is acting, and tokens carry proof of identity. Authentication establishes who you are, while RBAC decides what you may do.

Retrieval Augmented Generation

A large language model knows what was in its training data — and nothing else. Ask it about your company's internal policies, your product catalog, or last quarter's numbers, and it will either guess or confess ignorance. Retrieval Augmented Generation, or RAG, is the standard solution: before the model answers, your application looks up the relevant information in your own data and hands it to the model as context. This chapter explains why that works and how the pipeline is put together.

The Problem RAG Solves

Foundation models fail on private or recent knowledge in two ways:

  • Hallucination: forced to answer, the model fabricates a plausible-looking but wrong response.
  • Staleness: the model's knowledge is frozen at the point its training data was collected.

Both problems have the same root cause: the information the user needs is not present in the model's weights. RAG fixes this by supplying the information at request time, inside the prompt, so the model never has to guess.

RAG is not a model. It is an architectural pattern that combines an ordinary language model with a search system. The model does what it is best at — composing a fluent, grounded answer — and the search system does what it is best at — finding the right documents.

The RAG Pipeline at a Glance

RAG has an offline half and an online half. The offline half prepares your data; the online half answers each question.

  1. Ingest: Collect documents from storage — PDFs, Word files, web pages — and read them into text.
  2. Chunk: Split the text into pieces small enough to retrieve and process.
  3. Embed: Convert each chunk into a vector of numbers using an embedding model.
  4. Index: Store the vectors in a search index that supports similarity lookup.
  5. Retrieve: At query time, embed the user's question and find the most similar chunks.
  6. Augment: Insert the retrieved chunks into the prompt as context.
  7. Generate: Send the prompt to the chat model and return its answer.

Finding the Right Chunks: Keyword vs. Vector Search

Retrieval is the heart of RAG, and there are two broad ways to search:

ApproachHow it matchesGood atWeak at
Keyword (lexical) searchExact words and terms in the queryPrecise terms, names, error codesSynonyms, phrasing differences
Vector (semantic) searchMeaning similarity via embeddingsSynonyms, concepts, natural phrasingExact identifiers and numbers

Modern RAG systems usually combine both: keyword search catches the exact model number or error code, while vector search catches the question phrased completely differently from the document. Hybrid retrieval with a reranking step is the most robust baseline.

Why Chunking Quality Matters

The retrieval step is only as good as the chunks it searches over. A chunk that is too large buries the relevant fact in noise; a chunk that is too small loses context and meaning. Chunking strategy depends on the document — paragraphs and sections work well for prose, while tables may need to be kept whole. The retrieval quality you get is a direct reflection of the chunking choices you made at index time, so it is worth experimenting.

Test your pipeline on the failure cases, not the easy ones. Take ten questions that genuinely require the documents, retrieve for each, and count how many times the correct chunk appears in the top results. That number — retrieval hit rate — predicts answer quality better than any demo.

Grounding the Model: The Augment Step

Retrieval alone does not improve answers. The retrieved chunks must reach the model. The typical pattern is to build a system prompt that explains the role, then place the retrieved text in a clearly labeled context section, and finally give the user's question. The instructions also tell the model what to do when the context does not contain an answer — most importantly, to say so instead of inventing one.

RAG vs. Fine-Tuning

These two techniques are often confused, and they solve different problems:

  • Fine-tuning changes the model's behavior: format, tone, and skill at a task. It does not reliably add facts.
  • RAG supplies facts at request time: up-to-date, private, and verifiable. It does not change the model.

For most knowledge questions, RAG is the better fit: your data can be updated without retraining, and every answer is traceable to a source document. Fine-tuning complements it when the answer style itself must change.

Summary

RAG grounds model answers in your own data by retrieving relevant text at request time and passing it into the prompt. The pipeline runs from ingest, chunk, embed, and index to retrieve, augment, and generate. Keyword search, vector search, and hybrids each have their place; chunking and retrieval quality determine overall answer quality; and for factual, private, or recent knowledge, RAG is usually the right choice over fine-tuning.

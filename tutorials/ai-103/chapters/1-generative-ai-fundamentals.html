Generative AI Fundamentals

What Is Generative AI

Generative AI is a branch of artificial intelligence that creates new content instead of only classifying or predicting existing data. Where traditional machine learning models take an input and produce a label, a score, or a category, generative models produce fresh outputs — sentences, paragraphs, images, audio, or code — that resemble the patterns found in their training data. The word generative is the key: the model synthesizes something that did not exist before, conditioned on what the user asked for.

You interact with these models every day without necessarily realizing it. Autocomplete suggestions while typing an email, an image that appears after you describe a scene, a chatbot that answers customer questions in natural language, and a coding assistant that fills in a function body are all examples of generative systems at work.

How Generative Models Differ from Discriminative Models

It helps to contrast generative AI with the classic machine learning approach. A discriminative model learns the boundary between categories. Show it an image and it decides whether it contains a dog or a cat. Show it a sentence and it decides whether the sentiment is positive or negative. These models are excellent at understanding and labeling, but they cannot produce new content.

Generative models learn the underlying distribution of the training data. Once they capture that distribution, they can sample from it to create new instances. A language model learns the probability of word sequences and can therefore generate new sequences. An image model learns the distribution of pixel patterns and can paint new images. This ability to sample and synthesize is the defining trait of the generative family.

Key Capabilities of Generative Models

Modern generative models bring together several capabilities that earlier systems handled separately:

  • Text generation: Writing, summarizing, translating, rephrasing, and answering questions in natural language.
  • Image generation and editing: Producing pictures from a text description, modifying existing images, or filling in masked regions.
  • Audio and speech: Synthesizing realistic voices, transcribing speech, and translating spoken language.
  • Code generation: Producing programs, explaining code, writing tests, and converting between languages.
  • Structured output: Producing JSON, tables, and schemas that downstream applications can parse directly.

Large Language Models: The Foundation

The engine behind most modern generative applications is the Large Language Model, or LLM. An LLM is a deep neural network trained on enormous amounts of text — books, articles, code, and web pages. During training, the model learns statistical relationships between words and sentence fragments. When you provide a prompt, the model predicts what text is most likely to follow, one token at a time.

Tokens are small chunks of text. A token might be a whole word like cloud, or a partial word like generat. Models are measured partly by how many tokens they can process in a single request — the context window. Larger context windows allow the model to consider more instructions and more source material before answering.

The output of an LLM is fundamentally a prediction of the next token based on the previous tokens. This is why the exact same prompt can produce different answers across runs — the model samples from a probability distribution rather than always selecting the single most likely token. Sampling controls such as temperature and top-p influence how creative or how deterministic that sampling is.

Why Generative AI Matters for Applications

Generative models change what is possible to build with software. Before these models, a developer who wanted a system to understand natural language had to hand-craft rules or train specialized models. Now a single model provides conversational ability, reasoning, translation, summarization, and content creation through a simple API call.

For organizations, the practical impact shows up in concrete ways:

  • Customer support agents that answer questions around the clock.
  • Document processing pipelines that extract and summarize information.
  • Content workflows that draft marketing copy, reports, or product descriptions.
  • Developer tools that boost coding speed and catch mistakes.
  • Knowledge systems that let employees query company documents in plain language.

The Role of Prompts

Generative models are controlled through prompts — the instructions and context you give them. The quality of the output depends heavily on the quality of the prompt. A vague prompt produces vague results; a precise prompt with clear instructions, examples, and constraints produces far more useful output. This field of deliberately crafting inputs is called prompt engineering, and it is one of the core practical skills in this course.

Prompting goes beyond simply writing a sentence. Modern prompting includes system instructions that set the model's role and rules, few-shot examples that demonstrate the expected format, chain-of-thought reasoning that encourages the model to think step by step, and structured output instructions that force results into JSON or other schemas.

Throughout this course you will follow a consistent recipe for working with models: define the role and rules in a system prompt, provide the input or context, specify the desired output format, and then parse the result reliably in your application code.

Building Blocks Covered in This Course

This course treats generative AI as a practical engineering discipline. Beyond the models themselves, you will learn the infrastructure and services that make generative applications production-ready:

  • Microsoft Foundry for deploying and managing models in the cloud.
  • Prompt engineering for controlling model behavior reliably.
  • Agents that combine models with tools, memory, and workflows.
  • Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) for grounding answers in your own data.
  • Guardrails and content safety for deploying responsibly.
  • Observability, evaluations, and monitoring for measuring quality in production.
  • Orchestration frameworks such as LangChain and LangGraph.
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP) for connecting models to external systems.
  • Vision, speech, language, and document services for multimodal applications.

Summary

Generative AI is the discipline of creating new content from learned patterns, and large language models are its primary engine. These systems are controlled through prompts, integrated through APIs, and made reliable through engineering practices such as RAG, guardrails, and monitoring.

Overview of Fine tuning a model

The previous chapter showed you how to run a fine-tuning job on Azure OpenAI. This chapter steps back and explains what fine-tuning actually does to a model, which techniques exist, and — just as important — when fine-tuning is the right answer and when it is not.

What Fine-Tuning Changes in a Model

A pretrained model is a generalist. Fine-tuning continues the training process on a smaller, targeted dataset, adjusting the model's weights so its behavior shifts toward the patterns in that dataset. It is not adding a memory bank of facts; it is nudging the probabilities the model assigns to different responses.

In practical terms, fine-tuning teaches a model:

  • Format and structure: always reply as JSON, follow a fixed template, or match a house style.
  • Tone and persona: respond like a legal advisor, a friendly support agent, or a terse engineering assistant.
  • Task mastery: get better at a narrow, repeated task such as extracting fields from a message or converting product descriptions.
  • Limited domain conventions: internal jargon, product names, and expected response shapes.

A useful mental model: prompting steers the model at inference time; fine-tuning changes the model itself. The two are complements. Fine-tuning often works best when you first agree on a prompt — including a system message — and then train the model to follow it.

How Training Is Done

Fine-tuning on modern large models usually falls into one of two families:

TechniqueWhat it doesTypical trade-off
Full fine-tuningUpdates all of the model's weights on your datasetHighest flexibility, highest compute cost
Parameter-efficient methods (for example LoRA)Freezes the original weights and trains small adaptersMuch cheaper; smaller footprint; strong results for most tasks

Parameter-efficient approaches are why fine-tuning has become practical for ordinary teams: you get most of the benefit of retraining at a fraction of the cost, and the trained adapters are small enough to deploy and swap quickly.

What Fine-Tuning Does NOT Do

It is just as important to know the limits. Fine-tuning does not reliably:

  • Inject large amounts of new factual knowledge. If users ask about facts, retrieval is a far more reliable way to supply them.
  • Fix every weak output. If the base model struggles with a task, a small dataset may only polish, not repair, its behavior.
  • Remove fundamental safety behaviors. Content filters and moderation still apply to fine-tuned models and are part of your responsibility.

This is why fine-tuning and Retrieval Augmented Generation are often mentioned together: fine-tuning shapes how the model responds, while RAG supplies what it should talk about.

Use a decision ladder: can a better prompt fix it? Then prompt. Does the answer depend on facts your users need to be right about? Add retrieval. Only when the behavior — format, tone, task style — is still wrong should you reach for fine-tuning.

Risks and Pitfalls

Fine-tuning rewards care and punishes carelessness. Common failure modes include:

  • Overfitting: a small dataset makes the model memorize examples and fail on anything new.
  • Catastrophic forgetting: aggressive training on narrow data degrades the model's general skills.
  • Biased data, biased model: the tuned model inherits every skew present in the training set.
  • Evaluation gaps: without a held-out test set, "looks better in a demo" is easy to fool yourself with.

Data quality dominates every other decision in fine-tuning. A few hundred clean, consistent, representative examples frequently outperform thousands of noisy ones. Before starting any job, fix the dataset, not the hyperparameters.

Summary

Fine-tuning adjusts a model's weights to reshape how it responds: its format, tone, and performance on a narrow task. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA make it affordable, while the technique still cannot replace retrieval for factual accuracy. Use it as the last rung of the ladder — after prompting and after grounding with data — and treat dataset quality as the lever that decides success.

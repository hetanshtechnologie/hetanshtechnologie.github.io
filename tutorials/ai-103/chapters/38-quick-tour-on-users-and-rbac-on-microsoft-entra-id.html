Quick tour on users and RBAC on Microsoft Entra ID

The previous chapter gave you the theory of identity. This one is a quick, practical tour of the parts you will touch most often: users, groups, and role-based access control. You will learn how permissions flow from a directory object down to a specific Foundry project, and how to reason about who can do what — the skill that separates a secure deployment from a vulnerable one.

Users and Groups in Practice

Users are the people in your organization: employees, partners, and developers who need to reach the portal, manage resources, or call APIs. Groups bundle those people so that permissions follow the group, not the individual.

Groups are the lever that keeps access manageable:

  • You assign a group a role once, and every member inherits it.
  • A new employee joins the team — you add them to the group, and their access appears automatically.
  • Someone leaves — you remove them from the group, and their access disappears everywhere.

Without groups, every personnel change means hunting through dozens of individual role assignments. With groups, it is one edit.

There are two kinds of groups worth knowing: security groups, used for permissions, and Microsoft 365 groups, used for collaboration such as team sites and mailboxes. For controlling access to Azure resources you will work with security groups.

RBAC: Role-Based Access Control

RBAC is the authorization system for Azure resources. It is built on two ideas:

  • Roles are collections of permissions, for example "read resources" or "create resources."
  • Assignments bind a role to an identity (a user, group, or service principal) at a specific scope.

A permission only exists when all three parts are in place: a role, an identity, and a scope. Remove any one and the access disappears.

Scopes: How Far a Role Reaches

The scope is where RBAC becomes precise. Roles can be assigned at several levels, and each level covers everything below it:

ScopeCoversExample
Management groupMultiple subscriptionsCompany-wide read access
SubscriptionAll resource groups and resources inside itBilling viewer
Resource groupAll resources in that groupDeploy team on an app group
Individual resourceJust that resourceAccess to one storage account

Permissions are inherited downward, and an identity can hold multiple assignments. The effective permission is the union of all its assignments. This is why it is possible to be blocked at one scope and allowed at another — and why the narrowest scope that works is usually the right choice.

Built-In Roles You Will Meet

Azure ships many built-in roles, but a handful do most of the work in practice:

  • Reader: view resources but change nothing.
  • Contributor: create and manage resources, but cannot grant others access.
  • Owner: everything Contributor does, plus the ability to assign roles.
  • User Access Administrator: manages role assignments without managing the resources themselves.

Notice the line between Contributor and Owner: it is role assignment. Being able to hand out permissions is what makes an assignment dangerous, and that privilege should stay rare.

RBAC for Azure vs. Entra ID Roles

Azure has two separate role systems that are easy to mix up:

  • Azure RBAC controls access to Azure resources — resource groups, storage, and yes, your Foundry project. These are the roles listed above.
  • Entra ID roles control access to the directory itself — managing users, resetting passwords, and configuring identity settings. These are roles like Global Administrator and User Administrator.

A developer can have Contributor on a resource group (Azure RBAC) while having no Entra ID role at all. Keeping the two systems separate is what prevents someone who deploys resources from also managing the directory that protects them.

Applying RBAC to Your Foundry Project

Your prompt agent lives in a Foundry project that is itself an Azure resource. The same rules apply:

  • Give contributors the least privilege they need to do their job — creating agents, models, and tools.
  • Give readers view-only access so stakeholders can inspect agents without changing them.
  • Put the developers who need to invoke the published agent into a group, and assign that group a role with the specific permission to call it.
  • Assign role-based permissions through groups rather than individuals so access stays manageable as the team changes.

When you create a new Foundry project, resist the urge to grant everyone Owner. Start with a small Owners group, add a Contributors group for the working team, and add a Readers group for anyone who only needs visibility. You can always widen access later; tightening it is much harder once people depend on it.

Before you rely on any role, test the assumption. Sign in with a test account that holds only the role you assigned and try the exact workflow a real user would. "It should work" is not a security review; a dry run of the least-privilege setup is.

Summary

RBAC grants access by assigning roles to identities at a scope: users and groups are the identities, Azure roles define the permissions, and scopes decide how far the permissions reach. For your agents this translates into concrete decisions — who may create, who may view, who may invoke — and the general rule holds everywhere: assign the narrowest role, at the smallest scope, through groups, and verify the result.

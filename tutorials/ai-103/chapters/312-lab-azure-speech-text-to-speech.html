Lab - Azure Speech - Text to Speech

Before writing any code, it helps to hear what the Azure Speech service can produce. Speech Studio is a web portal where you can try text-to-speech with real neural voices, tune the speaking rate and pitch, and download the resulting audio — all without writing a single line of code. In this lab you will use Speech Studio to synthesize a sentence, experiment with voices and styles, and save the audio you like best for later comparison with the Python version in the next chapter.

Exercise: Synthesizing speech in Speech Studio

Step 1 — Open Speech Studio and choose Text to Speech

Browse to speech.microsoft.com and sign in with your Azure credentials. The first time you arrive, the portal may ask you to select a subscription and a Speech resource. If you do not have a Speech resource yet, follow the in-portal link to create one (or create it in the Azure portal first and come back).

Once you are in, open the Text to Speech gallery. You will see a page with an input box, a voice picker, and playback controls.

Step 2 — Pick a voice and listen

Type a sentence such as Welcome to the speech section of this course. Choose a voice from the dropdown — start with en-US-JennyNeural — and press the play button. You should hear a natural female voice reading your text. Now switch to a different voice, such as en-US-GuyNeural or a voice from another locale, and notice how the delivery changes.

Step 3 — Adjust the delivery

Most of the voice cards expose sliders for speaking rate and pitch. Slow the rate down and listen again, then speed it up. These two controls map directly to the rate and pitch attributes you will set programmatically with SSML in a later chapter, so experimenting here gives you a feel for the effect of each value.

Step 4 — Try a style

Neural voices that support expressive styles let you switch the emotional tone. In the voice settings, look for a style selector (values such as cheerful, sad, or excited). Select a style and replay the same sentence. The words are identical, but the delivery communicates something completely different.

Step 5 — Export the audio

Once you are happy with a voice and style, export the audio. The portal provides a download option that saves the synthesized clip as an audio file. Keep this file — in the Python lab you will generate a similar clip from code and compare the two.

What the Portal Shows You

Beyond playback, the Text to Speech page reveals the technical details behind your clip. You can inspect the SSML document that the portal generated for your selection. That document is exactly what an application sends to the service, so reading it is a practical way to learn how voices, rate, pitch, and style are expressed as markup.

Using Audio for Testing

Speech Studio is primarily a testing and prototyping environment. It is not designed to be embedded in production applications; real applications call the REST API or use the Speech SDK. But the audio you generate here is useful in two ways: you can play it for stakeholders to approve a voice direction, and you can feed it back into the service later to test speech-to-text recognition.

When you are deciding on a voice for a product, test the same text in several voices and styles, and listen with your actual audience in mind. A warm conversational voice fits a consumer app, while a clear neutral voice often suits IVR menus and announcements.

Summary

You used Speech Studio to synthesize speech from text, explored voices, rate, pitch, and expressive styles, and exported an audio file. This hands-on feel for the output will make the code you write in the next lab far easier to judge, because you already know what good synthesis sounds like.

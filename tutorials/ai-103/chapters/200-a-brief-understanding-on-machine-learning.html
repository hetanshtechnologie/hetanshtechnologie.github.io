A brief understanding on Machine Learning

Generative AI did not appear from nowhere. It is the latest branch of a field that has been growing for decades: machine learning. Before you can understand why language models behave the way they do, it helps to understand what machine learning is, how it is different from traditional programming, and what it means for a system to "learn" from data. This chapter gives you that foundation in plain language.

What Is Machine Learning

Machine learning is a way of building software in which the computer figures out the rules for itself, instead of having them written by a programmer. In traditional programming, you write explicit instructions — if the temperature is above 30 degrees, then print a warning. The program follows your rules exactly. In machine learning, you provide examples instead of rules, and the algorithm finds the pattern that connects the examples.

For example, you could show a program thousands of labelled photos of cats and dogs. The program does not receive a rule such as "cats have pointy ears." Instead, it studies the photos and learns, on its own, what visual features tend to separate the two groups. Later, when you give it a brand-new photo, it applies what it learned to guess which animal is in the picture.

How Machines Learn: Data, Features, and Labels

Three ingredients appear in nearly every machine learning project:

  • Data: the raw material. Emails, photos, sensor readings, text, and so on.
  • Features: the measurable properties of the data that the model uses — for an email, features might be word counts; for a photo, they might be colour and shape patterns.
  • Labels: the answers you are trying to predict — for an email, "spam" or "not spam"; for a photo, "cat" or "dog".

During training, the model is shown examples of features together with their labels. It adjusts its internal parameters until it can predict the labels from the features reasonably well. Once trained, the model can take features it has never seen and produce a prediction.

Three Kinds of Machine Learning

Not all learning looks the same. There are three broad families, distinguished by what kind of data they are trained on:

TypeData usedGoalEveryday example
Supervised learningData with correct answers (labels)Predict the label for new dataSpam detection, house-price prediction
Unsupervised learningData with no labelsFind structure or groups in the dataCustomer segmentation, anomaly detection
Reinforcement learningAn environment plus rewardsLearn a policy that maximises rewardGame-playing agents, robotics

Most of the models in this course are trained with supervised learning in spirit, but with a twist: a language model is trained to predict the next word in a sentence, which means every sentence in its training data is effectively a self-generated label. No human has to hand-label trillions of words — the data itself provides the answer.

Training Versus Inference

Machine learning systems have two distinct phases of life:

  • Training: the expensive, slow phase where the model learns from data. This is where most of the compute is consumed, and for large language models it happens in massive data centres over many weeks.
  • Inference: the fast phase where the trained model is used. Every time you ask ChatGPT a question or call a deployed model, you are doing inference.

This distinction matters practically. When you deploy a model in Microsoft Foundry, you are not training anything — you are setting up an inference service. The model's weights are already fixed from training; the deployment just makes them available to call.

Why Machine Learning Fails Sometimes

A model is only as good as its training data, and that creates well-known failure modes:

  • Bias: if the data over-represents one group, the model's answers will favour that group.
  • Overfitting: the model memorises the training examples and performs poorly on new, unseen ones.
  • Garbage in, garbage out: noisy or incorrectly labelled data produces an unreliable model.

Whenever you are surprised by a model's answer, ask two questions. First, what data would the model have seen during training? Second, was this an inference-time problem, such as a poorly written prompt? Most "wrong" answers trace back to one of those two.

Summary

Machine learning builds software by learning patterns from data rather than following hand-written rules. The field splits into supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning, and every system has a separate training phase and inference phase. Large language models are a special case: they learn from enormous amounts of text by predicting what comes next.

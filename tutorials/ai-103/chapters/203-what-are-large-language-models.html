What are Large Language Models

Behind ChatGPT and every other modern text assistant sits a large language model, usually abbreviated LLM. An LLM is a machine learning model trained on a truly enormous quantity of text, capable of producing fluent and contextually appropriate text in response to a prompt. This chapter explains what these models are, how they were built, how they generate text, and what their strengths and limits are.

The Basic Idea

The core task an LLM learns is deceptively simple: given a sequence of words, predict the next word. During training, the model is shown millions of books, articles, websites, and code repositories, and for every sentence it learns to predict what word comes next. Through this exercise, repeated billions of times, the model develops an internal understanding of language — grammar, vocabulary, facts, style, and even reasoning patterns.

At inference time, the same task powers everything. To answer a question, the model predicts the next word after your prompt, then the next word after that, and so on, building the answer one word at a time. The words are not chosen randomly: each one is chosen according to probabilities the model has learned.

The phrase "understand" is doing a lot of work here. An LLM has no consciousness or intention. It has learned statistical relationships between words. When its outputs look like genuine understanding, that is because human language itself is highly patterned, and the model has captured those patterns at an enormous scale.

Tokens and the Context Window

LLMs do not process words directly; they process tokens. A token is a chunk of text — often a word, but sometimes a part of a word. For example, the word "cloud" might be one token, while a long word like "generative" might be split into two or three tokens. The number of tokens in a piece of text is what models count, price, and limit.

The context window is the maximum amount of text — input plus output — the model can consider in a single request. Modern models have context windows of tens of thousands of tokens or more. The size matters because it determines how much instruction, background, and source material you can fit into one prompt before asking for an answer.

PropertyWhat it means
TokenThe atomic unit of text the model reads and writes
Context windowThe maximum tokens the model can see in one request
ParametersThe internal numbers the model adjusts during training, counted in the billions

Why "Large" Matters

The "large" in large language model refers to two things: the size of the training data and the number of parameters in the network. A model with, say, 175 billion parameters can capture far more subtle patterns than a model with 1 billion parameters. This is why modern LLMs can translate, summarise, reason, and write code — capabilities that were not explicitly programmed but emerged from scale.

You do not need to know exactly how many parameters a model has to work with it. What matters in practice is the trade-off: larger models tend to be more capable but slower and more expensive per call, while smaller models are cheaper and faster but less capable. Choosing a model is about balancing that trade-off for your task.

What LLMs Can Do

  • Write and edit text in almost any style and length.
  • Answer questions by drawing on what they learned during training.
  • Summarise and simplify long documents.
  • Translate between languages.
  • Reason about problems when asked to think step by step.
  • Generate and explain code, and convert between programming languages.
  • Extract structured information from unstructured text.

What LLMs Cannot Do

  • They do not have live knowledge. Their training data has a cutoff date, so they miss recent events unless connected to search.
  • They hallucinate. They can produce fluent, confident statements that are false.
  • They have no real memory. Everything they know about a conversation must fit inside the context window.
  • They are not calculators or databases. Arithmetic and precise facts are unreliable without help from tools.

Treat an LLM as a very well-read but fallible colleague. Use it for drafts, explanations, and creative work, but verify facts and figures against reliable sources, and ground important answers in your own data with retrieval augmented generation.

From LLM to GPT

GPT — Generative Pre-Trained Transformer — is the specific family of large language models built by OpenAI. Now that you understand what an LLM is, the next chapter breaks down that acronym, piece by piece.

Summary

A large language model learns to predict the next token by studying enormous amounts of text, and it generates answers token by token at inference time. Its capabilities — writing, summarising, translating, reasoning, and coding — emerge from its scale, while its limits — knowledge cutoff, hallucinations, and no durable memory — come from the same design. GPT, the topic of the next chapter, is OpenAI's family of these models.

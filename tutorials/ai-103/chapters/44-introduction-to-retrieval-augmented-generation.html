Introduction to Retrieval Augmented Generation

By now you have called models that answer questions from their own training data. That works well for general knowledge, but most real applications need the model to talk about information it never saw: your product manuals, your policies, your databases. Retrieval Augmented Generation, or RAG, is the standard pattern for giving a model access to external data without retraining it. Instead of hoping the model knows the answer, you fetch the relevant information at query time and hand it to the model as context. This chapter explains the problem RAG solves, how the pattern works, and the key design decisions you will meet in the labs.

The Limits of Unmodified Language Models

A model trained once on a fixed corpus has three practical weaknesses:

  • Knowledge cutoff: it knows nothing that happened after training, and nothing at all about your private documents.
  • No grounding: answers about niche, internal, or fast-changing topics are guesses, so it can produce confident nonsense.
  • No citations: it cannot point to a source document, which makes verification and trust hard.

Fine-tuning can shift a model's style and tone, but it cannot cheaply and reliably inject fresh or organization-specific facts, and it needs retraining every time the data changes.

The RAG Pattern: Retrieve, Augment, Generate

RAG splits the job into three clearly separated steps:

  1. Retrieve. Search your data store for the passages most relevant to the user's question.
  2. Augment. Insert those passages into the model prompt as additional context.
  3. Generate. Let the model compose an answer that is grounded in the supplied context.

Because the facts come from your index at query time, updating your data means updating the index, not retraining the model. And because the model sees the source passages, it can be told to answer only from them and to cite them.

The Pipeline in Detail

A RAG system has an offline half and an online half.

Indexing (offline). Raw documents are parsed into plain text, split into chunks of manageable size, converted into embeddings by an embedding model, and stored in a search index. Each chunk keeps metadata such as its source file and title.

Retrieval (online). The user's question is embedded with the same embedding model, and the search service returns the top-k chunks whose vectors are nearest to the question vector. Many systems combine this vector search with classic keyword search to improve recall.

Generation (online). The retrieved chunks are formatted into a context block, combined with a system instruction and the question, and sent to a chat model. The model answers strictly from the context, and the application can surface the source references to the user.

Chunking and Embeddings

Two decisions dominate indexing quality.

  • Chunk size and overlap: chunks that are too large dilute the relevant information; chunks that are too small lose context. A common starting point is a few hundred words per chunk with a small overlap between neighbors, tuned to your documents.
  • Embedding model: the embedding model must be the same for indexing and querying, and should match the languages and content types in your corpus. Models such as the text-embedding family in Foundry map text to vectors of hundreds of dimensions, where similar meaning sits close together.

RAG vs Fine-Tuning

ConsiderationRAGFine-Tuning
Fresh dataUpdate the index at any timeRequires retraining
Private dataRetrieved at query timeBaked into weights
CitationsEasy to provide sourcesNot natural
Training costNoneCompute and data prep
Best atFactual groundingStyle, format, domain fluency
Typical useKnowledge assistantsSpecialized writers, classifiers

The two are not mutually exclusive. Many production systems fine-tune for tone and use RAG for facts.

RAG only works as well as its retrieval. If the right passage is never found, the model cannot ground its answer, so evaluation should always measure retrieval quality (did we find the right chunk?) as well as answer quality.

Summary

RAG grounds language models in external data by retrieving relevant passages and adding them to the prompt at query time. It avoids retraining, keeps answers current, and enables citations. The pattern has three parts: indexing documents into chunks and vectors, retrieving the most relevant chunks for a question, and generating an answer from that context.

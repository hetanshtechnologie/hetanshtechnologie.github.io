Lab - Conversational Language Understanding - Training the Model

Your project now contains intents, entities, and a set of labeled utterances. In this lab you will start a training job, inspect how the service splits your data, review the evaluation results, and read the confusion matrix that shows where the model still struggles. Training is the step where the raw examples you entered become a usable prediction model.

Prerequisites

  • The CLU project with intents, entities, and labeled utterances from the previous labs.
  • At least a few utterances per intent so the training job has something meaningful to learn from.

Understanding the Training Split

When you train a CLU project, the service does not simply memorize your examples. It holds back a portion of your data to measure how well the model generalizes. By default the service splits the labeled data, reserving a small percentage for evaluation while the rest is used for actual training. The reserved examples are not shown to the model during training; they are used afterward to compute how many predictions were correct.

With very small projects the evaluation set may be tiny, which makes the reported accuracy numbers noisy. Treat those numbers as a rough signal and always verify behavior by testing new sentences yourself.

Step 1: Start a Training Job

Exercise 1: Train the model in Language Studio

  1. Open your project in Language Studio and select Training jobs from the left menu.
  2. Click Start a training job.
  3. Enter a name for the job, for example clu-model-v1.
  4. Choose the evaluation option. Leave the default data split unless you have a reason to change it.
  5. Click Train. The job moves through queued, running, and succeeded states. Training usually finishes in a few minutes.

Training is asynchronous. You can close the page and return later; the job status is preserved and the results are stored with the project.

Step 2: Review the Evaluation Results

When the job succeeds, Language Studio shows an evaluation page with aggregate scores for intents and entities:

  • Precision: of everything the model predicted as a given intent, how much was actually correct.
  • Recall: of everything that truly belonged to that intent, how much the model found.
  • F1 score: the harmonic mean of precision and recall, used as a single quality number.

Low scores usually mean either too few examples, inconsistent labeling, or utterances that genuinely overlap between intents.

Step 3: Read the Confusion Matrix

Exercise 2: Inspect where the model confuses intents

  1. On the evaluation results page, open the Confusion matrix view.
  2. Look for cells off the diagonal. A strong prediction where BookFlight was predicted but the true intent was GetHelp means your examples for those intents overlap.
  3. Click a confusing cell to see the exact utterances that were misclassified.
  4. Go back to data labeling and either rewrite the offending examples or add clarifying utterances.

Do not chase a perfect confusion matrix by adding the exact test sentences into your training data. The goal is to see the model handle new phrasing. If you add evaluation sentences verbatim, you only measure memorization.

Step 4: Retrain After Data Changes

Every time you edit utterances, entities, or intents, you must start a new training job before those changes affect the model. Keep a simple versioning habit: give each job a descriptive name, note what changed, and always verify the newest results before deploying. A trained model is not yet callable until you deploy it, which is the subject of the next chapter.

Summary

You started a training job, reviewed precision, recall, and F1 scores for intents and entities, used the confusion matrix to find weak spots, and learned that every data change requires a fresh training run. Your model now exists as a trained version. In the next chapter you will deploy it so applications can actually call it.

9. Hierarchical, Sequential, and Parallel Decomposition

Chapter 8 treated decomposition as the act of splitting a goal. This chapter treats the shape of the split as a separate decision. Two plans can contain the same subtasks yet behave very differently: one runs them as a tree of delegations, another threads them through a fixed chain, and a third scatters them across identical workers. The shape decides who holds context, how many calls you pay for, and how quickly a wrong answer becomes an expensive one. Skipping this decision produces plans that are executable but absurd — twelve sequential calls where twelve shards would do, or forty workers for stages that genuinely depend on one another.

Three shapes, three different cost curves

Name the shapes before comparing them. A hierarchical plan is a tree: a parent owns an outcome, splits it into children, and merges their results. A sequential plan is a chain: stage k consumes the typed output of stage k−1. A parallel plan is scatter/gather: a dispatcher partitions a collection, workers process disjoint slices, and a collector reduces them. Systems combine all three, but each shape has a distinct cost model.

Hierarchical1 + sum of branch nodesDepth × per-level latencyEvery level re-reads child summariesSummary drift between levels
SequentialNumber of stagesSum of stage latenciesContext grows along the chainEarly error contaminates all later stages

Depth and fan-out are the two knobs here. A depth of 3 with fan-out 4 yields 4 + 16 + 64 = 84 leaves plus the root: 85 invocations for "one research job," roughly 128,000 tokens at 1,500 tokens each. That may be the only way to cover sixty-four questions, but it must be explicit. Stop splitting when a leaf fits one sentence and its answer fits in 200 to 800 tokens of structured output.

Hierarchical plans: divide until each leaf is small enough

Hierarchy buys scope reduction: a parent decides which children exist and how to reconcile their answers without holding every document. It suits wide, heterogeneous work — auditing a codebase, surveying competitors, reconciling reports — because the parent works with an abstraction of each child, not raw evidence.

That abstraction is also the weak point. A two-sentence child summary cannot support verification of a discarded detail. Use a fixed return shape: claim, evidence_ids, confidence, open_questions. Evidence IDs let the parent fetch the artifact without re-running the leaf; confidence weights contradictory children; open questions preserve uncertainty rather than converting it into fact.

Hierarchy failure mode — sibling contradiction. Two leaves can return mutually exclusive answers because they saw different source versions. The parent must compare a source_version or as_of field before merging. Without it, the merge step becomes a vote between stale and fresh evidence, and the fresher answer can lose.

Set a depth ceiling and node budget first, for example "depth 3, 60 nodes, stop when estimated output is under 600 tokens." At the ceiling, switch strategy — sample, summarise, or escalate — rather than adding a level. Unbounded recursion makes the plan grow faster than any result.

Sequential plans: typed stages in a fixed order

Sequence is mandatory when one stage's output is the next stage's input: you cannot publish before validation or validate before extraction. The plan makes that dependency explicit and gives each stage a narrow contract. Consider a five-stage document workflow:

#StageInputRequired outputGate
1ExtractRaw PDF12 sections with offsetsPage count > 0
2NormaliseSectionsCanonical fields, unitsRequired fields present
3VerifyCanonical fieldsEach figure with source line0 unresolved conflicts
4ComposeVerified figuresDraft with citationsCitation count = figure count
5PublishDraftVersioned artifact IDReviewer approval token

Gates matter more than prompts. A gate is a cheap deterministic check between expensive probabilistic stages: if stage 2 emits a string where stage 3 expects a number, reject before stage 3 reasons about garbage. Return a structured error naming the field and expected type so the previous stage can repair its own output.

Errors propagate multiplicatively. Five stages at 0.98 reliability give 0.985 ≈ 0.904 clean runs; at 0.95 it is about 0.774. Long chains need checkpoints, not just gates. Persist each validated output keyed by run_id, stage_name, and input_digest, and replay from the last good stage. A stage that mutates the outside world must record its external side-effect ID so recovery does not duplicate it.

When a chain is too long. Chains of eight or more stages usually indicate that some stages can be merged or moved inside a parallel sharding step. Merge stages when the handoff loses more information than the separation gains. Split stages when different failure policies are needed — for example, a permissive drafting step followed by a strict validation step.

Parallel plans: independence is the entry requirement

Parallelism is legitimate only when slices are independent. Shard 240 tickets into 12 groups of 20 with the same instruction and output schema. Wall time is about max(worker_time) + merge_time; token cost is the sum: 12 × 2,500 plus a 6,000-token merge, about 36,000 tokens. Parallelism trades tokens for latency, not economy.

Three problems follow. Race conditions: two workers writing the same key need a deterministic key such as ticket_id + ":" + checksum and a collector that detects duplicates. Partial failure: at 0.9 per worker, all 12 succeeding has probability 0.912 ≈ 0.28. Declare a policy — all-must-pass, quorum (say 90% coverage), or best-effort with a gap list. Barriers stop the collector starting early; close them on all-success, timeout, or terminal failure, and handle late results deliberately.

plan:
  shape: parallel
  shard_key: ticket_id
  workers: 12
  barrier:
    wait_for: all | quorum | timeout
    quorum_coverage: 0.90
    timeout_seconds: 120
  policy: if (coverage < 0.90 && retries_left > 0) retry_missing()
  merge: dedupe_by( ticket_id ) -> sort( severity ) -> emit( report )

Barrier and policy are data, not prompt prose: a dispatcher can enforce them without interpreting language.

Choosing a shape: a decision table and a hybrid

Use dependency structure as the primary signal, then adjust for risk and budget. If every step needs the same 30,000-token reference, a chain or shared artifact beats a tree that copies it into every leaf.

SignalPrefer hierarchicalPrefer sequentialPrefer parallel
Subtask dependenciesParent needs child results; siblings independentStrict producer/consumer orderNone between slices
Data homogeneityHeterogeneous subproblemsEach stage does a different jobIdentical job on many items
Latency targetModerate; depth dominatesLoose; sum dominatesTight; longest shard dominates
Token budgetMedium to highLowest for narrow tasksHigh; one call per shard
Failure containmentGood if parent validates childrenPoor without checkpointsGood with per-slice retries
VerificationPer-leaf evidence requiredGate at every boundarySample or full-check at merge

Hybrids are the norm: a regional analysis might use a tree at the top (one coordinator, four leads), a pipeline per region (extract → normalise → verify), and shards inside extraction. Use hierarchy for abstraction, sequence for order, parallelism for throughput. Never add a shape without naming the problem it solves.

Cost and coordination in practice

Estimate invocations, tokens per invocation, and coordination messages. Summarising 60 tickets hierarchically (1 root, 6 clusters, 60 leaves) costs 67 calls and about 134,000 tokens. Four sequential stages at 8,000 tokens cost 32,000 but propagate upstream errors. Twelve shards at 2,500 plus a 6,000-token merge cost 36,000 and finish in one shard's time. An 8% retry rate raises expected calls to about 72, 4.3, and 13; messages add latency and places for state to get lost.

Architect's heuristic. Choose the least expensive shape whose dependency structure matches the task. Add hierarchy only when one context cannot hold the problem; add sequence only where order is real; add parallelism only where slices are independent. A shape that does not correspond to a real property of the work is pure overhead.
Exercise 9.1 — Choose and justify a shape

For each case, name the primary shape, one reason based on dependencies, and one failure mode you would guard against.

  2. A team asks an agent to assess a 400-file codebase for security risk, produce a ranked list, and cite the exact lines involved.
  3. A legal assistant must draft a clause, check it against a policy manual, obtain an approval token, and archive the approved version.

Hints: (1) sharding; (2) evidence IDs; (3) gates and checkpoints.

Exercise 9.2 — Estimate the bill

A plan processes 96 documents. Compare a chain of 4 stages at 9,000 tokens each with 16 parallel shards of 6 documents at 2,200 tokens each plus a 5,000-token merge. Assume a 10% retry rate on invocations, and coordination messages of 120 tokens each: 3 messages per invocation for the chain, 4 per shard for the parallel plan. Which plan costs fewer tokens, and which finishes sooner if each model call takes 6 seconds and messages add no measurable latency?

Summary

  • Hierarchy reduces scope through abstraction; its risk is information loss between levels.
  • Sequence encodes real dependency order; its risk is error propagation and serial latency.
  • Parallelism buys latency at the price of more tokens, retries, and merge complexity.
  • Choose the shape from task structure first, then adjust for budget and verification needs.
  • Express barriers, policies, and output contracts as data so they can be enforced without prompt interpretation.

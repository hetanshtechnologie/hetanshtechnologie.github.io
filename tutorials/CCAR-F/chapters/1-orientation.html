1. How to Use This Book

A syllabus is a list of destinations. It tells you which topics exist but says little about the order in which they make sense, or about the reasoning that connects them. This guide is built to supply that missing structure. It is an independent study companion: it reorganises a published certification blueprint into a learning path, restates every idea in original language, and adds worked numbers, configuration fragments, decision tables, and failure modes that the blueprint does not provide.

Read it as an engineer, not as a student memorising definitions. The questions you will face are design questions: given constraints on latency, cost, determinism, and risk, which architecture do you choose and why? A fact recalled without a justification is nearly useless here. A trade-off you can defend under pressure is the whole game.

What this guide covers — and what it does not

The scope is the craft of building systems around a language model: deciding when a conversation is enough, when a pipeline is safer, and when an autonomous loop is justified; designing tool contracts a model can actually use; orchestrating work across steps and agents; managing prompts and context as engineering resources; and operating the result in a development environment and in production. The seven parts move from foundations to practice to examination.

It is deliberately not a product manual. Vendor documentation changes faster than any book can track, and it describes features rather than judgement. It is also not a reproduction of any course. Chapter titles here are paraphrased, examples are invented for this text, and no lecture title, duration, instructor name, or provider description appears anywhere in these pages.

Why the order differs from a blueprint

A certification blueprint is organised by exam domain, which is convenient for reporting and terrible for learning. Terms get defined before their motivation is visible, and a concept needed on page three may be explained on page thirty. This book rearranges the material so that each idea is introduced at the moment you need it.

The result is a deliberate dependency chain. You meet the three system patterns before you meet tools, because you cannot judge agency without a baseline. You meet tools before the loop, because a loop with no actions is just a reasoning exercise. You meet stop conditions before architecture selection, because you cannot compare designs without knowing what constrains them. A topic that appears early is often revisited later in a different context; that repetition is intentional, not an editing error.

Do not treat chapter numbers as difficulty ratings. Chapter 6 is short but decides many exam scenarios; Chapter 43 looks like terminology but governs the economics of long sessions. Skimming "easy" chapters leads to guessing between plausible answers.

A study method that survives the exam

Passive highlighting produces little durable recall. Use a cycle instead: predict, read, verify, apply, revisit. Write down what you expect a section to say, mark where you were wrong, then apply the idea — sketch a design, answer a scenario, or change a configuration.

  1. Predict. Spend two minutes on the headings and write three claims you expect to be true.
  2. Read once, actively. Note every sentence that changes a claim.
  3. Apply. Complete the exercises; write actual answers, not mental ones.
  4. Verify. Attempt the five review questions without looking back.
  5. Revisit. Return to missed items after one day, three days, and one week.

For scale: fifty-one chapters at roughly ninety minutes each is about seventy-six hours of first-pass study, plus two twenty-question practice papers and a review week. That is about nineteen hours per week over four weeks, under ten over eight. Below six hours per week, extend the schedule rather than skip exercises.

Exercise 1.1 — Baseline your knowledge. Write down twelve terms you associate with agentic design (for example: tool contract, step budget, handoff, compaction). Rate each 0 = unfamiliar, 1 = recognise, 2 = can explain, 3 = can apply to a design. Re-rate the same list after Chapter 18. Target a shift of at least eight points across the list; a smaller shift means you are reading without applying.

Keep a decision journal: for each chapter, record one decision and the constraint that drove it. By the final week you will have a catalogue of trade-offs rather than highlighted pages.

The exam mindset: justify, do not recall

Scenario questions rarely ask "what is X?" They describe a system with awkward constraints and ask what you would change, or which option is least risky. The correct answer is usually the one that names the binding constraint. Consider a support system handling 12,000 requests per day with a two-second response target and an error budget of 0.1 percent. A fully autonomous loop that makes four model calls per request might average six seconds and fail the target; a fixed pipeline with one call and two deterministic lookups might answer in 700 milliseconds. The "more intelligent" option loses on the constraint that matters.

Practise a three-beat answer: name the constraint, the mechanism that satisfies it, and the cost accepted. Here the two-second target binds; a fixed pipeline removes variable iterations; novel requests fall to a fallback instead of being reasoned through. "Use a workflow because it is cheaper" misses the latency budget entirely.

Exercise 1.2 — Rewrite a recall answer as a design answer. Take the claim "an agent uses tools." Rewrite it as a justified decision for a system that must process 500 invoice PDFs nightly, where 97 percent follow a known template and 3 percent do not. State the constraint, the chosen mechanism, the cost accepted, and one measurement that would tell you the choice was wrong. Keep the whole answer under 120 words.

Anatomy of a chapter and how to use it

Each chapter has a small set of sections, at least two exercises, at least one code or configuration fragment, five review questions, and a short summary. The coloured callouts carry specific jobs. A note adds nuance that is easy to miss on a first pass. A tip is a practical shortcut you can apply immediately. A warning marks a failure mode that appears in real systems and in exam scenarios.

Code fragments are illustrative, not copy-paste solutions. They show the shape of a contract, a policy, or a state transition so you can reason about it. Angle brackets and ampersands are written as HTML entities so the fragments render safely; for example a placeholder appears as <tool_name> and "research and review" as "research & review". When you see a fragment, ask what invariant it enforces and what happens when that invariant is violated.

Review questions are diagnostic. If you miss one, find the single paragraph that answers it and add it to your revisit list rather than rereading. The summary is a retrieval cue: read it before a chapter to prime expectations and after to check retention.

Conventions, scope boundaries, and a first checklist

Chapter titles match the index and serve as revision anchors. Precise terms are defined at first use and reused consistently. Tables compare options on the same axes; worked numbers are rounded to expose relationships, not to model a vendor's pricing.

Three boundaries: this guide promises better decisions, not a score; it does not replace hands-on work with a development environment; and it treats no architecture as universally correct — the answer is a function of constraints.

  • Commit to a schedule before Chapter 2; a plan you can keep beats an ideal plan you abandon.
  • Write answers in full sentences during practice, even when the real exam is multiple choice.
  • Track why you chose an option, not just which option you chose.
  • Revisit missed questions on a spaced schedule rather than rereading everything.
  • Build at least one small artifact per part of the book.

A chapter is finished when you can state its central trade-off in one sentence and name a decision it changes.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does this book reorder topics instead of following the blueprint's domain sequence?
  2. What are the five steps of the recommended study cycle, and which step produces the most useful study material?
  3. A system has a two-second response target. Why does that constraint favour a fixed pipeline over a four-call autonomous loop?
  4. What is the purpose of the warning callout, and how should you respond when you encounter one?
  5. State the three-beat structure of a strong scenario answer and explain why each beat is necessary.

Summary

  • This is an independent study guide that reorganises a blueprint into a learning path and uses entirely original explanations.
  • Topics are introduced in dependency order: patterns, tools, loops, controls, architecture, then decomposition.
  • The effective study cycle is predict, read, apply, verify, revisit, with spaced returns to missed items.
  • Exam questions test justification: name the binding constraint, the mechanism, and the accepted cost.
  • Callouts and exercises have distinct roles; code fragments illustrate invariants rather than ready-made solutions.
  • Plan roughly seventy-six hours of first-pass study plus practice papers and a final review week.

49. Exam Readiness and Study Plan

What this chapter is for

Knowing the material and being ready to answer exam questions are two different skills. The first is built by reading, building, and making mistakes. The second is built by practising decisions under time pressure, recognising how questions are framed, and recovering quickly when you are unsure. This chapter turns the technical content of the book into a repeatable preparation routine. It also sets expectations about what the two practice papers in this book can and cannot tell you.

The certification name in this book is used as a topic label only. This is an independent guide. It does not contain official questions, and nothing here should be treated as a guarantee of exam content. We focus on the durable engineering judgements that a well-designed certification would reasonably test: choosing an architecture, bounding a loop, designing a tool contract, containing a failure, and preserving context across a long session.

A note on the practice papers. Chapters 50 and 51 contain original questions written for this book. They are designed to test the same kinds of decisions as the exam domains, not to reproduce any real test item. Use them to find weak spots, not to predict exact questions.

The five domains as a study map

The syllabus behind this guide groups topics into five domains. Each domain corresponds to a distinct kind of judgement. When you review, tag every weak answer with a domain so you can see where your effort should go.

DomainCentral questionTypical exam trap
Agentic architectureWho controls the next step, and how is that control bounded?Choosing an agent where a deterministic workflow would be cheaper and safer.
Task decomposition and planningHow is a large goal broken into verifiable pieces?Splitting work by activity rather than by outcome, leaving nobody accountable for a result.
Multi-agent orchestrationHow do specialised actors exchange state without losing continuity?Passing large prose blobs between agents instead of typed, minimal payloads.
Reliability and oversightWhere are failures detected, and who approves risky actions?Trusting prompt instructions as a safety control on an irreversible path.
Tool design and MCPIs the boundary between model and system precise, secure, and observable?Publishing broad tools with vague descriptions and trusting the model to use them carefully.
Claude CodeHow is a coding assistant configured, scoped, and automated?Putting everything in one global instruction file and fighting precedence bugs.
Prompt engineeringIs the instruction specific, structured, and testable?Adding more words instead of adding constraints and examples.
Context managementWhat survives, what is discarded, and how do we know it is true?Summarising away the exact facts that made an answer correct.

Notice that the domains overlap. A handoff is simultaneously an orchestration problem, a context problem, and a reliability problem. Exam questions often sit at these intersections. When you meet one, identify which domain owns the decision and which domains supply constraints.

Diagnose before you study

Before reviewing anything, spend forty-five minutes on a cold self-assessment. Do not open the book. Write short answers to twelve prompts drawn from different chapters, such as:

  1. Name three signals that a fixed workflow is the better choice than an agent.
  2. Describe the difference between a stop condition and a step budget.
  3. List four fields that belong in a handoff payload.
  4. Explain why a prompt-level rule is not a security boundary.
  5. Give two properties of a good tool description.
  6. State the difference between an MCP tool and an MCP resource.
  7. Explain how CLAUDE.md files resolve conflicts.
  8. Describe when extended thinking is worth its cost.
  9. Name three failure modes of schema-constrained output.
  10. Explain why summaries lose facts and name a mitigation.
  11. Describe what stratified sampling protects against.
  12. State what a claim-to-source mapping is for.

Score each answer as solid, partial, or missing. Be honest — the purpose is to allocate time, not to feel good. A useful rule is to spend half of your remaining study time on "missing" topics, a third on "partial", and only the remainder on "solid" topics that need a quick refresh.

Exercise 49.1 — Domain histogram. Take the twelve prompts above and tag each answer with a domain. Count your partial and missing answers per domain. Write the three weakest domains at the top of a page; every study session until the exam should touch at least one of them.

A four-week preparation schedule

The schedule below assumes roughly six to eight hours a week. Compress it if you have less time, but keep the order: architecture before orchestration, tools before protocol details, and reliability before context. Later topics assume earlier ones.

WeekFocusChaptersPractice output
1Foundations and control1–12Write one-page architecture decision for a familiar task.
2Orchestration and reliability13–18Draw a handoff schema and a retry/fallback table for a multi-agent flow.
3Tools, MCP, and Claude Code19–36Build one small tool with strict validation and one CLAUDE.md hierarchy.
4Prompts, context, and exams37–51Run both practice papers, review every miss, re-answer weak domains.

Each session should follow the same rhythm: ten minutes of recall without notes, thirty minutes of focused reading or building, fifteen minutes of questions, and five minutes writing what you got wrong and why. The last step matters most. A mistake that is written down becomes data; a mistake that is merely felt becomes anxiety.

How to answer scenario questions

Scenario questions give a short situation and ask for the best action. They are not trivia tests. Use a consistent four-step method:

  1. Name the failure. State in one sentence what is going wrong or what risk is present. If you cannot name it, re-read the stem.
  2. Locate the boundary. Decide which component owns the decision: model, tool, orchestrator, harness, or human. Most wrong answers put responsibility in the wrong place.
  3. Choose the cheapest effective control. A schema check beats a review prompt; a deterministic branch beats a model decision; blocking beats warning on an irreversible action.
  4. Keep the system observable. Prefer the option that leaves a trace, a version, and a reason. If two answers are equally effective, the more inspectable one is usually correct.

Be suspicious of answers that add a model call to solve a problem that code can solve. Also be suspicious of answers that add an agent to solve a problem that a branch can solve. In architecture questions, simplicity is not a style preference — it is a reliability property.

When you are unsure. Eliminate options that put untrusted text in a position of authority, that remove a human check from an irreversible action, or that discard the evidence needed to debug a later failure. Three eliminations usually leave the answer.

Building a revision set that actually helps

Most revision notes fail because they are copies of the source. Instead, make your notes answer-shaped. For each chapter, write five questions in your own words, then answer them without looking. Keep the ones you missed. After three passes, the surviving pile is your real revision set — small, personal, and high yield.

A second useful artefact is a decision table with three columns: situation, default choice, reason to override. For example: "Task path is fixed and audited → fixed workflow → override only if steps genuinely vary per request." Fifteen such rows will cover most of the architecture domain. A third artefact is a failure catalogue: failure name, detection signal, containment, and the trace field that proves it happened.

Exercise 49.2 — Override table. Create twelve rows in the situation/default/override format. Draw from architecture, tool design, prompting, and context. Then swap a colleague's rows with your own and try to answer theirs.

The week before

  • Stop learning new frameworks. Consolidate what you already know.
  • Re-run the two practice papers, but only the questions you previously missed.
  • Rebuild one small system from memory: a loop, a tool, a validation gate, and a handoff.
  • Read your failure catalogue aloud. If you cannot explain a row, you do not know it.
  • Sleep properly for two nights before the exam. Recall is state-dependent, and fatigue destroys scenario reasoning faster than it destroys factual recall.

On the day

Read every stem twice before looking at options. Underline the constraint that changes the answer — "irreversible", "untrusted", "must survive a restart", "cannot be observed". Manage time in blocks: answer everything you can in the first pass, mark uncertain items, and return to them. Do not let one hard question consume the budget for three easy ones.

If a question seems to describe two correct actions, ask which one addresses the root problem rather than the symptom. Adding a retry to a non-idempotent tool treats a symptom; making the tool idempotent addresses the root. Tightening a prompt treats a symptom; enforcing a schema addresses the root. This distinction recurs throughout the exam.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is a cold self-assessment more useful than re-reading highlights?
  2. Which two domains most often overlap in handoff questions?
  3. State the four steps of the scenario-answer method.
  4. Why should revision notes be written as questions rather than summaries?
  5. Give two examples of an answer that treats a symptom instead of a root cause.

Summary

  • Diagnose weak domains before planning study time; allocate effort by evidence, not comfort.
  • Follow a four-week sequence from architecture through context, with practice at every stage.
  • Answer scenarios by naming the failure, locating the owning boundary, choosing the cheapest effective control, and preserving observability.
  • Build a personal question set, a decision table, and a failure catalogue rather than copying notes.
  • In the final week, consolidate, re-answer misses, and protect sleep.

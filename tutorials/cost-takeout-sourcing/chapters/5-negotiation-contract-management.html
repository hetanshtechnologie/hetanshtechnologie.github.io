5. Negotiation & Contract Management

Capturing the Value

Negotiation as a Discipline

Negotiation converts sourcing leverage into contract value. Strong negotiators prepare, not persuade. They enter with a clear target, a walk-away point, a tradeable concessions list, and a fact base that justifies every ask.

Key Idea: The goal is not to "win" against the supplier. It is to reach an agreement that is value-accretive for the client while remaining commercially sustainable for the supplier.

Preparing the Negotiation

  • Target price — based on should-cost, benchmarks, or market data, not a round number.
  • BATNA — best alternative to negotiated agreement (other bidders, incumbent, insourcing).
  • Tradeables — volume commitments, longer terms, payment terms, scope, specifications, indexation.
  • Bundling — combining categories or sites to increase leverage.
  • Roles — lead negotiator, technical expert, commercial analyst, notetaker.
  • Plan B — walk-away points and response to supplier counterarguments.

Cost Modeling in Negotiation

Should-cost modeling estimates what a product or service genuinely costs to deliver, giving you the negotiating floor. For a manufactured item:

Should-Cost = Raw Material Cost
           + Direct Labor Cost
           + Manufacturing Overhead (allocated)
           + SG&A (allocated)
           + Profit Margin
           + Logistics & Duties

Even a rough should-cost exposes overpriced bids and anchors the discussion away from "your budget is too low."

Key Contractual Terms

  • Scope, specifications, and service levels (SLA / KPI)
  • Pricing, payment terms, invoicing, and GST treatment
  • Warranties, quality, and acceptance criteria
  • Liability, indemnities, and insurance
  • Intellectual property and confidentiality
  • Force majeure and business continuity
  • Term, termination, and exit rights
  • Dispute resolution and governing law
  • Change control and continuous improvement commitments

Contract Lifecycle Management

Signing is the beginning. Contract management includes maintaining a contract register, tracking renewal and termination dates, monitoring compliance with negotiated terms, managing amendments and change orders, and feeding contract data back into sourcing decisions. Missed renewals are a classic source of silent cost leakage.

Real-World Example: During negotiation of a 3-year facilities services contract, the team uncovers that the supplier prices include a 10% "management fee" on subcontracted work. They use should-cost to break the fee structure into transparent components, agree a cap, and add an annual efficiency review clause — capturing an additional 6% over the term.

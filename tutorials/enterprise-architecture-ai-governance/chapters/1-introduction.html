1. Introduction to Enterprise Architecture

Chapter 1

What is Enterprise Architecture?

Enterprise Architecture (EA) is the discipline of understanding and structuring an organization — its business capabilities, information, applications, technology, and the people who run them — as a coherent whole. It answers three questions that most organizations struggle with: where are we today, where do we need to go, and how do we get there.

EA is not a shelf of diagrams. It is a working management practice that links business strategy to execution. It translates executive intent into a structured view of the enterprise so that decisions about technology, investment, and change are made consistently rather than in silos.

Why Enterprise Architecture Matters in Modern IT

Modern IT environments are more complex than ever. Consider what a single enterprise now operates:

  • Hybrid and multi-cloud platforms spanning on-premises data centers, public clouds, and SaaS vendors.
  • Thousands of applications of varying age, quality, and duplication.
  • Data estates spread across warehouses, lakes, and lakehouses with inconsistent definitions.
  • Security and compliance pressure from regulators, auditors, and customers.
  • Rapidly evolving AI — including generative AI — that raises new architectural and governance questions.

Without architecture, this complexity creates duplication, technical debt, and risk. With architecture, it becomes a managed portfolio: choices are made deliberately, dependencies are understood, and change can be planned and sequenced.

Mental model: Think of EA as the "master plan" for the enterprise. A city grows badly without a plan — roads built ad-hoc, utilities tangled, expansion blocked. With a plan, growth is deliberate and infrastructure supports the future. EA plays the same role for the digital enterprise.

Architecture Domains

Enterprise architecture is commonly divided into four connected domains:

DomainWhat It CoversKey Question
Business architectureStrategy, capabilities, processes, organizationWhat does the business do, and how?
Data architectureData assets, models, flows, governanceWhat data do we have, and where?
Application architectureApplications, integrations, servicesWhat systems deliver the capabilities?
Technology architectureInfrastructure, platforms, networks, cloudWhat technology runs the applications?

The four domains must be consistent with each other. A business change usually ripples through all of them — for example, launching an AI-powered service touches business capability (new offering), data (training and inference data), applications (new services and APIs), and technology (GPU/cloud capacity).

Frameworks: Why TOGAF and Others Exist

Architecture is complex enough that frameworks emerged to structure the practice. A framework provides a common language, a method, and a set of models. The most widely adopted is TOGAF (The Open Group Architecture Framework).

TOGAF provides two things of enduring value:

  • The ADM (Architecture Development Method): a step-by-step method for developing architecture — from architecture vision, through business/data/application/technology architecture, opportunities and solutions, to migration planning and implementation governance.
  • The Architecture Content Framework: a consistent way to describe deliverables, artifacts, and building blocks so that different teams produce comparable architecture.
Reality check: No organization "does TOGAF" literally end-to-end. Mature teams adopt the parts that fit: the ADM's discipline of moving from current-state through target-state to roadmap, the vocabulary, and the governance habits. Treat TOGAF as a toolkit, not a prescription.

Aligning Technology Investments with Business Strategy

The core value proposition of EA is alignment. Technology spending is wasted when it is disconnected from strategy — systems that support processes the business no longer runs, platforms that duplicate each other, and innovations that never connect to revenue. Architecture creates alignment in concrete ways:

  • Capability-based planning: investment is linked to the business capabilities that create value, not to departments or projects.
  • Portfolio management: applications, data, and infrastructure are treated as portfolios with rationalization decisions (keep, invest, consolidate, retire).
  • Reference architectures: approved patterns for common needs (e.g., how AI services are integrated) reduce duplication and risk.
  • Investment sequencing: the target architecture defines what to build first, so that each step unlocks the next.
  • Benefits tracking: architecture decisions are tied to measurable outcomes, so the value is visible.
Key principle: Architecture must be the bridge between "what business wants" and "what IT builds". If the architecture function only produces diagrams, it has no influence. If it makes decisions and is accountable for outcomes, it has value.

The Growing Role of AI Governance in Digital Transformation

Digital transformation now runs on data and increasingly on AI. That makes AI governance — the structures, policies, and controls that ensure AI is used responsibly, legally, and safely — a core part of enterprise architecture, not a side concern.

AI governance enters the architecture conversation in several places:

  • Decisioning: who decides whether an AI use case is acceptable, and under what conditions.
  • Data governance: AI is only as good as its data; governance covers quality, privacy, and lineage.
  • Model and prompt lifecycle: models and prompts must be versioned, tested, deployed, and monitored like any enterprise asset.
  • Risk and compliance: regulations such as the EU AI Act, GDPR, and sector rules impose obligations on AI use.
  • Responsible AI: fairness, transparency, human oversight, and contestability are architectural requirements, not afterthoughts.

An architecture that ignores AI governance will find AI initiatives stalling — blocked by legal, resisted by customers, or causing incidents. Architecture that embeds governance from the start makes AI adoption a managed, repeatable capability.

Architecture as a Continuous Discipline

EA is not a one-time project. It is a rhythm of activities that keeps the enterprise coherent as it changes:

  • Maintain the baseline: keep current-state architecture current and trusted.
  • Shape the target: update the target-state as strategy changes (including AI strategy).
  • Govern the change: review major initiatives against architecture standards (architecture review boards — Chapter 5).
  • Plan the roadmap: sequence investments so change is affordable and low-risk.
  • Measure and adapt: track outcomes and adjust.

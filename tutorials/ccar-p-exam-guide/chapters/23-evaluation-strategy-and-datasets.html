Chapter 23 — Evaluation Strategy, Datasets, and Grading Methods

Learning objectives

  • Define evaluation objectives that reflect task success, safety, reliability, and user value.
  • Design representative datasets and test cases, including edge cases and adversarial or ambiguous inputs.
  • Choose grading methods—deterministic checks, human review, and model-assisted evaluation—appropriate to each criterion.
  • Interpret evaluation results with attention to coverage, bias, variance, and measurement limits.
  • Integrate evaluation into development and release decisions as a continuous practice.

Key concepts

Why evaluation strategy comes before metrics

Evaluation is how teams determine whether an AI-enabled system meets its intended purpose under realistic conditions. Without a clear strategy, teams often measure what is easy—such as average response length or superficial pass rates—rather than what matters. A strong evaluation strategy begins with objectives: what outcomes define success, what failures are unacceptable, and which trade-offs the system must balance. These objectives should align with user needs, risk profile, and operational constraints identified during design.

Different capabilities require different evidence. Factual question answering may emphasize groundedness and citation accuracy; structured extraction may emphasize schema compliance and field correctness; workflow assistance may emphasize task completion and safe escalation. A single aggregate score can hide critical weaknesses, so strategies typically use multiple criteria evaluated separately. This multidimensional view supports better decisions about readiness, routing, guardrails, and improvement priorities.

Evaluation is not a one-time gate. Models, prompts, retrieval corpora, tools, and user behavior change over time. Continuous evaluation—during development, before release, and in production monitoring—helps detect regressions and drift. The strategy should specify when evaluations run, what datasets they use, how results are compared, and who acts on findings.

Exam note: Match the evaluation method to the criterion. Use objective checks for format and deterministic rules, and careful human or rubric-based review for nuanced qualities like helpfulness or reasoning adequacy.

Building representative datasets and test cases

An evaluation is only as informative as its test cases. Representative datasets reflect the real distribution of inputs the system will face: common requests, important variants, difficult edge cases, incomplete information, and inputs that test boundaries. If datasets contain only easy or idealized examples, results will overestimate readiness. Coverage should be intentional, including categories that matter for risk and value such as high-impact scenarios, multilingual or domain-specific phrasing, and cases requiring refusal or escalation.

Each test case should define enough context to judge outcomes. This may include the input, relevant source materials or environment state, constraints, and expected properties of a correct response. For some tasks, exact expected outputs are appropriate (for example, classification labels or normalized fields). For others, correctness is better expressed as criteria: includes required elements, cites approved sources, avoids unsupported claims, or follows policy. Clear rubrics reduce ambiguity and improve consistency across reviewers and runs.

Datasets require maintenance. As products evolve, new failure modes and user intents emerge. A regression set of critical cases helps ensure fixes and improvements do not break essential behavior. Data governance matters as well: evaluation data may contain sensitive information and should be handled with appropriate access controls, minimization, and retention policies.

Grading methods and their limits

Grading methods translate responses into judgments. Deterministic checks evaluate objective properties: JSON validity, required fields present, allowed values, citation format, or forbidden strings. They are fast, consistent, and scalable but limited to what can be specified explicitly. Human review can assess nuance—helpfulness, reasoning quality, tone, and contextual appropriateness—but is costly, slower, and subject to variability unless guided by clear rubrics and calibration.

Model-assisted evaluation can scale certain judgments by applying rubrics to outputs, comparing responses, or checking claims against provided evidence. It is useful for large datasets but introduces its own error modes and biases; evaluators may be sensitive to phrasing, prefer verbosity, or miss domain-specific issues. When used, model-assisted grading should be validated against human judgments on samples, monitored for drift, and applied to criteria where it is reliable.

Many systems use layered grading: deterministic gates for must-pass constraints, plus rubric-based scoring for quality dimensions. Pass/fail thresholds should reflect risk. A minor style issue may be acceptable, while a policy violation or unsupported high-impact claim may be blocking. Reporting should separate dimensions so teams can see where performance is strong or weak.

Common mistake: Relying on a single automated score to represent overall quality. Aggregated metrics can mask critical failures in safety, grounding, or edge-case handling.

Designing trustworthy comparisons and experiments

Evaluation often informs choices: which prompt, model, retrieval setting, or workflow performs better. Trustworthy comparison requires controlling variables and using consistent datasets and criteria. Changing multiple components at once makes attribution difficult. When possible, compare variants on the same cases and report both overall and segmented results. Small differences may fall within noise, especially with limited datasets or stochastic outputs, so teams should consider variance and repeat runs where appropriate.

Segmentation reveals important patterns. A variant may improve average quality but regress on a critical subset, such as high-risk requests or a particular language or domain. Decision-making should weight results according to impact, not just averages. Evaluation should also consider operational factors—latency, cost, and failure rates—alongside output quality, since a “better” answer that is too slow or expensive may not be viable.

Documentation supports credibility and learning. Recording dataset composition, grading rubrics, evaluator configuration, run conditions, and known limitations helps others interpret results correctly. Transparent evaluation practices reduce the risk of overclaiming and make future comparisons meaningful.

Worked scenario

Context

A team is preparing to release an assistant that extracts key fields from incoming requests and generates a short summary for triage staff. Early testing shows high average accuracy on a small set of clean examples. Before release, stakeholders ask for confidence that the assistant will handle messy inputs, avoid inventing missing fields, and produce outputs that downstream tools can parse reliably.

Analysis

The existing evaluation is too narrow: clean examples do not represent real variability, and average accuracy hides specific failure types. The stated concerns map to distinct criteria—format compliance for downstream parsing, field correctness, and behavior under missing or ambiguous information. These require different grading approaches. Deterministic checks can validate structure and required fields; comparison against labeled references can assess extraction accuracy; rubric-based review can judge whether summaries are faithful and appropriately cautious when information is absent.

The dataset must expand to include typical cases, malformed or incomplete inputs, conflicting information, and previously observed problem patterns as regressions. Results should be segmented by input quality and request type to reveal where performance is weak. Release readiness should depend on must-pass constraints (parseability and no invented required fields) as well as acceptable quality levels.

Resolution

The team builds a representative evaluation set with labeled fields and rubrics for summary faithfulness and uncertainty handling. They implement deterministic schema and constraint checks as blocking gates, run extraction accuracy comparisons, and sample rubric-based reviews for nuanced cases. Evaluation reports show strong performance on clean inputs but lower reliability on incomplete requests, where the assistant sometimes infers missing values.

Based on segmented results, the team updates instructions and validation to require explicit “unknown” values when evidence is absent and adds targeted regression cases. Re-evaluation demonstrates improved compliance on must-pass criteria across segments. The release decision is made using multidimensional evidence rather than a single average score, increasing confidence in real-world behavior.

Common mistakes

  • Evaluating only easy, clean cases that do not reflect production variability or risk.
  • Using vague rubrics or unlabeled criteria, leading to inconsistent and uninformative judgments.
  • Applying model-assisted grading without validating its reliability for the task.
  • Relying on aggregate averages that hide regressions in critical segments.
  • Treating evaluation as a one-time event instead of maintaining regression and continuous checks.

Chapter summary

  • Evaluation strategy should be driven by clear objectives and multidimensional criteria aligned to value and risk.
  • Representative datasets include typical, edge, boundary, adversarial, and regression cases with well-defined expectations.
  • Deterministic, human, and model-assisted grading methods each have strengths and limits and should be matched to criteria.
  • Trustworthy comparisons require controlled conditions, segmentation, and awareness of variance and measurement limits.
  • Continuous, documented evaluation supports reliable release decisions and long-term quality management.

Checkpoint questions

  1. What is the best starting point for an evaluation strategy?

    A. Selecting a single easy-to-compute metric
    B. Defining success, unacceptable failures, and criteria aligned to task goals and risk
    C. Collecting only clean, ideal inputs
    D. Waiting until after release to measure outcomes

  2. Which grading approach is most appropriate for verifying JSON schema compliance?

    A. Unstructured human impression only
    B. Deterministic automated checks
    C. Ignoring format because content matters more
    D. Random sampling without rules

  3. Why should evaluation results be segmented?

    A. To hide weaknesses in averages
    B. To reveal performance differences across important case types or risk levels
    C. To eliminate the need for rubrics
    D. To guarantee statistical certainty in all cases

  4. What is a key risk of model-assisted grading?

    A. It can never be scaled
    B. It may introduce biases or errors and should be validated for the task
    C. It is identical to deterministic checking
    D. It removes the need for criteria

  5. Which dataset element best protects against reintroducing known issues?

    A. Only new random cases
    B. A maintained regression set of critical previously observed failures
    C. Removing difficult examples
    D. Single-example smoke tests only

  6. When comparing two system variants, what practice improves trust in the conclusion?

    A. Change prompts, model, and retrieval simultaneously
    B. Use the same dataset and criteria, control variables, and examine segmented outcomes
    C. Report only the best-looking average
    D. Avoid documenting run conditions

Answers and rationale

  1. Correct: B. Strategy begins with objectives and risk-aligned criteria. A, C, and D lead to incomplete or late insight.
  2. Correct: B. Schema compliance is objective and well suited to deterministic checks. A is inconsistent for this criterion, C ignores a requirement, and D is unreliable.
  3. Correct: B. Segmentation exposes strengths and weaknesses that averages can conceal. A misstates the purpose, C is false, and D overclaims.
  4. Correct: B. Model-based evaluators can be useful but have limitations and must be checked. A, C, and D are incorrect.
  5. Correct: B. Regression sets preserve coverage of known-critical behaviors. A, C, and D provide insufficient protection.
  6. Correct: B. Controlled, well-documented comparisons support valid interpretation. A, C, and D undermine attribution and transparency.

Chapter 2 — From Business Needs to Measurable Outcomes

Learning objectives

  • Convert ambiguous stakeholder requests into clear problem statements and outcome-oriented objectives.
  • Distinguish goals, requirements, constraints, assumptions, and non-goals in an AI initiative.
  • Define measurable success criteria and guardrails before selecting models, tools, or orchestration patterns.
  • Align technical decisions with the needs of users, operators, and governance stakeholders.
  • Use a structured framing workflow to reduce rework, scope creep, and misaligned implementation.

Key concepts

Turning broad requests into solvable problems

AI initiatives often begin with aspirational language: make service faster, summarize documents, automate decisions, or add an assistant to a product. These statements express intent but do not define a problem that can be designed, built, or evaluated. A problem statement makes the context explicit: who is affected, what task or decision is difficult today, what outcome should improve, under which conditions the solution must operate, and how improvement will be recognized.

Specificity changes design choices. “Automate report writing” is open-ended and invites uncontrolled scope. “Help operations analysts produce weekly incident summaries from approved logs within 30 minutes, with traceable sources and no exposure of restricted fields” immediately implies requirements for data access, grounding, privacy, latency, and auditability. It also clarifies what is not being attempted, which protects the team from building a general solution to a narrow need.

Clarity does not require complete certainty at the start. Early framing can include open questions, but those questions must be visible and assigned for resolution. Hidden ambiguity tends to reappear later as architectural churn, inconsistent expectations, or failed evaluations.

Exam note: When a scenario presents a vague request, the strongest first step is usually to clarify objectives, users, constraints, and success criteria rather than to select technology immediately.

Goals, requirements, constraints, assumptions, and non-goals

Precise terms prevent teams from talking past one another. These concepts are related but serve different roles in framing and decision-making.

Term Meaning Example Design impact
Goal The outcome the initiative seeks to achieve Reduce time to resolve routine inquiries Sets direction and value hypothesis
Requirement A capability or condition the solution must satisfy Provide answers with citations to approved sources Drives features, interfaces, and acceptance criteria
Constraint A limit that cannot be violated Restricted data must remain within an approved boundary Eliminates unsuitable options
Assumption Something treated as true but not yet verified Source documents are updated weekly Creates risk if false and must be validated
Non-goal Work explicitly out of scope for the current effort Automated final decisions for high-risk cases Prevents scope creep and unrealistic expectations

Confusing a preference with a constraint leads to poor trade-offs. “Responses should be fast” is a preference until a threshold is defined; “interactive responses must return within a fixed time budget” is a constraint. Treating an assumption as fact is equally risky. If a design assumes clean, current, and accessible data but the reality differs, grounding quality and user trust will degrade regardless of model capability.

Non-goals deserve special attention in AI work because stakeholders may infer broad autonomy from narrow capabilities. Stating what the system will not do—such as taking irreversible actions without approval or handling out-of-scope domains—protects users and focuses delivery.

Stakeholder alignment and competing priorities

Most AI solutions serve multiple stakeholders. End users want usefulness and speed. Product owners want measurable impact and adoption. Operations teams want stability and manageable cost. Security and compliance functions want controlled data handling and auditability. Domain experts want correctness in context. These priorities often compete, and architecture is where the balance becomes concrete.

A stakeholder view helps identify decision rights and unresolved tensions. Who approves risk acceptance? Who owns data quality? Who defines acceptable failure behavior? Who responds when the system behaves unexpectedly? Without answers, teams optimize for the loudest request or the easiest metric, and important safeguards may be treated as optional.

Stakeholder Primary concern Framing question Architectural implication
End user Task completion and trust Does this help me do the work correctly? Usability, grounding, clear uncertainty handling
Product owner Value and adoption Are we improving the target outcome? Measurable objectives, feedback mechanisms
Operations Reliability and cost Can we run this predictably within budget? Observability, capacity planning, fallback paths
Security/compliance Risk and accountability Is data handled and recorded appropriately? Access controls, logging, policy enforcement
Domain expert Correctness in context Are outputs valid for real cases? Representative evaluation, review workflows, escalation

Alignment does not require maximizing every priority. It requires making trade-offs explicit, documenting them, and tying them to the problem statement. In exam scenarios, answers that acknowledge multiple stakeholders and propose proportional controls are often stronger than answers that optimize a single dimension.

Defining outcomes and success criteria before building

Technology choices can only be judged against intended outcomes. Outcome-oriented framing starts with the change you want to see, then derives requirements and measures. Useful success criteria are specific, observable, and relevant. They include targets (what good looks like), guardrails (what must not worsen), and a baseline for comparison.

For an AI assistant, targets might include task success rate, grounded response rate, or time saved per case. Guardrails might include sensitive-data exposure incidents, incorrect escalation rate, or cost per successful task. Without these elements, teams default to subjective impressions or demo performance, which rarely predict production value.

Common mistake: Treating benchmark scores or isolated demonstrations as sufficient evidence that a solution meets domain-specific needs, constraints, and workflows.

It is helpful to separate leading and lagging indicators at the framing stage. Lagging indicators confirm realized impact, such as reduced handling time or fewer errors after adoption. Leading indicators provide earlier signals, such as retrieval relevance, correction frequency, or guardrail triggers. Both are needed to guide improvement and validate value.

A structured framing workflow

A lightweight workflow can convert ambiguous input into an actionable brief:

  1. Capture the request in stakeholder language. Preserve intent before reinterpretation.
  2. Draft a problem statement. Specify who, what, why, and under which conditions.
  3. Identify goals, constraints, assumptions, and non-goals. Make trade-offs and unknowns explicit.
  4. Define success criteria and guardrails. Include measurement approach and baseline needs.
  5. Record open questions and owners. Assign responsibility for resolving critical unknowns.
  6. Validate alignment. Confirm that the framed outcome is valuable and feasible to assess.

This workflow creates traceability. When a later design choice is questioned, the team can refer to agreed objectives and constraints rather than relying on memory or shifting preferences.

{
  "initiative": "policy_support_assistant",
  "problem_statement": "Service staff need faster, consistent answers to policy questions using approved sources.",
  "primary_users": ["frontline_service_staff"],
  "goals": [
    "reduce average time to find a policy answer",
    "improve consistency of responses"
  ],
  "constraints": [
    "use only approved knowledge sources",
    "no disclosure of restricted customer data",
    "escalate low-confidence or high-impact cases"
  ],
  "assumptions": [
    "approved sources are versioned and accessible"
  ],
  "non_goals": [
    "automated final decisions for disputes"
  ],
  "success_criteria": {
    "targets": ["task_success_rate >= 0.85", "median_response_time <= 5s"],
    "guardrails": ["sensitive_data_incidents == 0", "incorrect_escalation_rate <= 0.05"]
  }
}

Try it: Take a proposed AI feature and complete a similar brief. If constraints or guardrails are unclear, treat that as a signal to gather information before design decisions.

Worked scenario

Context

A regional operations group requests “an AI tool to handle vendor onboarding emails.” Staff currently read incoming messages, extract company details, check required documents, and request missing information. The process is slow and inconsistent. Leadership wants faster turnaround, while the compliance function warns that onboarding involves sensitive business data and that incorrect approvals create contractual risk. The request does not specify scope, data sources, success measures, or where human involvement is required.

Analysis

The request states a desired improvement but lacks a concrete problem statement and measurable outcomes. Automating message drafting alone would create visible activity without addressing the highest-risk steps: accurate extraction, document verification, and controlled status changes. The primary constraints involve data handling, correctness, and accountability. Key unknowns include which email types are in scope, which systems are authoritative, what defines a complete submission, and which actions may be automated versus recommended.

Assumptions about data quality and document availability must be surfaced. Non-goals should clarify that the system will not approve vendors autonomously or operate outside approved systems. Success criteria should balance efficiency gains with guardrails that prevent unauthorized access and improper actions.

Resolution

The architect facilitates a framing session and produces a brief focused on assisting staff to triage onboarding emails, extract required fields, and identify missing documents using approved systems, with human confirmation before any status change. Goals, constraints, assumptions, and non-goals are documented. Targets include reduced triage time and improved completeness of initial responses; guardrails include no unauthorized data access, no automated approval, and traceable recommendations.

Open questions—such as authoritative systems and document categories—are assigned owners with deadlines. With this framing, the team can evaluate solution options against explicit outcomes and risks rather than demonstration appeal, reducing the likelihood of rework and misalignment.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting a feature request as a problem statement without identifying the underlying task and outcome.
  • Failing to distinguish hard constraints from preferences, leading to non-viable designs.
  • Leaving assumptions implicit, especially about data quality, availability, or process consistency.
  • Omitting non-goals, which encourages scope creep and unrealistic expectations of autonomy.
  • Selecting technology before defining measurable success criteria and guardrails.

Chapter summary

  • Clear problem statements translate vague requests into solvable, outcome-focused work.
  • Goals, requirements, constraints, assumptions, and non-goals serve different roles and must be explicit.
  • Stakeholder alignment requires visible trade-offs and documented decision ownership.
  • Targets and guardrails defined before building enable meaningful evaluation and reduce demo-driven decisions.
  • A structured framing workflow creates traceability from business need to architectural choice.

Checkpoint questions

  1. What is the most effective first response to an ambiguous request to “add AI” to a workflow?

    A. Select a high-capability model to maximize quality
    B. Build a prototype before clarifying objectives
    C. Clarify the problem, users, constraints, and success criteria
    D. Automate every step to demonstrate impact

  2. Which item is best classified as a constraint rather than a goal?

    A. Improve user satisfaction with faster responses
    B. Reduce manual effort for routine tasks
    C. Prohibit processing restricted data outside an approved boundary
    D. Increase consistency across teams

  3. Why are non-goals important in framing an AI initiative?

    A. They list optional features to add if time permits
    B. They explicitly limit scope and set realistic expectations
    C. They replace the need for success criteria
    D. They eliminate stakeholder disagreement

  4. A team chooses a solution based only on benchmark performance without domain evaluation. What is the main risk?

    A. The solution may not meet real workflow, data, and constraint needs
    B. The solution will always exceed operating budgets
    C. The solution cannot be monitored in production
    D. The solution will automatically violate policy requirements

  5. Which practice best supports alignment among stakeholders with different priorities?

    A. Optimize for a single metric chosen by developers
    B. Document goals, constraints, trade-offs, and decision ownership
    C. Defer risk discussions until after deployment
    D. Assume speed is the universal priority

  6. What is the purpose of listing assumptions in a framing brief?

    A. To treat unverified beliefs as confirmed requirements
    B. To avoid stakeholder review
    C. To surface uncertainties that could affect design validity
    D. To finalize the architecture immediately

Answers and rationale

  1. Correct: C. Clarifying problem, users, constraints, and success criteria provides the foundation for sound design. A and B jump ahead without context. D risks unsafe or unnecessary automation.
  2. Correct: C. A prohibition on processing restricted data outside an approved boundary is a non-negotiable limit. A, B, and D describe desired outcomes rather than hard constraints.
  3. Correct: B. Non-goals define out-of-scope work, reducing creep and aligning expectations. A mischaracterizes them, C is false because criteria remain necessary, and D overstates their effect.
  4. Correct: A. Benchmarks may not reflect domain tasks, data conditions, or constraints. B, C, and D state outcomes that are not guaranteed by benchmark-only selection.
  5. Correct: B. Documented priorities, trade-offs, and ownership make decisions transparent and accountable. A, C, and D ignore or defer critical stakeholder concerns.
  6. Correct: C. Assumptions highlight uncertainties that require validation. A, B, and D contradict the purpose of making assumptions explicit.

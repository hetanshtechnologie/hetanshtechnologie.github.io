8. Emotional Intelligence

Emotional Intelligence

What is Emotional Intelligence?

Emotional Intelligence (EQ) is the ability to recognize, understand, manage, and reason with emotions — both your own and others'. Popularized by Daniel Goleman, EQ is often a better predictor of success than IQ.

The Four Components of EQ

  • Self-Awareness — Recognizing your emotions and their impact. Knowing your strengths, weaknesses, values, and triggers.
  • Self-Management — Controlling impulsive feelings and behaviors. Adapting to changing circumstances. Includes emotional regulation, transparency, and initiative.
  • Social Awareness (Empathy) — Understanding others' emotional states. Sensing group dynamics and power relationships. Service orientation toward others' needs.
  • Relationship Management — Influencing, coaching, and developing others. Managing conflict, building bonds, and fostering collaboration.

Self-Awareness Practices

  • Emotion Journaling — Write down what you felt, what triggered it, and how you responded
  • Mindfulness — Observe your thoughts and emotions without judgment
  • Feedback Seeking — Ask trusted people how they perceive you
  • Pause Before Reacting — Count to 10 before responding in emotionally charged situations

Empathy

Three types of empathy:

  • Cognitive Empathy — Understanding someone's perspective (perspective-taking)
  • Emotional Empathy — Feeling what someone else feels (emotional resonance)
  • Empathic Concern — Being moved to help (compassion)

Practice empathy by listening without judgment, asking how someone feels, and validating their emotions ("That sounds really difficult").

EQ in the Workplace

High EQ correlates with better leadership, teamwork, customer relationships, and stress management. Leaders with high EQ create psychologically safe environments where people feel valued and heard.

Practice Task: Keep an emotion journal for one week. Write down three moments each day where you felt a strong emotion — what triggered it, what you felt, and how you responded. At the end of the week, look for patterns. Also practice perspective-taking: in a disagreement, write down the other person's point of view as if you were them.

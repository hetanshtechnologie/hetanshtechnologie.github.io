4. Verbal Communication

Verbal Communication

Effective Speaking

Verbal communication is the use of words to convey information. Effective speaking involves choosing words carefully, organizing thoughts logically, and delivering with appropriate pace, volume, and tone.

  • Be Clear — Use simple language, avoid ambiguity
  • Be Structured — Organize your message: opening, body, conclusion
  • Be Concise — Respect others' time, get to the point
  • Be Confident — Use a steady voice, avoid fillers ("um," "like")

Conversational Skills

Good conversations follow a flow: initiate (greeting, small talk) → build (share ideas, ask questions) → conclude (summarize, close politely. Tips include asking open-ended questions, finding common ground, sharing the talk time, and reading social cues for when to move on.

Questioning Techniques

  • Open Questions — "What do you think about...?" encourages elaboration
  • Closed Questions — "Did you finish the report?" gets a specific answer
  • Probing Questions — "Can you tell me more about...?" digs deeper
  • Leading Questions — "Don't you think this is the best option?" influences answer
  • Reflective Questions — "So you're saying that...?" checks understanding

Giving a Speech or Presentation

Structure: tell them what you're going to say (opening), say it (body), tell them what you said (conclusion). Practice out loud, use notes not scripts, make eye contact, and pause for emphasis. Know your audience and tailor content accordingly.

Oracy & Rhetorical Techniques

Oracy is the ability to express yourself fluently and grammatically in speech. Rhetorical techniques like triples (rule of three), rhetorical questions, analogies, and contrast can make speech more persuasive and memorable.

Register in Language

Language register refers to the level of formality: frozen (legal documents), formal (presentations), consultative (professional conversations), casual (friends), and intimate (close relationships). Match your register to the context.

Practice Task: Record yourself speaking for 2 minutes on a topic you know well. Listen back and count your filler words ("um," "uh," "like"). Practice the same talk again, reducing fillers. Then practice asking open-ended questions in a conversation — try to ask at least 3 that begin with "What," "How," or "Why."

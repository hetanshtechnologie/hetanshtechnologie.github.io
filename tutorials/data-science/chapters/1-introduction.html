1. Introduction to Data Science

Chapter 1

What is Data Science?

Data science is the discipline of extracting insight and value from data through a combination of statistics, programming, domain knowledge, and communication. It turns raw data into decisions: predictions, recommendations, dashboards, and products.

Data science sits at the intersection of three skills:

  • Mathematics & Statistics — the theory of uncertainty, distributions, and inference.
  • Programming & Engineering — collecting, cleaning, and modeling data with code (mostly Python).
  • Domain & Business Knowledge — knowing which problems matter and what a good answer looks like.

That last piece is why data science is not just "statistics with Python." A model that predicts perfectly but answers the wrong question is worthless. The best data scientists spend as much time framing the problem as building the model.

The Data Science Workflow

Almost every project follows the same loop, known by names such as CRISP-DM (Cross Industry Standard Process for Data Mining):

  1. Understand the problem. What decision are we informing? Define success concretely (e.g., "reduce customer churn by 10%").
  2. Collect data. Identify sources, gather samples, and document provenance.
  3. Clean & prepare. Fix missing values, types, duplicates, and errors; engineer features.
  4. Explore & visualize. Generate hypotheses, find patterns, and surface data problems.
  5. Model. Choose algorithms, train, validate, and compare performance.
  6. Interpret & communicate. Translate results into decisions the business can act on.
  7. Deploy & monitor. Put the model in production and watch it drift.

You will go through these stages many times per project — the loop is the point, not the order.

The Data Science Lifecycle in a Company

Inside an organization, data science depends on a healthy data ecosystem:

  • Data producers — transaction systems, apps, IoT devices, third-party feeds.
  • Data platform — warehouses, lakes, and pipelines built by data engineers.
  • Data scientists — turn that data into models and analysis.
  • Consumers — product teams, executives, and external users who act on the output.
Good to know: most real-world projects fail not on modeling but on data quality, unclear objectives, or a mismatch between the model's output and the decision it should drive.

13. Time Series Analysis

Chapter 13

What Makes Time Series Special

Time series data has order: rows are not exchangeable. Neighboring observations are correlated, so the tools from Chapter 10 (which assume independence) do not apply directly. Time series work powers forecasting — demand, revenue, load, prices — and anomaly detection.

Components of a Time Series

  • Trend — long-term direction (up, down, flat).
  • Seasonality — repeating pattern with fixed period (daily, weekly, yearly, holiday effects).
  • Cyclicality — longer, irregular cycles (economic booms/busts).
  • Noise — irregular, unpredictable movement.

Decompose any series with statsmodels:

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose

df = df.set_index("date").sort_index()
result = seasonal_decompose(df["revenue"], model="additive", period=12)
result.plot()
plt.show()

Stationarity

Most classical models assume stationarity: constant mean and variance over time (no trend, no changing seasonality).

from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

stat, p_value = adfuller(df["revenue"])[:2]
print(p_value)   # p < 0.05 -> stationary

Non-stationary? Common fixes:

  • Differencing: df["revenue"].diff() removes trend; seasonal differencing removes seasonality.
  • Log transform stabilizes variance.
  • Model the components explicitly instead (Prophet, or include trend/season dummies).

Autocorrelation

Check how a series correlates with its own past. ACF shows correlation at each lag; PACF shows the partial correlation after removing intermediate lags. These plots guide ARIMA orders.

from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf

plot_acf(df["revenue"].diff().dropna(), lags=24)
plot_pacf(df["revenue"].diff().dropna(), lags=24)
plt.show()

ARIMA

ARIMA combines AR (autoregression on past values), I (differencing to reach stationarity), and MA (moving average of past errors): ARIMA(p, d, q).

from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

model = ARIMA(df["revenue"], order=(1, 1, 1))
fit = model.fit()
print(fit.summary())

# Forecast the next 12 periods
forecast = fit.forecast(steps=12)
print(forecast)

Choosing p, d, q: use auto_arima (pmdarima) or ACF/PACF plots plus AIC comparison of a small grid.

Never train/test randomly on time series. Split chronologically: train on the past, test on the future (e.g., last 20% of dates). A random split leaks the future into training and flatters your scores.

Holidays & Exogenous Variables

Real demand is driven by calendars (Diwali, Christmas, fiscal year-end). Two ways to handle it:

  • Add holiday/external regressors (weather, promotions, events) to a model that supports them.
  • Use a model designed for this, such as Prophet or SARIMAX with exogenous regressors.
# Prophet: trend + seasonality + holidays handled automatically
# pip install prophet
from prophet import Prophet

forecast_df = df.reset_index().rename(columns={"date": "ds", "revenue": "y"})
m = Prophet(seasonality_mode="multiplicative",
            yearly_seasonality=True, weekly_seasonality=True)
m.add_country_holidays(country_name="IN")
m.fit(forecast_df)
future = m.make_future_dataframe(periods=90)
forecast = m.predict(future)
m.plot(forecast)

Evaluating Forecasts

Report error relative to the scale of the data:

  • MAE — average error in units.
  • MAPE — average percentage error (be careful with near-zero values).
  • RMSE — penalizes large errors.
  • MASE — compares against a naive baseline; < 1 means better than "predict last value."
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

y_true, y_pred = test["revenue"], test["forecast"]
print("MAE:", mean_absolute_error(y_true, y_pred))

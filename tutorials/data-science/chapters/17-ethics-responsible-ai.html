17. Ethics, Bias & Responsible AI

Chapter 17

Why Ethics is a Technical Skill

Models are not neutral. They are trained on the world as it is — including its biases — and then applied at scale, amplifying both strengths and harms. Responsible data science treats fairness, privacy, transparency, and accountability as engineering requirements, not afterthoughts.

Where Bias Enters

  • In the data: historical discrimination, biased sampling, small or missing groups, proxy variables (e.g., postal code standing in for race).
  • In the label: the target itself may encode bias — e.g., "employee performance" scores influenced by the boss's bias.
  • In the model: optimization can sharpen disparities even when the goal seems neutral.
  • In deployment: who uses the model, on whom, with what recourse, and how errors fall.
Proxy problem: even if you remove a protected attribute (race, gender), correlated features like income, address, or name can reintroduce it. Removing the column is not removing the bias — audit the model's behavior across groups, not just the inputs.

Fairness Metrics

There is no single "fairness." Different definitions can conflict, so you must choose and defend one per context:

MetricQuestion it answers
Demographic parityAre positive outcomes equally likely across groups?
Equalized oddsAre error rates equal across groups (given true label)?
Equal opportunityIs recall equal across groups?
CalibrationDo predicted probabilities mean the same across groups?
# Example: compare recall across groups
for group in df["group"].unique():
    mask = (df["group"] == group) & (df["y_true"] == 1)
    recall = df.loc[mask, "y_pred"].mean()
    print(group, round(recall, 3))

Privacy & Data Protection

  • Minimize: collect and keep only what is needed.
  • Anonymize: remove identifiers; beware that combining datasets can re-identify people.
  • Consent & purpose: use data for what people were told it would be used for.
  • Security: encrypt at rest and in transit, restrict access, and never store secrets in code.
  • Regulations: know the rules that apply to you — GDPR (EU), CCPA (California), and India's DPDP Act 2023.

Transparency & Explainability

Stakeholders and regulators need to understand what a model does and why it reached a decision. Techniques:

  • SHAP / permutation importance — which features drove each prediction (Chapter 11).
  • Model cards — a one-page spec: purpose, data, limitations, evaluation, intended and prohibited uses.
  • Documentation of data lineage — where data came from, how it was cleaned (Chapter 3).
  • Human review for high-stakes — hiring, lending, and healthcare decisions need a human in the loop with clear recourse.

Accountability & Governance

Someone must own the model's behavior. Good governance looks like:

  • A named owner for every model in production.
  • Review gates before deployment (ethics + technical sign-off).
  • Incident response for harmful outputs.
  • Monitoring bias metrics after launch, not just accuracy (Chapters 15 and 16).
  • Clear paths to challenge or override a model's decision.

Practical Checklist Before Shipping

  1. List the people affected and the stakes if the model is wrong.
  2. Check representation and performance across groups.
  3. Document data provenance and limitations.
  4. Define who is accountable and how decisions can be challenged.
  5. Plan monitoring for drift, bias, and performance.
  6. Write the model card.
Practice Task: For your best model, write a model card with: purpose, training data, evaluation metrics (overall and per group), known limitations, intended uses, and prohibited uses. Then audit the model across at least two subgroups of your data (e.g., by city or plan) and report at least one fairness metric per group. State explicitly what your model should NOT be used for.

7. Statistics & Probability

Chapter 7

The Two Branches of Statistics

  • Descriptive statistics — summarizing what you observed (means, spreads, distributions). You used these in Chapter 5.
  • Inferential statistics — using a sample to say something about the larger population, with a quantified level of uncertainty. This is the theory under every A/B test and every model evaluation.

Descriptive Statistics in Practice

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

spend = np.array([120, 250, 480, 180, 610, 220, 90, 330])

print("mean   :", np.mean(spend))
print("median :", np.median(spend))
print("std    :", np.std(spend, ddof=1))   # sample std
print("IQR    :", np.percentile(spend, 75) - np.percentile(spend, 25))
print("skew   :", stats.skew(spend))
print("kurt   :", stats.kurtosis(spend))

Bayes' rule is the workhorse of inference:

P(A|B) = P(B|A) * P(A) / P(B)

Example: a test for a disease is 99% accurate. 1% of people have the disease. If you test positive, what is the chance you have it?

P(disease)      = 0.01
P(pos | disease) = 0.99
P(pos | healthy) = 0.01
P(pos) = 0.99*0.01 + 0.01*0.99 = 0.0198
P(disease | pos) = 0.99*0.01 / 0.0198 = 0.5   # only 50%!
Lesson: a "99% accurate" test on a rare condition gives a positive result that is still only 50% likely to be real. Base rates matter — this confusion causes real-world errors in medicine, hiring, and fraud detection.

Sampling & the Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem says: if you take many samples from a population (any distribution, finite variance) and compute their means, the distribution of those sample means approaches a normal distribution as sample size grows. This is why we can compute confidence intervals and p-values with normal-based formulas even when the underlying data is skewed.

# Demonstrate the CLT: means of samples from an exponential
sample_means = [np.mean(np.random.exponential(scale=2, size=50))
                for _ in range(5000)]
print(np.mean(sample_means), np.std(sample_means, ddof=1))
# ~2.0 and ~0.28 = sigma/sqrt(n) = 2/sqrt(50)

Standard Error & Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval for a mean is sample_mean ± z * SE, where SE = sample_std / sqrt(n).

data = np.random.normal(loc=100, scale=15, size=400)
n = len(data)
se = np.std(data, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.975)          # 1.96 for a 95% CI
lo, hi = np.mean(data) - z*se, np.mean(data) + z*se
print(f"95% CI: ({lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f})")
Common misinterpretation: a 95% confidence interval is not "95% chance the true value is inside." It means: if you repeated the experiment many times, about 95% of the intervals would contain the true value. It is a statement about the procedure, not about any single interval.

Choosing Between Parametric and Non-Parametric

Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) assume distributions (usually normality) and use their parameters. Non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, bootstrap) make weaker assumptions and are safer for small or skewed samples — at the cost of a little power. With large samples, the difference usually disappears.

11. Threat Intelligence Integration

Chapter 11

What is Threat Intelligence?

Threat intelligence (TI) is evidence-based knowledge about adversaries — their capabilities, infrastructure, and methods — that helps an organization detect, prioritize, and respond to threats. In practice, most SOCs use two kinds:

  • Strategic intelligence: high-level trends, adversary profiles, industry risk (for leadership and planning).
  • Tactical / operational intelligence: specific indicators of compromise (IOCs), tools, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) that feed detections and playbooks.

For SIEM and SOAR, the tactical layer matters most: it is what gets queried during enrichment and matched against events.

Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

IOCs are the concrete data points used to detect compromise:

IOC TypeExampleHow It's Used
IP address203.0.113.45Block on firewall, match in SIEM connection logs
Domain / hostnameevil-domain.exampleBlock on DNS/proxy, match in email logs
URLhttps://evil-domain.example/rzBlock on web filter, scan in phishing
File hash (MD5/SHA1/SHA256)e3b0c442... (SHA256)Reputation check, block in EDR/AV
Email address / senderbad@evil.exampleQuarantine in email gateway
Mutual TLS certificateSHA256 fingerprintDetect C2 infrastructure reuse
Important nuance: An IP can change hands or be legitimate infrastructure shared with benign users. Hashes expire quickly as malware changes. Domains are dynamic (DGA, fast-flux). Good intelligence programs treat every IOC as a temporal, confidence-weighted fact, not an eternal truth.

STIX and TAXII: The Standard Formats

To share intelligence between tools and organizations, the industry standardized on:

  • STIX (Structured Threat Information Expression): a JSON format for representing threat data — indicators, observables, malware, campaigns, threat actors, relationships. Example: "This indicator (IP) is malicious, is part of campaign X, targeting finance sector."
  • TAXII (Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information): the transport protocol for exchanging STIX objects between TI servers and consumers.
// Simplified STIX 2.1 indicator (IP)
{
  "type": "indicator",
  "id": "indicator--8e2e2d2b-17d4-4cbf-938f-98ee46b3cd3f",
  "name": "Malicious IP",
  "pattern": "[ipv4-addr:value = '203.0.113.45']",
  "valid_from": "2026-01-01T00:00:00Z",
  "labels": ["malicious-activity"],
  "modified": "2026-06-01T00:00:00Z"
}

Platforms like MISP, OpenCTI, and vendor feeds expose STIX/TAXII, letting a SIEM ingest indicators and a SOAR query them during playbooks.

Intelligence Feeds and Sources

  • Open-source: AlienVault OTX, IBM X-Force Exchange, VirusTotal (partially), AbuseIPDB, free MISP instances.
  • Commercial: Recorded Future, CrowdStrike Intel, Mandiant, Palo Alto Unit 42, Anomali, ThreatConnect.
  • ISAC/ISAO: sector-specific sharing groups (finance, healthcare, energy) — often the most relevant to your industry.
  • Internal: intelligence your own IR team generates from past incidents — often the most valuable of all.

Intelligence-Driven Detection in the SIEM

In the SIEM, threat intelligence powers two things:

  1. Enrichment at query time: each event's IPs, domains, and hashes are checked against TI. Alerts get a reputation field: "malicious", "suspicious", "unknown".
  2. Indicator matching rules: a rule that flags "any event touching a known-malicious IOC". These are simple but noisy unless confidence and time-window are managed.
# KQL concept: match connection events against an ingested TI watchlist
let malicious_ips = ThreatIntelIndicator
    | where isActive
    | where IndicatorType == "Ipv4Address"
    | project IpAddress;
CommonSecurityLog
| where TimeGenerated > ago(1h)
| where SourceIP in (malicious_ips) or DestinationIP in (malicious_ips)
| project TimeGenerated, SourceIP, DestinationIP, DeviceAction

Intelligence Enrichment in the SOAR

During playbooks, the SOAR queries TI services to answer questions like:

  • "Is this hash known-malicious? With what confidence?"
  • "Who owns this IP? Is it a known C2 server?"
  • "Was this domain registered recently? Does it resemble known phishing infrastructure?"
  • "Has this file family been seen attacking our sector before?"

Enrichment results drive decisions: escalate, block, or close. Without enrichment, automation is blind; with it, automation has the context to act correctly.

Intelligence Lifecycle and Hygiene

Poorly managed intelligence is worse than none — stale or wrong IOCs create false positives and missed detections. Run a lifecycle:

  1. Collect: pull from sources on a schedule via TAXII/API.
  2. Process: normalize, deduplicate, dedupe across feeds.
  3. Validate & score: apply confidence, relevance, and lifetime per IOC.
  4. Deploy: push active indicators to SIEM watchlists, firewalls, email gateways, EDR.
  5. Monitor & retire: expire indicators on their valid-to date; review hits for accuracy.
Common Failure: Organizations ingest 10M indicators, auto-block all of them, and break the internet for their users — or, conversely, disable intelligence after too many false positives. Quality over quantity: curate, validate, and set strict confidence thresholds before automation acts on intel.

The Intelligence-Feedback Loop

The best intelligence programs are bidirectional:

  • Detections based on intel generate findings.
  • Confirmed IOCs from incidents are published back to the TI platform and sector-sharing groups.
  • Over time, the organization both consumes and produces intelligence, improving detection for the whole community.

Threat Hunting with Intelligence

Intelligence also powers proactive hunting: analysts use recent intel ("this group now uses PowerShell obfuscation") to design hunts across historical data, looking for missed compromises before they escalate.

Practice Task: Sign up for a free TI source (OTX or AbuseIPDB) and a free malware-reputation API. Build a small enrichment "utility" in your head or on paper: for a given file hash and IP, list the fields you would query, the thresholds you would set for "malicious" vs "unknown", and the automation decisions each outcome would trigger. Then write a STIX 2.1 indicator for a hypothetical malicious domain with proper confidence and expiry.

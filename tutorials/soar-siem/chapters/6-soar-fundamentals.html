6. SOAR Fundamentals

Chapter 6

What is SOAR?

SOAR stands for Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response. Coined by Gartner, it describes platforms that help security teams coordinate tools and processes, automate repetitive tasks, and execute incident response faster and more consistently.

The three words describe three distinct capabilities that a SOAR platform combines:

CapabilityWhat it doesExample
OrchestrationConnects and coordinates multiple security tools via integrations and APIsSIEM hands an alert to a SOAR, which queries an EDR and a threat-intel feed
AutomationExecutes routine, predefined actions without human interventionAutomatically quarantine a file hash on every endpoint
ResponseManages the end-to-end incident workflow: cases, tasks, evidence, approvals, documentationCreate a case, assign it to an analyst, track MTTR, close with a full timeline
Key Idea: A SOAR is not just "automation." Automation without orchestration is a script that can't reach the tools. Orchestration without automation is a fancy integration framework. Response without either is a ticketing system. The value is the combination, plus the governance and audit trail that make it trustworthy.

The Problem SOAR Solves

Before SOAR, incident response was dominated by manual, repetitive, inconsistent work:

  • Analysts spent 30-60 minutes per alert gathering context across 5-10 tools.
  • Playbooks existed as PDFs and wiki pages — followed differently by every analyst.
  • Every response was a snowflake: no standard evidence, no standard actions, no audit trail.
  • Staff turnover and alert fatigue meant knowledge lived in individual heads.
  • MTTR was measured in hours or days because the bottleneck was human.

SOAR converts this into: alerts flow in from SIEM, automated playbooks gather context in seconds, routine cases resolve automatically, hard cases reach humans with a prepopulated case, and every step is logged and repeatable.

Brief History of SOAR

  • Early 2010s: Emerged from Security Operations Analytics (SOA) and incident response platforms (IRP). PhishMe/COFENSE brought phishing automation to market.
  • 2017: Gartner coins the term SOAR, consolidating Orchestration, Automation, and Response into one category.
  • 2019-2021: Splunk acquires Phantom; Palo Alto Networks matures Demisto → Cortex XSOAR; Microsoft builds automation into Sentinel; open-source options (Shuffle, TheHive/Cortex) appear.
  • 2022+: SOAR capabilities get embedded into SIEM/XDR platforms ("integrated SOAR"), and AI-assisted playbook generation emerges.

Orchestration, Automation, and Response in Detail

Orchestration

Orchestration is about connection. A SOAR platform has a library of connectors — prebuilt integrations for SIEMs, EDRs, firewalls, ticketing systems, threat-intel feeds, identity providers, email gateways, and collaboration tools. Orchestration lets one playbook chain actions across tools: read an alert from the SIEM, check a hash with VirusTotal, look up the asset in CMDB, block the IP on the firewall, and log the case in Jira.

Automation

Automation is about doing. Automated steps are actions a machine executes deterministically: queries, enrichment calls, containment actions, notifications. Automation is graded by coverage (what fraction of alerts can be resolved without a human) and confidence (how reliably the automation acts correctly).

Response (Case Management)

Response is about managing the work. SOAR platforms include a case-management layer: incidents/cases with fields, severity, ownership, task lists, evidence attachment, timelines, and reports. This is what turns "a bunch of automated actions" into a defensible, auditable incident record — essential for legal, compliance, and continuous improvement.

SOAR Business Value

  • Faster response: MTTR drops from hours/days to minutes for automated playbooks.
  • Consistency: the same incident is handled the same way every time, by any analyst.
  • Scale: handle 10x alert volume with the same team size.
  • Staff retention: analysts focus on interesting investigations, not repetitive clicks.
  • Audit and compliance: every action is logged with timestamps and evidence.
  • Knowledge capture: playbooks encode institutional knowledge that survives turnover.

SOAR vs RPA vs Scripts

It is easy to confuse SOAR with related automation approaches:

ApproachFocusKey difference from SOAR
Scripts (Python/PowerShell)Automate a single taskNo orchestration, case management, or governance across tools
RPA (Robotic Process Automation)Automate UI clicks in business appsGeneric business automation; SOAR is security-workflow-specific
CI/CD pipelinesBuild and deploy softwareNot designed for alert-driven, human-in-the-loop security workflows
Workflow tools (Zapier, Logic Apps)Connect apps genericallySOAR adds security context: severity, IOCs, cases, evidence, threat intel
SOAREnd-to-end security responsePurpose-built: SIEM ingestion, playbooks, cases, approvals, metrics

SOAR and the Human Element

The best SOAR programs use a "human-in-the-loop" philosophy: automate what is safe and repeatable, and escalate what needs judgment. Human approval gates are built into playbooks for risky actions (isolating a production server, changing firewall rules). The platform's job is to present a human with enough context to approve or deny in seconds, not to replace human judgment.

Anti-Pattern: "Automate everything." Unreviewed automation can cause massive self-inflicted damage — quarantining the domain controller, blocking the CEO's IP, or disabling critical services. Design each playbook with blast-radius analysis and approval gates where appropriate.

SOAR Adoption Pitfalls

  • Tool-first, process-later: buying a SOAR before defining processes guarantees confusion.
  • Playbook sprawl: hundreds of untested playbooks nobody trusts.
  • Automation theater: "automation" that still requires an analyst to click through every step.
  • Bad data in, bad actions out: automation amplifies poor-quality alerts; fix detection first.
  • No metrics: if you can't measure MTTR improvement, you can't show value or iterate.
Analogy: SIEM tells you a fire exists. SOAR is the building's automated fire response: it alerts dispatch, closes fire doors, sends water to the right zone, and logs everything — while still calling a human firefighter for anything unusual. The firefighter does the judgment; the system does the speed.
Practice Task: Pick three alerts your SIEM (real or imagined) produces most often. For each, write a one-page "automation candidate" brief: (1) the decision path a good analyst follows, (2) which steps are safe to automate, (3) which steps need human approval, and (4) what data/tool integrations would be required. Estimate how many minutes per alert automation would save.

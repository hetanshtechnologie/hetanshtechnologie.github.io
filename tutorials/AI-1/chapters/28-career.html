28. Careers, Networking, and the AI Talent Market

The market for AI talent

Every company now wants AI capability, but the scarce skill is not prompt writing — it is turning a model into a reliable system. Teams need people who can design evaluations, secure tool access, control cost, and explain trade-offs to product and legal stakeholders. That is precisely the skill set this book has been building. If you can demonstrate it, you are speaking the language hiring teams are trying to learn.

The market is noisy. Job titles vary wildly, and "AI engineer" can mean anything from fine-tuning models to wiring an API into a chatbot. Focus on the underlying capabilities rather than titles. Read the responsibilities: do they mention evaluation, production reliability, RAG, tool integration, or infrastructure? Those are signals of real engineering work.

Build a portfolio that proves judgement

A list of certificates is weak evidence. A working system with a clear explanation of trade-offs is strong evidence. Build two or three projects that demonstrate different parts of the stack:

  • A RAG assistant with retrieval metrics, a no-answer path, and source citations.
  • An agent with tools and guardrails that shows a trace, an eval suite, and a cost budget.
  • A local or offline feature that works without a cloud call.

For each project, write a short README that explains the problem, the architecture, the evaluation approach, what failed, and what you would do next. Hiring teams care about how you think about failure. A project that documents its limitations is more convincing than one that claims perfection.

Show the eval, not just the demo. Include your eval harness and a few real failure cases. It demonstrates engineering maturity and gives interviewers something concrete to discuss.

Connecting with companies and recruiters

Opportunities rarely come from cold applications alone. A thoughtful conversation with a practitioner or recruiter is far more valuable. The key is to make it easy for them to say yes to a conversation: be specific, be brief, and lead with value.

  • Research the team and their actual product before reaching out.
  • Reference something concrete — a blog post, a talk, a repository — not a generic compliment.
  • State who you are, what you have built, and one relevant question.
  • Offer something: a short write-up, a demo link, a useful observation about their system.
  • Keep the first message under a hundred words.

Recruiters respond to clarity. Tell them the kind of role you want, the stack you know, and what would make a role interesting. A vague "open to opportunities" forces them to guess.

Talking with industry leaders

Senior engineers and leaders are often happy to discuss their work, but they are busy. Ask for fifteen minutes about a specific decision rather than general career advice. "How did your team decide between a graph-based orchestrator and an SDK approach?" is a question worth answering. "Can you tell me about your journey?" usually is not.

Bring your own observations. After reading about a company's system, share one thing you noticed and one question it raised. This turns a one-way request into a two-way exchange and makes you memorable for the right reasons.

Using events as a career lever

Conferences and workshops are unusually good networking environments because the shared context is already there. The AI-India event captured in this book is a perfect example: practitioners, engineers, and learners in the same room, discussing real production problems.

Make a plan for events: choose three sessions, prepare one question for each, and introduce yourself to two people with a specific reason. After the event, follow up within forty-eight hours with a short note referencing something you discussed. A handful of genuine follow-ups beats a hundred exchanged business cards.

The workshop sessions are especially valuable. Working through a hands-on lab alongside others creates a natural conversation and gives you something to show afterwards. Chapter 31 covers the LangGraph and TypeScript workshop from this event as a portfolio-ready exercise.

Interview preparation

Expect questions that test judgement rather than recall. Practice explaining:

  • When you would use a graph-based agent versus a simple prompt.
  • How you would know if your eval suite was lying to you.
  • What you would check when answers slowly got worse in production.
  • How you would stop an agent from misusing a tool.
  • How you would cut cost without degrading quality.

Each of these maps to a chapter in this book. Your answers should include a concrete example, a trade-off, and a measurement. That combination signals experience.

Continuous learning without burnout

The field moves fast, and trying to follow every release is exhausting. Build a stable mental model — which is what this book provides — and then track changes relative to it. When a new framework appears, ask which layer it occupies and what problem it solves. Most "new" things are familiar ideas in new packaging.

A sustainable rhythm is one small experiment a week and one deeper project a quarter. Write down what you learned. Public notes and small write-ups build a reputation over time far more reliably than sporadic bursts of intense study.

Case study: turning a capstone into an interview conversation

Consider a learner who has completed the evaluated RAG assistant from Chapter 31. Instead of listing "built a RAG chatbot" on a résumé, they prepare a short evidence package and use it to steer interviews toward judgement rather than trivia. The package has four artefacts.

The one-page architecture note

A single page shows the pipeline — hybrid retrieval, reranker, grounded generation, no-answer path — and names the three hardest decisions. One decision might be the abstention threshold: set too low, the assistant refuses valid questions; set too high, it fabricates. The learner records the chosen value and the eval result that justified it.

The eval report

A short table reports recall@5, groundedness, and abstention accuracy over a 120-question set. It also lists three real failures and their fixes. This is the artefact that changes the conversation, because it invites the interviewer to ask about measurement rather than recall frameworks.

metric              baseline   after fix
recall@5             0.71       0.86
groundedness         0.78       0.93
abstention accuracy  0.64       0.89
failures documented  0          3

The numbers matter less than how they were produced. The 120 questions are held out from the documents used to tune the retriever, so the scores are not a measure of memorisation. Each metric is reported with a confidence interval, because a two-point improvement on a small set may be noise. Being able to explain that distinction in an interview demonstrates more judgement than any impressive-sounding number, and it naturally leads to questions about how the learner detects overfitting to an eval set.

Reusing the package in outreach

The same package makes outreach specific. Instead of a generic note, the learner can write: "I built an evaluated RAG assistant with an explicit abstention path and a 120-question eval set; I noticed your team works on retrieval quality and had a question about how you calibrate judges." Because the claim is concrete and the question is specific, it is easy to answer. The capstone becomes a networking asset as well as an interview answer.

From claim to question

In the interview, the learner does not say "I know RAG". They say: "My first eval showed 78% groundedness; the biggest error was stale chunks surviving re-indexing. I added a version field and re-embedded only changed documents, which took it to 93%. Here is how I measured it." That statement contains a problem, a decision, and a measurement — exactly what hiring teams are probing for.

Being honest about limits

The learner also names what the project does not do: no multi-turn memory, no permission-aware retrieval yet. Volunteering limitations builds credibility and gives the interviewer more to discuss. A candidate who can criticise their own system sounds like an engineer, not a demo operator.

Rehearse the numbers, not a script. Know your baseline, your final score, and the one change that moved them. If you cannot explain a metric under a follow-up question, it is worse than not citing it at all. Practise answering "how do you know?" out loud before the interview.
Exercise 28.3 — Build your evidence pack. Choose one project and write the three lines you would say in an interview: the problem, the key decision, and the measurement that confirmed it. Then list one limitation you would volunteer.

Common pitfalls in career building

Career advice is easy to give and hard to apply, but a few mistakes recur often enough to be worth naming. Each has a concrete correction.

PitfallWhy it hurtsWhat to do instead
Collecting certificates as proof of skillThey show attendance, not judgement, and hiring teams have learned to discount them.Ship one system with an eval report and a documented failure.
Showing a demo with no evaluationAnyone can wire an API into a chat UI; the interesting question is whether it works reliably.Include your harness, metrics, and failure cases in the README.
Generic outreach messages"Open to opportunities" and vague praise give the recipient nothing to respond to.Reference something specific, state what you built, ask one sharp question.
Asking leaders for their whole career storyIt is a large, unfocused request that is easy to postpone indefinitely.Ask for fifteen minutes about one specific decision they made.
Exchanging contacts with no follow-upA conversation that ends at the event is forgotten within a week.Send a short, specific note within forty-eight hours.
Chasing job titles over responsibilities"AI engineer" means different things in different teams; the title hides the work.Read for signals like evaluation, RAG, guardrails, and reliability.
Following every framework releaseConstant context switching produces activity without depth and leads to burnout.Keep a stable mental model and map new tools onto its layers.

None of these require talent to fix; they require a small, repeatable process. Write it down once and reuse it for every opportunity.

Review questions: career strategy

These questions help you turn the chapter's advice into decisions you can act on. Answer honestly, with your current projects in mind.

  1. What does a portfolio project need beyond a working demo to prove engineering judgement, and why does each extra artefact matter?
  2. Rewrite a generic outreach message of your own into one that is specific, brief, and easy to answer. What did you cut?
  3. Why is a question about one specific technical decision more likely to get a useful reply than a request for general career advice?
  4. You attend an event and meet several people. Describe a follow-up note that would be remembered, and one that would be ignored.
  5. How would you decide which new framework deserves your attention this month, given a stable mental model of the stack?

Summary

  • The scarce skill is turning models into reliable systems, not writing prompts.
  • Portfolio projects should prove judgement with evals, traces, and documented failures.
  • Specific, brief, value-led outreach works best with recruiters and leaders.
  • Events create shared context; follow up within forty-eight hours.
  • Interviews test trade-off reasoning backed by measurement.

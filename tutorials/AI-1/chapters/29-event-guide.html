29. AI-India 1st Edition: Event Guide

The event behind the book

This study companion is built around the themes of the first edition of AI-India, a one-day gathering of practitioners, engineers, architects, and leaders focused on applied artificial intelligence. The talks moved from research-flavoured questions about self-improving agents to the unglamorous realities of evaluation, guardrails, cost, and offline inference. That mix is exactly what makes the event useful as a learning map: it shows both the ambition and the engineering discipline that production AI requires.

The day ran from a morning registration through a sequence of focused talks, a lunch break, an afternoon of deeper infrastructure and safety topics, a tea break, and a closing hands-on workshop. The sequence below is presented in its original event order; the book itself reorganises the themes into a learning path.

How the talks connect

Read as a whole, the schedule tells a story. The morning asked how agents can improve themselves and how we can tell whether they actually did — a question that quickly becomes a question about evaluation and measurement. The local and offline talks then challenged the assumption that intelligence lives in the cloud. After lunch the focus moved down the stack: serving models efficiently, routing requests intelligently, connecting tools through a standard protocol, and protecting production systems with guardrails. The workshop brought every idea back to something you can type into an editor.

This book mirrors that arc while adding the foundations the talks assumed: models, prompting, retrieval, tool calling, and framework comparisons. Part VII then returns to the event itself, with this session map and the reconstructed workshop in Chapter 30.

Session themes at a glance

  • Agent improvement and failure modes — self-modification, reflection, and the limits of internal feedback.
  • Evaluation and Reliability — harnesses, judge calibration, mutation testing, silent degradation, and cost/security checks in CI.
  • Local AI — open-weight models such as Qwen, Gemma, and GPT-OSS; runtimes such as Ollama and LM Studio; local embeddings and RAG; NeMo Guardrails, Presidio, and Langfuse.
  • On-device AI — offline-first experiences and the constraints of mobile hardware.
  • Inference engineering — serving, batching, quantisation, and self-hosted models.
  • Model routing — matching requests to models by cost, latency, and quality.
  • MCP at scale — serverless and stateless tool servers.
  • Guardrails — production safety patterns and agentic risk classes.
  • Hands-on — a LangGraph and TypeScript workshop covering graph basics, a tool-calling ReAct agent, and stateful conversational memory.

Getting the most from the event material

If you attended, use this chapter as a map back to the talks and test whether your notes match the themes described here. If you did not attend, treat the schedule as a curriculum: pick one session a day and read the corresponding chapter. The companion chapters expand each short talk into explanations, comparisons, and exercises you can work through at your own pace.

Worked example: building a personal study track from the session map

The schedule in this chapter is more than a record of one day; it is a ready-made curriculum. Here is how a learner might turn the twelve sessions into a two-week study track, with concrete time boxes and outputs. The example uses only session topics, so it works whether or not you attended.

Mapping sessions to chapters

Each session is paired with the chapter that expands it, and each chapter gets a deliverable. The mapping looks like this:

Session topicChapterDeliverable
Self-improving agents19One page on internal versus external feedback
Evals and silent failure16–18A 20-case eval harness sketch
Local AI ecosystem24A local model running on your machine
Offline on mobile25A feature-fit classification
Inference engineering22A batching and quantisation note
Model routing23A three-model routing table
MCP at scale13–14One stateless tool server sketch
Guardrails20A risk-to-control map
Workshop30A running LangGraph agent

The pairings deliberately compress related talks: evaluation and silent failure are studied together because one is the tool and the other is the risk it guards against; local and offline topics sit side by side because they share hardware constraints. Skipping a pairing leaves a gap you will feel later, when a chapter assumes a concept you never covered.

Time-boxing the track

Assuming about 75 minutes a day, the learner assigns one session per day for nine days, reserves day ten for the workshop rebuild, and uses days eleven and twelve for review. A simple tracker keeps it honest:

day  topic                     output          done
1    self-improving agents     one page        [ ]
2    evals & silent failure      20 cases        [ ]
...
10   workshop rebuild          running agent   [ ]
11   review weak spots         notes           [ ]
12   pick capstone (Ch. 31)     project brief   [ ]

The point is not to consume twelve chapters passively. Each day ends with an artefact you can point to, which is why the track ends naturally at the capstone chapter.

Keeping the track honest

A plan this tidy rarely survives first contact with a real schedule, so the learner adds two rules. First, if a day is missed, the artefacts — not the calendar — define progress: you move on only when today's output exists, even if it takes two sittings. Second, each artefact gets a one-line note on what was surprising or unclear. Those notes become the review material for days eleven and twelve, and they often reveal that a topic you found easy on first reading has a gap you only noticed when you tried to build something.

Pair with the exercises. Every chapter in this book ends with exercises, and the day's artefact can be one of them. Choosing the exercise instead of inventing your own keeps the output concrete and gives you something to compare against the chapter's stated goals.
Exercise 29.3 — Rebalance the track. You only have five days. Choose five sessions that form a coherent subset, justify why you dropped the others, and name the capstone brief each one prepares you for.

Common pitfalls when reusing event material

A one-day event is a rich source, but it is easy to use it badly. These pitfalls apply whether you are studying the material yourself or adapting it for others.

PitfallWhy it mattersBetter approach
Treating talk order as a learning orderThe day was designed for an audience, not for a beginner; prerequisites appear late.Follow the book's chapters, which rebuild the foundations first.
Assuming a session topic equals the whole subjectA thirty-minute talk samples a theme; it cannot cover the trade-offs.Use the session map to find the chapter, then study the full treatment.
Reusing photographs or logos without rightsImages of people and brand marks need permission; reuse can create legal problems.Use only files you can publish, credit the source, keep an attribution page.
Adding individual names and affiliations casuallyThis guide deliberately omits them; inserting them changes the nature of the work.Discuss topics and decisions without attaching them to people.
Reading passively without an artefactA day of talks feels productive but leaves nothing you can demonstrate.End each study block with a note, a config, or a small piece of code.
Skipping the workshopThe talks explain ideas, but the lab is where the ideas become skills.Rebuild Chapter 30's agent and extend it, even if you never attended.

The session map is best treated as an index into the book, not as the book itself. Use it to choose where to go next, then do the deeper work in the companion chapters.
